A Chronicle ◆ From Karaikudi to Koramangala

Our Story

A small dining room on 6th Block runs on the patience of a brass pot, the memory of a grandmother's pantry, and the stubborn idea that Chettinad is not heat — it is structure.

Meena Alagappan was thirty-one when she resigned from a senior product role at a software firm on Old Airport Road. It was March 2016. Her notice period ran six weeks, she spent three of them in a town two hours south of Madurai picking through eleven tin trunks of her grandmother's kitchen in the village of Karaikudi.

She came back to Bangalore with a steel trunk, forty-two pieces of cookware, eleven hand-written recipe ledgers, a brass urli, and the conviction that the food she had eaten every summer of her childhood — the dry mutton chukka, the shallot-thick vetha kuzhambu, the unromantic dignity of properly made kara kuzhambu — had no honest representation in the city she now called home.

Kalasha is, in some essential way, the act of bringing those ledgers into daily use. The morning prep list — chalked in slate at 7.15 a.m, six days a week — is a direct descendant of the Karaikudi household sheet. The shopping route is shorter, but the questions it answers are the same.

"My aunt told me the kitchen has only two tools that matter — your grandmother's vessels, and the time you are willing to give them. Everything else is decoration." — Meena Alagappan, Chef & Owner

Section I · Origins

Karaikudi to Koramangala

The Chettiars were a merchant community whose trading routes once stretched from the Coromandel coast to Burma, Ceylon, and Penang. Their kitchens absorbed everything that travelled with them — star anise from the spice ships, palm sugar from the Malayan coast, the obsessive Tamil regard for the tempering pan — and rendered it back as a cuisine of disciplined austerity. Meena's grandmother kept a Karaikudi for sixty-one years. The pantry was inventoried twice a month: forty eight named spices, eleven varieties of dried lentil, a clay pot of milagu kuzhambu that was replenished but never emptied. When the house was sold in 2019, three of the inventory ledgers came to Bangalore.

Kalasha is, in some essential way, the act of bringing those ledgers into daily use. The morning prep list — chalked in slate at 7.15 a.m, six days a week — is a direct descendant of the Karaikudi household sheet. The shopping route is shorter, but the questions it answers are the same.

Section II · The Room

The Athangudi Floor

The dining room floor is laid in Athangudi tile, sourced directly from a fourth-generation workshop in Athangudi village, fourteen kilometres from Karaikudi. The tiles are made on a glass plate, one at a time, by hand, with cement, oxide pigment, and river sand — the same technique used in the great Chettinad mansions of the late nineteenth century.

We chose four patterns: a peacock-blue compass rose, a vermilion lotus medallion, a gold-leaf rotated square, and a plain ivory ground. Eleven hundred and eighty-four tiles arrived in four wooden crates in February 2020. They were laid by two craftsmen from the village over nineteen days. The floor is cool underfoot in summer; it is also slightly uneven — each tile carries the print of the hand that pressed it.

Section III · The Vessel

The Brass Pot

An eeya chombu is a pot turned from kalai — a tin alloy native to the Tamil south. It looks unremarkable: squat, lipped, the colour of weather. It is not unremarkable. The metal is soft enough that the pot must be lifted with both hands and washed only with tamarind. It cannot be set on a high flame. It cannot be left empty on heat.

What it does, in exchange for this fussing, is hold sambar at a low murmur for six hours without breaking the dal, without souring the tamarind, without dulling the curry leaf. The lentils stay distinct. The aroma stays sweet. There is no substitute. Stainless steel makes a faster sambar; an eeya chombu makes a different one.

Ours was inherited. It has been on the Kalasha pass since March 2025; after eighteen months of seasoning. It produces forty-two litres of sambar a day, is cleaned overnight on a banana leaf, and set on the stove again at 6.00 a.m.

The Hands at the Pass

Those Who Cook For You

Head Cook

Rajeshwari M.

Rajeshwari runs the savoury line and the morning grind. She came to Kalasha in 2018 from a family canteen in Devakottai where she had cooked for forty-four mill workers a day since she was sixteen. She does not believe in measuring spoons.

Pastry Lead

Anand Subramanian

Anand left a hotel pastry kitchen in Chennai to build the dessert programme at Kalasha. He keeps the paal payasam reduction at exactly two-and-a-half hours, and is responsible for the four sweets we change with the moon-month. Anand trained in Kandy in 2013, with three months in Madurai distinct.

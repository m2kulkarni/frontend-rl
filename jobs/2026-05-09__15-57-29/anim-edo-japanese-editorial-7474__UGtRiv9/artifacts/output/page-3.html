藍染 Indigo at Aizome-an

The fifth-
generation
dyer who refused
synthetic blue

By Hana Ueda
Photographs by S. Tanizaki
Workshop visit, March 2024

Shoko Furuyama broke Karōzan-fude, a 200-year-old earthenware pot on the day of her father's funeral. She is the fifth generation. In the 1970s the workshop nearly closed. Two ageing apprentices left.

I
PORTRAIT · A NISHIJIN VAT, MARCH 2024 — sukumo composted from Tokushima leaves, sixth fermentation week.

That Furuya can (in 2024 from her workshop deep in Nishijin) work indigo as a Tenpō-era dyer worked indigo is a small miracle. The labour, the social grammar, the chemistry of the bath, the rhetoric of how to scold an apprentice — these survived because she refused, three times, to let synthetic dye into the room.

"This is not a metaphor I am imposing. The vegetable bath is alive: it eats sugar from the wheat-bran she stirs in every morning at six. It exhales ammonia. It drinks its own scum. If you starve it, if you mistime the lime water, if you let an unwashed steel pot near it — you have to start the conversation again."

A Conversation, Transcribed

Q · 質問
You inherited the Aizome-an from your father in 1989. What changed?
Nothing, on purpose. The light fixtures had to go from incandescent to LED in 2008 because the city said so. I have a new pot from a potter in Shigaraki. Otherwise the room is the room my great-grandmother walked into.
Q · 質問
When did synthetic indigo arrive?
1897. BASF. By the 1920s it was thirty times cheaper than ours. Most workshops closed before the war. The ones that survived sold to dyers who wanted to write 'indigo' on a label and meant it as costume.
Q · 質問
And the synthetic vat — does the colour differ?
To the kimono buyer, no. To the cloth, yes. Synthetic strikes flat and uniform. Vegetable indigo deposits in microlayers, oxidises overnight. A jacket that has been worn for five years tells you what kind of light it was hung in.
Q · 質問
Who buys it now?
A theatre company in Kyoto. Two kimono ateliers in Kanazawa. A man in Brooklyn who pays in cash. The Imperial Household Agency, occasionally, on quiet orders for the Shōsōin restoration.
"The bath has been fermenting since 1834. My job is not to dye cloth. My job is to keep something alive long enough to hand it to whoever comes next."
SHADE · 色見本

Pale Aizu Blue

Three days, eight dips. The mid-tone of a country sky in early March. Worn by women in their thirties in 1782, by Aizome-an's apprentice now.

SHADE · 色見本

Deep Navy

Twenty days, twenty-four dips. The colour the cotton goes only when the bath is in its third week and the morning is cold and quiet.

SHADE · 色見本

Warrior's Blue

Eight years (the longest dye in the Aizome-an records), thirty-six dips. A black so blue you can still see the indigo if a strong noon light hits the cloth.

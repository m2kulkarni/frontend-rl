Tsumago-
juku, in the quiet
of January.

A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the eaves.

DEPARTED Nagiso Stn., 14 Jan
0648 — JR Chuo
ARRIVED Tsumago, 14 Jan
0840 — local bus
SNOW 47 cm at midnight
14 Jan
LANTERNS LIT 16:08

The bus from Nagiso station lets you off at the lower end of Tsumago-juku just as the snow starts. By the road the pillar already squints below the tree-line down past the cedars to the river. There it is, the corner of slate that has, on cue, made it to the cover of the JNTO booklet.

The first preservation ordinance, the first of its kind in Japan, had passed in 1968. Behind the geography of refusal, the Tsumago-juku Hozonkai (Preservation Society) was — and is — the formal arrangement of villagers who agreed they would not sell their houses, alter their façades, or turn their farms into ramen shops.

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became Tsumago has been told often enough that the residents are tired of telling it, but it bears repeating. By the late 1960s the village had been emptied by Nagano-bound migration. The post-houses were boarded. A schoolmaster, a priest, and a former councillor formed the Hozonkai, drew a breath, and wrote three principles into a covenant that a postmaster, a county clerk, and a former councillor would not sell, would not lend, would not lease.

The principles still hang, in a careful Edo-style brushwork, in the entryway of the Tsumago-juku museum. The reconstruction is honest about being a counterfeit — humble, transparent, born of a desperate refusal. They mean: this is the village. Look long enough and you will see what we have erased.

Gohei-mochi, miso, walnut

I stayed at the Matsushiro inn, four rooms above a restaurant that has served travellers, by the proprietress's reckoning, since 1614. Dinner on the second night was gohei-mochi — pounded rice glazed with a paste of crushed walnut and white miso, grilled over binchotan with a precision that suggested the proprietor's wife had threaded a needle in pace with the lacquer between strokes.

ACCUMULATION · 14–15 JANUARY 2024

On the morning of departure I measured the snow against the lower edge of the Matsushiro signboard with a folded notebook: forty-seven centimetres, give or take the thickness of the lacquer. The Nagano Forecast Office reported forty-nine for the Kiso valley as a whole. The figures agree more closely than the local agencies.

I left on the 09.42 bus, then the train, where lights from JR East flickered through Nagano on a long tunnel of January, and went home. I should not have stayed. The road I'd just walked was the road home.

— By Ryōsuke Mori
Field Editor · Dispatched from Nagiso, January 2024

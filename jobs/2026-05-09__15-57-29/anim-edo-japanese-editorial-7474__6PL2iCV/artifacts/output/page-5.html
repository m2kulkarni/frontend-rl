FIELD REPORT·NAGISO-MURA

Tsumago-
juku, in the quiet
of January.

A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the snow.
DISPATCH
FROM THE FIELD

By Ryōsuke Mori
Travel-correspondent. Thirteen days in the snow on the Nakasendō.

VISITED 14–17 JANUARY 2024

The bus from Nagiso station lets you off at the lower end of Tenmadō-zaka, where the road slopes uphill between the cedar-shingled, hipped-roof shopfronts. By that hour the village should already have closed for lunch, but the shutters are open along this row of the road. It is January 14th, the year is 2024, and the snow that arrived just before dawn has yet to be raked into the trough.

I am here to write about a town that refused. In 1968 the residents of Tsumago, who had watched the rest of the Kiso valley lose its post-houses to the highways and the souvenir trade, signed a covenant. They drafted what is now called the Tsumago Charter, the gist of which is: do not sell, do not rent, do not damage. In a country where each generation has been taught to demolish the previous one's house and rebuild on the ruins, this was not as small a thing as it sounds.

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became Tsumago has been told often enough that the residents are tired of it; you can sit in the kissaten Wakihonjin Okuya and read the back issues of the Nagiso Town newsletter, which devotes a column-inch each January to the next ten residents in the Charter rotation. The fee for participating is steep: you give up the right to siding, to aluminum shutters, to satellite dishes facing the street. The reward is that, in exchange, you live in a place that has not changed since your grandparents lived in it.

The principles still hang in a corner of the entryway of the Tsumago-juku Preservation Society building. Three lines, brushed in black ink. Don't sell. Don't lend. Don't damage. The fourth, added in 1971, prohibits ANY construction of any kind that obscures the original style and form. The fourth was the addition that broke the will of three of the original signatories. They moved away.

A diner of denshōkina

I stayed at the Matsushiro-ya, four doors down from the Wakihonjin Okuya, where the proprietor is the great-grand-niece of the family who ran the original honjin under the Tokugawa. The futon was put away every morning at six and unrolled at eight in the evening, with the same weight of efficiency that the previous Matsushiro-ya generations applied to the same pair of futons. The room was three-and-a-half tatami, a kotatsu, a kakemono of a winter pine, and the morning at the bath. I was given the upstairs to myself.

ACCUMULATED · 14–17 JAN 2024
Snow, on the slate roofs of the post-town's principal street, by the morning of the seventeenth. Half the average for January in this valley.
On the morning of departure I measured the snow against the lower edge of the Matsushiro-ya signboard with a folded notecard. Forty-seven centimetres, give or take. The proprietor (Sayako Tasaka) signed off on my measurement. You may take the card with you, she said.

I left on the 09:42 bus. The Kiso valley was still under deep cloud at noon. The post-town was just behind me. Some of it would, I expected, still be there next year, and the year after, and the year after that. Most of Japan would not.

About the Museum &
History of the
Cloister

A century and a quarter of antiquaries' vow, the social cost of the cathedral building age — glass, vellum, and stone gathered to the room of the Priory of Saint-Aldelm-the-Aphis.

Three Foundations

1897

The Antiquary's Vow

Édouard de Saint-Pierre, antiquarian of Reims and resident of Fontainebleau, vows in his sixty-first year never to assemble or display any object salvaged from the cathedral fabric of France.

1924

The Glass Bequest

Upon the antiquarian's death, his heirs entrusted his collection to a board of trustees. The museum opens to the public, MCMXXIV, displaying twenty-eight panels of stained glass and a nameless arch from the cloister of Saint-Pons-de-Thomières.

1944 — 1962

The Rebuilding

After incendiary damage in the late years, the cloister walk and the lancet hall were rebuilt in part by apprentices of the Compagnons du Devoir, founded MDCCCXCVII. Permanent collection grew sevenfold under the tenure of director Léon Roussel.

An Account of the Cloister

[motif: quatrefoil]

The Priory of Saint-Aldelm-the-Aphis stood empty from the suppressions of 1791 until Édouard de Saint-Pierre, then fifty-four years of age and lately retired from his commerce in ecclesiastical antiquities of Reims, took possession of its ruined chapter house and west cloister in the autumn of 1897. He installed his private collection — manuscript leaves, capital fragments, and a set of three rose-window medallions attributed to the workshop of Saint-Denis — in the surviving south range, opening one room in scholars by appointment.

Following the bequest of 1924 the trust acquired contiguous parcels to the north and east, regularising the precinct to its present 4.6 hectares. The west lancet hall, completed under the Forrer atelier in 1962, runs ninety-three metres along the former ceremonial nave; its twenty-two paired lancets house the rose of the de la Saint-Pierre glass at the original cill height. The Rose Window Study wing, dedicated in May 1929, completes the north-west angle of the cloister and provides climate-controlled vitrines for the medallion fragments and a small chapel for the conservation of pigments and lead came.

The Fifty-Three Stations,
Two Hundred Years On

ByMariko Sato
Issued11 Sept 2024
SubjectEdo Travel
Read22 min
DepartureNihonbashi ArrivalSanjō Ōhashi Distance492 km Walked21 days PhotographsS. Tanizaki

At 5:42 on the morning of the third of May, with the bronze plague of Nihonbashi still cold under my palm, I shouldered a thirteen-kilo pack and set out for Kyoto on foot — a journey Hiroshige rendered in fifty-five woodblock prints in 1833, and which the post-runners of the Tokugawa shogunate were expected to finish in three days. I gave myself twenty-three.

The Tōkaidō, the eastern sea road, was always more idea than infrastructure. By the time Utagawa Hiroshige published his Gojūsan-tsugi series with the Hōeidō publishing house, the route had been formally surveyed for two centuries: fifty-three official post-towns between Edo and the imperial capital, each with its honjin for daimyō retinues, its rice merchants, its bathhouses, its quarrels. What Hiroshige did was less to document than to translate — the road as weather, as encounter, as the slight hesitation of a porter at a river crossing.

I carried two books, Smith and Foster's 1986 translation of the series, with its lavish foldouts of the hand-edition; and a 1974 walking guide by the Shizuoka Prefectural Tourist Bureau, which I had bought for ¥400 at a Jimbōchō second-hand stall the week before leaving. The guide was thirty years older than I assumed I would still find roads in.

Hiroshige's genius was to draw not the road but the weather of being on it — the moment a porter shifts his load, the second a traveller realises the rain will not stop before nightfall. — from the introduction, Smith & Foster, 1986

The Lake, the Pass, the Print

The Hakone print is the one everybody knows: the procession threading a path between fanged blue-and-orange mountains, Lake Ashi a flat grey wedge below. Hiroshige's mountains are exaggerated — the geologist Tanaka Kōji, in a 1999 paper for the Kanagawa Museum, compared the print's gradients with the actual contours and found the artist had multiplied the steepest faces by roughly 1.4. I climbed them on the eighth day, in fog so dense I could not see the lake at all, and concluded that 1.4 was if anything a conservative figure.

The old checkpoint, the Hakone-juku sekisho, was reconstructed in 2007 from the original Tokugawa-era specifications. The reconstruction is honest about being a reconstruction: laminated panels, a gift shop, a polite young woman in period costume who told me she was studying logistics at Tōkai University and worked weekends. She also told me — and I have not been able to verify this — that the cedar beams were sourced from a forest that had supplied the original 1619 structure.

Where the Inn Still Stands

Of the fifty-five stations, only a handful retain a structure that a Tempō-era traveller would recognize. Goyu is one of them. The Ōhashi-ya, an inn that has operated continuously since 1648, sits on a curve of the old road five hundred metres from a soulless prefectural bypass. The current proprietor, Ōhashi Sanae, is the seventeenth of her line. She charged me ¥6,800 for a room with a view of a bamboo grove and a breakfast of grilled mackerel, miso, three pickles, and rice cooked in an iron pot.

«We had Hiroshige's grandson here in 1888,» she said, pouring tea. «He stayed two nights. The register survives.» She would not show it to me — it is on loan to the Tokyo National Museum until October — but she had a photocopy taped to the kitchen wall.

I stopped at a 7-Eleven for a rice-ball. The clerk, when I told her I was walking to Kyoto, replied as if walking to Kyoto were within the normal grammar of human ambition.

Naoko Furusawa works barefoot beside a 200-year-old earthenware vat sunk into the floor of a Nishijin machiya. She is the fifth Furusawa to do so. The vat, she explains, has not been emptied since 1834.

The 200-year-old earthenware vat (kame) is sunk in by 6 in the workshop's beaten-earth floor. Fed daily with sukumo, lye, wheat-bran and sake, the bath has not been allowed to die since the year of the Tenpō reforms. A copper kettle hangs perpetually beside it.

— K. Aizawa, IX/2024

To that Naoko can you ever walk past and the noodle shop on Hosoo Street and the practiced eye, but the work goes on under the gable. Aizome-an is one of the last houses still doing both halves: the cultivation of the polygonum that yields sukumo, and the fermentation, and the dyeing. Most of her clients order in twelve-foot bolts of cotton and silk, mailed in cardboard tubes from Kyoto Station. Some, like the kabuki costume departments, demand specifically what synthetic indigo can never produce — a depth that holds up under tungsten light.

A Conversation, Transcribed

TR: Could you describe what makes this vat singular?

Because it has been alive — fed and not allowed to die — since 1834. Tetsujirō, my husband's great-great-grandfather, started it. He was a third son with no inheritance, and the vat was what he could afford.

TR: Has anyone ever proposed buying it?

Of course. Twice in the postwar period. We refused both times. The bath does not survive the move; the bath is a place, not a thing.

TR: Will it pass to a sixth generation?

I have a daughter, Yoshie. She is twenty-eight, lives in Berlin, designs apps. We have not had this conversation directly. The bath does not require a Furusawa, only someone who knows it and is willing to feed it every morning before sunrise.

TR: Has anyone outside the family ever fed it?

My apprentice, Akiko, since 2019. So in some sense the question of succession has already been answered, even if the surname has not yet caught up.

TR: What about the synthetic indigo developed in Germany in 1897?

It is faster, cheaper, and steady. Steady is the wrong word for a colour. A real bath is never steady; it is in conversation with the season, the room, the dyer's hands. Synthetic indigo is what you get when you stop wanting a conversation.

"The bath has been fermenting since 1834. My job is not to dye cloth. My job is to keep something alive long enough to hand it to whoever comes next." — Naoko Furusawa · Aizome-an
Recipe · 一
Sukumo · Pale Moon

Polygonum tinctorium leaves. The leaves arrive from a co-op in Tokushima already composted into sukumo, the dark crumbling brick from which the bath is fed daily.

Recipe · 二
Sleep eight nights

The fresh-fed bath is never used the day of feeding. It must rest at least eight nights for the iron to settle. The dyer feels for ready-ness with the back of a hand.

Recipe · 三
Warrior's Blue, twelve dips

The deepest indigo bolts get twelve dips: in, out, hung in air for oxidation, then back in. Each dip multiplies the depth. After twelve, the cloth is the colour of a winter sky at four in the afternoon.

The Fifty-Three Stations, Two Hundred Years On

Hiroshige walked the Tōkaidō in 1832 and returned with the prints that would, within a decade, define how the rest of the world pictured Japan. This autumn, our editors retraced the route on foot from Nihonbashi to Sanjō Ōhashi — fifty-three stations, four hundred ninety-two kilometers — to ask what survives of the road, the inns, and the hand-cut blocks that first carried its image abroad.

Tsumago-juku in Winter

The forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō was preserved in 1971 by villagers who refused both the highway and the souvenir trade. In January, with the slate roofs under snow and the lanterns lit by four, it remains the closest a traveler can come to the road Bashō knew.

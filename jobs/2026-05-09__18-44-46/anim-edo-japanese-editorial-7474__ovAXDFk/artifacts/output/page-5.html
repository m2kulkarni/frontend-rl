Field Report · No. 5 · Nakasendō

Tsumago-
juku, in the quiet
of January.

A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards and — almost — the sound of the snow.

Dateline Tsumago, Nagiso
Visited 6th–8th January 2025
By Ryōsuke Mori
Editing Hana Ueda
Travelogue Tsumago is the 42nd of the 69 stations on the inland-route Nakasendō. East-west: between Nakatsugawa & Yabuhara.

The bus from Nagiso Station lets you off at the lower end of Terashita-machi a little after three in the afternoon. By that hour the post has already slipped behind the Kiso ridge and the street — narrow, unpaved beneath its frozen mud — is the colour of weak tea. There are no cars. The 1968 preservation ordinance, the first of its kind in Japan, had seen to that before I was born, what it could not legislate, the geography of the valley legislated for it. Tsumago simply has nowhere for a car to go.

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became Tsumago has been told often enough that the residents are tired of telling it; but it bears retelling, for this offer too has more general application. By the 1960s the post-towns of the Nakasendō had been emptied for three generations: railway, then highway, then automobile. The young left for the cities, the old shut up the heavy paper screens and went south. By 1965, fewer than two-hundred people lived in the town, half of those over sixty, and the local primary school had closed.

The principles still hang, in a printed frame, in the entryway of the Tsumago-juku Preservation Society Office: roofs slate-grey, no advertisements, no neon, and the lacquered — deep enough to the slate beneath the snow. It works.

Gohei-mochi, miso, walnut

I stayed at the Matsushiro-Inn, four rooms above a restaurant that has served travellers, by the proprietress's reckoning, since 1614. Dinner on the second night was gohei-mochi — pounded rice flattened to the shape of an oar leaf, brushed in a glaze of grilled walnut, miso and sugar — and grilled river-fish with a salt crust. The pour of unwarmed shōchū from the cellar was rough but warm.

The piers of mushroom & freedom of unfinished mochi, with peeled nomura, a bowl of pulse stew & lacquered bowls. I have spelled the local recipe through a paper screen translucent with effective use of brushed kanji.

On the morning of departure I measured the snow against the lower edge of the Matsushiro signboard with a folded notebook; forty-seven centimetres, give or take the thickness of the lacquer.

I left on the 6:14 bus. The fare back from Tsumago to Nagiso Station is ¥870; the ride takes exactly twelve minutes; in 2017 the post office in Nagiso closed but the post-box on the eastern wall is still emptied at noon by a man in a green parka.

— R. Mori, Returns Section · Tsumago, January 2025

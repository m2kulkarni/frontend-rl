Tsumago-
juku, in the quiet
of January.

A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the snow.

執筆 · Ryōsuke Mori

写真 · S. Tanizaki

From Nagiso, Nagano-ken

Filed 14 January 2024

The bus from Nagiso station lets you off at the lower end of Terashita-dōri, where a fine of three thousand yen still carries the legitimate threat of three thousand yen. By the time you walk the three hundred metres to the post-town proper, the snow has all but blanked the signboards. From here, the daily logic of Tsumago is the same logic the village agreed to in 1971, which is the same logic the inn-keepers had agreed to centuries earlier: the eaves do not move, the windows do not enlarge, the lanterns are lit by four.

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became Tsumago has been told often enough that the residents are tired of telling it, but it bears retelling. By 1965 the post-town had emptied to fewer than 130 inhabitants. The road between Tsumago and Magome — the famous walking section of the Nakasendō — had become, for a generation, a goat track. The post-roads themselves had been silenced fifty years earlier when the Chūō line was driven through the next valley.

The principles still hang, in unprinted form, in the entryway of the Tsumago-juku Preservation Society office: the inn is faded, the cypress is washed, the eaves are repaired before they fall. They are repaired with the same wood, by hand. The geometry has been preserved with such severity that a friend once said, after coming up the platform, that he was almost frightened. There is what you do not invent, and what you do not throw away. There is what you do, eaves angle, the snow letting go of the cedar branches outside.

Gohei-mochi, miso, walnut

I stopped at the Matsushiro-ya, four rooms above a restaurant that has served travelers, by the proprietress's reckoning, since 1714. Dinner on the second night was the gohei-mochi, rice paddled flat with a wooden mallet, charred with a walnut-miso glaze, soft in the centre and salty at the edges. The set was washed down with a copper kettle of cha. The grain at Matsushiro is local Shinshū rice and walnuts from the wood that climbs the high pass between Magome and Sumi. From the verandah window, the snow letting go of the cedar branches outside.

The meal arrived on a tray of unfinished hinoki, with pickled momiji-fu — a kind of red-leaf seitan dyed in beetroot and shoyu — and the local Suwa sake, served warm in tin cups. Between bites, the snow setting through the cedar branches.

On the morning of departure I measured the snow against the lower edge of the Matsushiro signboard with a folded notebook: forty-seven centimetres, give or take the thickness of the lacquer. The Tsumago-juku Regional Forecast Office reported forty-nine for the day, called for any tight by.

I left on the 09:42 bus. From the back window the post-town stood half lost into the cedar exactly as Hiroshige drew it in 1817. In the post-print he the Esan station, attendants with a lantern, the only reasonable course of action would be to walk through Bash through, in fact, the corridor of letters; if a road remains a road only when it is walked.

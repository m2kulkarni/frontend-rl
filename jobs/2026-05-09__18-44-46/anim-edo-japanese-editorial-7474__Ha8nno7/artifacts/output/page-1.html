The Fifty-Three
Stations, Two
Hundred Years On

Hiroshige walked the Tōkaidō in 1832 and returned with the prints that would, within a decade, define how the rest of the world pictured Japan. This autumn, our editors retraced the route on foot from Nihonbashi to Sanjō Ōhashi — fifty-three stations, four hundred ninety-two kilometres — to ask what survives of the road, the inns, and the hand-cut blocks that first carried its image abroad.

Hokusai's Wave, Read Slowly

The most reproduced image in the world deserves an hour, not a glance. We examine the 1831 keyblock — Prussian blue, foam claws, the sliver of Fuji — and trace how Katsushika's geometry of pressure made a single sheet outlast its empire.

Indigo at Aizome-an: A Kyoto Vat, Unbroken Since 1789

Down a lane in Nishijin, master dyer Tomiko Furuya tends sukumo fermented from Tokushima leaves her grandfather first composted. We spent three days at her workshop watching cotton lift from the vat the colour of a winter sky at dusk.

By Hana Ueda · Photographs by S. Tanizaki

第三篇 · III
FIELD REPORT · 妻籠宿

Tsumago-juku in Winter

The forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō was preserved in 1971 by villagers who refused both the highway and the souvenir trade. In January, with the slate roofs under snow and the lanterns lit by four, it remains the closest a traveler can come to the road Bashō knew.

By Ryōsuke Mori · Dispatched from Nagiso

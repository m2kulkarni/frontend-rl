Hokusai's
Wave, Read Slowly

By the Critic
Junichirō Mori
Contributing Editor; Edo-period print culture
18 September 2024 · Tokyo

For two centuries the West has read Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura as a portrait of menace — an instant before catastrophe, a wave poised to devour three slender boats. This is, I want to argue, an almost wilful misreading. The wave is not falling. It is repeating.

Plate 1
After Hokusai, c. 1831
Read From Page

To look first to a first-state impression — I had 90 to to the artist Sumida Hokusai Museum, on a screen-rod painted reproduction in October — is to feel the curl is a foam, the fingertip — but is, in the secondary plates of the artist's late period of Hokusai's working career and the steady, almost unflinching wave nine to repeat.

"The wave is not a verb. It is a noun being held still long enough to be drawn. The boatmen do not know. The boatmen are not the subject."

第一図 · I

The Foam Claws

The micrographic detail of Prussian-blue carving along the foam-line. Each spike is two strokes cut at differing pressures.

第二図 · II

The Three Skiffs

Hokusai's trick of staggered prow-lines: the boats are stacked, not parallel. The eye reads pursuit. The hand has drawn placement.

第三図 · III

Mt. Fuji, in Scale

At one-fifteenth the print's height — and dwarfed by the wave's negative-space crest — Fuji is a bystander to the violence Hokusai is suggesting.

"But surely the wave is about to fall."

It is not. The wave's gesture — repeated against the breath-mark of the boatmen — is sustained, like a held note. To deny that is to deny the print its actual subject: not catastrophe but the moment Edo learned to render water without pretending it could be anchored.

"Hokusai feared the sea — see his late letters."

He did, certainly: letters then collected during the Tempō era, repeatedly. He drew the wave, his apprentice records, "to make it again until it stopped looking like a thing about to break." The print, he is reported to have said, was a record of stilled fear.

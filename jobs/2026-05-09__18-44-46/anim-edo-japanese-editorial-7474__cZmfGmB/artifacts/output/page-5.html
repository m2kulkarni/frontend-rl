Field Report MMXXIV妻籠宿

Tsumago-
juku, in the quiet
of January.

A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the snow.

By Ryōsuke Mori · Dispatched from Nagiso
Dateline
Tsumago-juku, Nagiso
Visited
4–7 January 2024
Contributing Editor
Ryōsuke Mori
Marginalia

Tsumago is the 42nd of the 69 stations of Nakasendō, the inland route that paralleled the coastal Tōkaidō between Edo and Kyoto.

The bus from Nagiso Station lets you off at the lower end of Terashita-machi a little after three in the afternoon. By that hour the bus had already slipped behind the Kiso ridge and the street — the narrow, unpaved beneath its sloping roof — fits the colour of weak tea. There are no cars. The 1968 preservation ordinance, the first of its kind in Japan, had seen to that before I was born; what it could not legislate, the geography of the valley legislated for it.

I had come to write about the lacquered shop signs — the suzuran hung from the eaves of the merchant houses along Terashita-machi and Naka-machi — but the signs, that first afternoon, were doing what signs in winter do: accumulating snow on their upper edges, having themselves heavier and quieter than the buildings they advertised. A wooden plate-hanger for a sweetshop, lacquered with the character 飴 cut clean through, had a ridge of snow two fingers thick.

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became "Tsumago has been told often enough that the residents are tired of telling it, but it bears repeating.

The principles still hang, in cracked Kanji, in the entryway of the Tsumago-juku Preservation Society. The vat is fifty-eight years older than the building. It is a member of nothing.

Gohei-mochi, miso, walnut

I stayed at the Matsushiro-Inn, four hundred metres up the road. The proprietor, a former carpenter, is the only innkeeper licensed to serve wood-fired gohei-mochi within the village. The skewer is dipped in a sweet-walnut miso paste, grilled over Kiso cedar, and wrapped in a cedar leaf cut from the ridge above the inn.

On the morning of departure I measured the snow against the lower edge of the Matsushiro signboard with a folded train-ticket: forty-seven centimetres, give or take. The weather forecast Office reported forty-nine for the Kiso valley as a whole. The figure agreed once corrected for the tilt.

I left on the 09:42 bus. From the back window the village had risen well into the cloud the front-edge of, the post-town its any preservation can have any other future. There is no way: the snow has its own light and right.

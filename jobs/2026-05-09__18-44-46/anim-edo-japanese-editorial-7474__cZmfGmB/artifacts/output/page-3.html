Indigo at Aizome-an

Photographs by

Naoko Furusawa works barefoot beside a 200-year-old earthenware vat sunk into the floor of a Nishijin machiya. She is the fifth Furusawa to do so. The vat, she explains, has not been emptied since 1834.
Aizome-an
I
Plate 1 · Kame-vat, Morning
— K. Aoyama, March

The Furusawa vat is 200 years old, and the months drop on Aizome-an's dyeing floor go down to listen for it in the afternoon. By that hour the vat has already slipped behind the Higo edge of the street — the narrow, unpaved beneath its sloping roof. There is no signage outside its enclosure.

For five generations the family has cultivated sukumo, the Japanese composted-leaves indigo from which a vat is born. They worked the vat until two days before the war reached Kyoto in 1944, and resumed three months after the surrender. Naoko Furusawa, born 1958, is the fifth to keep the vat alive.

A Conversation, Transcribed

— Are you the only person here? Today, yes. My apprentice has gone to Tokushima for the harvest. The vat does not require attendance every minute. It requires attendance every day.
— What do you feed it? Wheat bran. Lime, sometimes. The leaves. We feed at four-fifteen every morning. The vat takes it as well as the body takes warm water.
— Why did your grandfather refuse the synthetic dye? He had hands. He could feel the difference at the wrist. He used to say the synthetic dye stains the cotton; the natural dye stains the air around it.

There were exactly three months in 1834 — the third moon of the Tempō era — when this vat was first stoppered. The cotton it stains today is still cotton. The colour it stains it is still the colour of a winter sky at dusk.

Yes, you can buy this colour at any home-store. No, you cannot.

The principles tell long, in cracked Kanji, in the entryway of the Tsuchiya-juku Preservation Society. The vat is fifty-eight years older than the building. It is a member of nothing. It will outlive whoever next reads them aloud.

— What was your apprenticeship? I trusted my father. He trusted his. The transmission was hands. We do not romanticise: we read carefully, and we cite our sources.
"The bath has been fermenting since 1834. My job is not to dye cloth. My job is to keep something alive long enough to hand it to whoever comes next." — Naoko Furusawa, Master Dyer
Plate 2第二図

Pale Stone Blue

First dip. Three minutes in the vat, four in the air. Cotton, undyed, lifts the colour of unsettled rain.

Plate 3第三図

Deep navy
night sky

Third dip. The fibre has darkened by half. Hung in shadow, the cloth is read as black until it is laid in the sun.

Plate 4第四図

Warrior's
indigo dye

Eighth dip. The colour the warrior class reserved for inner linings; cool, almost black, almost legible only by candlelight.

CHAPTER III · ON METHOD

THE QUIET DISCIPLINE
OF KĀSHĪ-KĀRĪ

A note from the workshop on how a single mosaic panel — from gridded paper cartoon to grouted spandrel — passes through four stages, seven colors, and the patient hands of those who come before.

◆ I · PREMISE ◆

A CRAFT HELD IN THE HAND

The mosaic, in the Safavid manner we practice in Kucheh-ye Khaju, is not a decorative trade laid over architecture but a discipline that begins with measured paper and ends centuries later in the bare hand of the conservator.

It begins with a section. The full bay reduces to a tile, the colour to seven, the polychromy to lapis and gold leaf available on commission. Each panel is bedded in a lime sherbet, drawn from a chalcedony tone, and the gach-burī laying off-loyal to the saith section. Through measured discipline, the western sacred is not transferred wholesale: the tail-glyph reads as the same chamber's outline.

The atelier was founded in 1386 (2007) of the common reckoning on the principle that nothing is hurried. A grouted of one square metre, on its own without a medium ornament desire, occupies the workshop for between forty and sixty working days. We do not accept commissions that ask us to compress this. The reason is technical, not sentimental: the cuts will not align, the glaze will not vitrify, and the gach-burī will not cure if the schedule is forced.

What follows is a working description of each stage, the materials and temperatures involved, and a short note on the line of teachers from whom this method reaches us. It is offered for those who wish to commission work, study with the studio, or simply understand what they are looking at when they read words on Isfahani domes.

◆ II · PROCESS ◆

THE FOUR STAGES

— I —

ṬARRĀḤĪ

Lining & Drawing

The panel is drawn at full scale on gridded vellum — twenty squares to the girih — using compass, straight-edge, and a Karachian brush in iron-gall ink. We resolve the proportion strict in revision, the bench-test re-coloured, then the proportion-of-Saʻd hood, drawn first by sight and only afterwards in compass.

14-21 DAYSVELLUM · INK · COMPASS
— II —

BORĪDEH-KĀRĪ

Cutting & Sorting

Glazed tile blanks, fired the previous month at 1240°C in the small downdraught kiln behind the courtyard, are carved up the haft-rang in the workshop. Tiles are the cuts laid into a shaped place is then dressed with the wheel — a small chert hammer carved with the bird-like edge of the Yazd quarries in significant lines. A grade kept may demand from for thrice the cuts.

21 DAYSKĀSHI · KILN · CHISEL
— III —

CHĪDEMĀN

Setting & Lay-up

The cut pieces are laid face-down on the cartoon on a low table, glaze-side to the paper, in the reverse of their final reading. This is the stage that distinguishes mosaic (muʻarraq from painted ceramic mock-up) so far) actually a kind of inverse house: the dressing crew faces the back-cuts into a single transposable slab.

10-21 DAYSBENCH · PASTEBOARD
— IV —

BAND-KESHĪ

Grouting & Setting

On site, the slab is offered up in a freshly floated bed of gāch — a lime-gypsum mortar of our own gauging, three parts slaked lime to one part fine alabaster from Yazd. Once the bed has taken, the cartoon paper is sponged away and the joints are pointed with a black-tinted lime grout drawn into the channels with a horn nozzle.

5-9 DAYSSITE-LIME · GROUT

Each glaze is mixed in a ceramic mortar from raw oxides — cobalt for the lapis, copper for the turquoise, antimony of lead for the gold — bound with a borax and quartz fritz ground in our atelier. Fired once at 920 °C on a 4-hour rising curve and cooled overnight; the lighter tones are open-fired and the kiln has gone cold to the touch.

Hokusai's
Wave, Read Slowly

By Kenji Akiyama · Contributing Editor · 12 minute read · 18 OCTOBER MMXXIV

Filed

Print Analysis

Subject

"Under the Wave off Kanagawa"

Print Year

1831

Pigment

Prussian blue (bero-ai)

For two centuries the West has read Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura as a portrait of menace — an instant before catastrophe, a wave poised to devour three slender boats. This is, I want to argue, an almost wilful misreading. The wave is not falling. It is repeating.

To read that in a five-inch keyimpression — I had it in the print room of the Honda Bekkan Museum, on a grey Wednesday in March — is to feel the image break open. The reading by Roe Sang-ne is in 1842, when the print was found that the original Hokusai modelling, the wood of the engraver of Bombay, did not lift its pose: it should be set off — to read his peaks, then. The block is an arc, with the height-line on the wave parallel to the arc — the wave-foam imitating "the foam-claws."

This is not a metaphor I am imposing. The insights for tomographic and morphological scanning span what scholars have come to call the "exit-rotation" hypothesis. Look at the boats: they are oriented towards the wave, not flighing from it. The boatmen are pulling on, not braced for impact, and several lean forward with the small grace one finds in a sailor who knows the dimension of the loaded sea.

The wave is not a verb. It is a noun being verbed — water performing its grammar.

To stand close to a first-state impression — I had it for one minute, in the print room of the Honda Bekkan Museum, on a grey Wednesday in March — is to be made strangely aware of how Hokusai's reading of the sea anticipates Japonisme by half a century. Without it, French painters of the late nineteenth century would not have read Hiroshige's atmospheres the way they did, and the colour blue would not be the same century.

Read this way, the print's unique structure of contemplating moves something. The morning-blue of the night-sky, the bright-blue tetra of the spell, and the unnerving dancer-and-fearless-shape that is "the wave-foam" — none of this is the work of a manic dragon-tamer. It is the work of a slow visual grammarian. Hokusai is parsing seawater.

The wave is not a verb. It is a noun being verbed.

Read the print one element at a time, beginning with the foam claws — small drips that seem about to break free of the wave's edge. They will not. They will gather and fall back. This is the central paradox of the print: stasis disguised as catastrophe.

Counter-Views

"The wave is the wave. There is nothing to read except technique." — Iwasaki Tetsuo, in his notes for a 2018 Aichi Prefectural Museum catalogue.

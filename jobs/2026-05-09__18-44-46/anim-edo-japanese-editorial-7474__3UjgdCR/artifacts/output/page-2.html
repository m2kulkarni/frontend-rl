The Fifty-Three
Stations, Two
Hundred Years
On

BY MARIKO SATO · 12 MIN READ
WALKED
22 days · Sept 2024
FROM
Nihonbashi, Tokyo
TO
Sanjō Ōhashi, Kyoto
DISTANCE
492 km
STATIONS
53 + termini

A chill, on the morning of the fifty-fifth day. We left the inn at Mishima before five and walked half a kilometer in the dark to the station house — not because the train was running yet, but because the station-master's tabby cat, who has held this post unchallenged since 2018, would emerge from the heated waiting room only when bribed with a small piece of dried bonito.

The Tōkaidō, the eastern coast road, was begun under the Tokugawa within months of Sekigahara, in the first decade of the seventeenth century. By 1635, when the alternate-attendance system fixed the residency pattern of every domain lord in the country, the road carried — at any moment — perhaps three or four thousand persons of consequence, and four or five times as many merchants, pilgrims, hauliers, runners, and innkeepers' apprentices, all moving in both directions, by foot and by palanquin.

The First Hour Out of Edo

Hiroshige's prints render the route at the slowest available human pace. They are sequential not because they tell a story but because the road itself — five-day sections, six-day sections, a week of weather — was a story. To walk it, in 2024, is to feel how heavily station numbers depend on geography. The first ten dissolve themselves into the suburb of Greater Tokyo. The next fifteen are the corrugated coast of Sagami and Suruga, where Fuji is to the west and the sea is to the east, and they are equally close, and the road is the seam.

I supposed I'd been fit a normal 1832-walking distance, but my brisk pace down through Hodogaya was barely seven kilometres an hour against the wind, and the inn at the end of it was eighteen kilometres beyond. By the night I'd already begun to suspect the schedule had been written for stronger ankles than mine.

Hiroshige's genius was to draw not the road but the troubles of being on it — He invented a perfect ability to register a small humiliation and a real distance in the same image.

The Lake, the Pass, the Print

The fortieth of fifty-three stations is Ishiyakushi. Hiroshige's print of it gathers eight figures around a chestnut tree, none of them looking up at it. There is a thin red lacquer on the woman's straw hat. The pigment is now thought to be safflower, which Hiroshige knew would fade to peach in fifty years and to pink in a hundred and fifty; the print, like the season it depicted, was meant to be perishable.

I stood at Ishiyakushi-juku, what's left of it, for two hours. There is an unmown grass plot on the highway side and a sign that translates, with an editor's wry insistence, as: This is approximately the location of the inn that was probably here. A single chestnut tree leans into the light off the highway.

The cat had also done the same calculation, and had decided, before the inn-keeper's daughter could bring out a saucer, that the day would be too cold for poetry — that it would be a day for sleeping near the kerosene heater. We watched her decide, and did the same.

Where the Inn Still Stands

At the fifty-first, Kameyama, every cedar pole that survives the highway widening has been numbered, lacquered, and re-erected. A volunteer named Mr. Ikegawa, who is seventy-one and was sweeping the steps when we arrived, said the work has taken twenty-three years and is not yet finished. The inn-keeper's pictographs on the inside of the second-floor shutter — left there by a man who was a guest of his ancestors in 1837 — are now under a sealed plexiglass panel that costs the village eight hundred thousand yen a year to dehumidify.

I had walked four hundred and twenty kilometres looking for what is altered by attention to where it is in space. At Kameyama, with the inn-keeper's pictographs sealed under plexiglass, I found that I had been the one altered.

Arrival, Without Ceremony

What the conclusion takes from this: if Hiroshige did the impossible thing — drew a road as a sequence of fifty-three intelligible places — what we have done is the more modest thing. We have walked a road that no longer wishes to be a road, and asked only what it remembers of itself. The answer, in late September, is: a great deal, in patches; nothing at all, in stretches; and, in one chestnut grove at Ishiyakushi, the same dappled light it remembered in 1832.

The cat had decided, with the wisdom available only to cats and to bureaucrats nearing retirement, that the question itself was less interesting than its asking.

SIDEBAR · 補注

Five Stations Where the
Hooper Still Stands

Of the original fifty-three honjin (officials' inns), seven survive in any structural sense; only five are accessible to the public on a normal week. They are: Futagawa (1807), Hamamatsu (1813), Tsuchiyama (1779), Kameyama (1837), and Otsu (rebuilt 1922 to a Bunsei plan).

CARTOGRAPHY

The 1843 Kanseimei road-book lists 492.0 km from Nihonbashi to Sanjō Ōhashi. We measured 488.6 by GPS in 2024 — chiefly the difference of new bypasses around Hakone and Suzuka.

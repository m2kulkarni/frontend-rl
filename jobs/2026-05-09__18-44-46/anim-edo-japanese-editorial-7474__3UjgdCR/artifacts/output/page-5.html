Tsumago-
juku, in the quiet
of January.

A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the snow.
VILLAGE · 妻籠宿
PROVINCE · KISO, NAGANO
RESIDENT POPULATION · 174
ANNUAL VISITORS · 89,000
ELEVATION · 420 m
DISPATCH
15 January 2024
BY
Ryōsuke Mori,
Tsumago-juku, Nagiso
READ
14 minutes

The bus from Nagiso station lets you off at the lower end of Tsumago at five minutes past ten in the morning. By that hour the slate roofs already drop the small icicles they have been growing since dawn — six, seven, then a clatter of forty as the sun comes over the cedar line. The first hour in town is the icicles' hour. By eleven, the village is dry.

I had come to write about the village's preservation order, the 1968 ordinance that froze the eaves, signboards, and shingled roofs to a Bunsei-period plan. There is a particular satisfaction to writing about a place that has been declared, by law, to look like it does — but the geography of an old post-town does not, of course, take legal advice. The road bends because of the ridge, the ridge bends because of the river, and the river is in no negotiation.

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became Tsumago has been told often enough that the residents are tired of telling it; but it is one of the very few real success stories of village preservation in modern Japan. In 1968, after sixteen years of decline of the Kiso valley as a whole, the village mayor and the heads of the four eldest families signed a covenant: no neon signage, no aluminum sash; door fronts to remain hand-cut cedar; eaves to project no further than the original Bunsei plan; the highway re-routed; the souvenir trade refused.

The principles still hang, in a printed frame, in the entryway of the Tsumago-juku Preservation Society office. The volunteer named Mr. Takahashi, who is seventy-one and whose mother retains the seniority among that gener­ation, said: "It does not look beautiful because it is old. It looks beautiful because we agreed thirty years ago not to break the rules."

A dinner of mountain produce

I stayed at the Matsushiro Inn, four rooms above a restaurant that has served travelers, by the proprietress's reckoning, since 1834. Dinner on the second night was gohei-mochi — pounded rice pressed flat with a walnut miso, grilled over coals and pulled apart with the fingers. The ginger pickled to its left, the tea poured to its right.

I ate the gohei-mochi, the soup of unfermented miso, and rested afterwards on a futon laid by hand. In the corridor the floorboards talked back. The smaller of the two pillars in the main room is dated to a man named Kaze­matsu, in 1837. The chestnut floor has been polished, by the Inn since 1834, with the same recipe that the cedar fronts are.

On the morning of departure I measured the snow against the lower edge of the Matsushiro signboard with a folded notebook, forty-seven centimeters, just at the thickness of the lacquer. The biggest fall in twenty years and, by the proprietress's calm reckoning, "the third Tuesday running."

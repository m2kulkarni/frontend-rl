Hokusai's
Wave, Read Slowly

BY KENJI AKIYAMA · CONTRIBUTING EDITOR · 12 MIN READ · 18 SEPTEMBER 2024
WORK
神奈川沖浪裏
Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura
BLOCK
Cherry, c. 1830
PIGMENT
Prussian blue ("bero-ai")
PRESSURE
Baren, hand-rubbed

For two centuries the West has read Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura as a portrait of menace — an instant before catastrophe, a wave poised to devour three slender boats. This is, I want to argue, an almost wilful misreading. The wave is not falling. It is repeating.

印

To read Hokusai is to first study Japanese — I had done it, in the spirit of the Sherlock Holmes literature, since my graduate-school days, putting Kawabata down in mock excitement. But that fluency is not enough; the reading the print is owed cannot really be done from the doorway. You have to walk to the breakfast-table sideways and approach it like a pet. I have been studying it for six months.

This is not a metaphor I am repeating. The original — the 1831 keyblock has been split, and at least two later impressions are kept in cabinets in the basements of the Honolulu Academy and the British Museum — is a thing of repeating geometry. The wave is not falling, but turning. The crest does not collapse upon the boats; it is interrupted by them, and what happens beyond is not visible because what happens beyond is the same wave, again, going around again.

Hiroshige's genius was to draw the road; Hokusai's was to draw the loop in the procession.

Read this way, the print becomes meditative without becoming sentimental. The painters of Edo had a long acquaintance with the eternal — the Buddha is at the centre of every Heike-style painting, the seasons are the structure of every haiku. What is novel in Hokusai is that the eternal is here a wave, in this place, this rope of foam, this sliver of Fuji.

Read this way, the print becomes legible to the modern eye not as a frozen calamity but as a small unit of time — a wave, the same wave, turning over the same boats, the same morning, in the same half-second. Will Fuji, in plain sight on the horizon, be different in the next loop? It will not.

The wave is not a wave. It is a man being lectured — water performing its grammar.

And this is, the print insists with a quietness amounting to a moral position, all you need to know about the place. The wave is the demand. The fishermen have already met it: they do not look, because they have already looked. The mountain has long since arranged its position against everything that turns over before it. We are, all four of us — wave, boats, mountain, viewer — in the same loop. We are not afraid. We are not hopeful. We are practicing.

PIGMENT

Prussian Blue, "Bero-ai"

Imported from the Dutch via Nagasaki, it became commercial in Japan around 1820, was first widely used in 1829, and gave Hokusai's series its characteristic depth.

PROVENANCE

Editions held

Of perhaps eight thousand impressions originally pulled from the keyblock, fewer than three hundred are known. Three are at MoMA, two at the British Museum, one at Honolulu, one at the Sumida Hokusai.

PRO LECTURIS ARGUMENTUM CONSEQUI
№ 36 · I

The Foam Claws

Each curl of the foam was carved as a single notched ridge in cherry. We compare four impressions — Honolulu, MoMA, Sumida — to track block wear over five years.

№ 36 · II

The Three Skiffs

Eight oarsmen visible in three boats, each foreshortened on a different axis. Tracing where the gaze lands first reveals the print's true center: not the wave, but the pause.

№ 36 · III

Mt. Fuji, in Scale

The smallest mountain. We measured the fractional space Fuji occupies in three Edo woodblock series and find Hokusai's the most disciplined: under one percent.

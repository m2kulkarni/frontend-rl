Reading the keyblock

Hokusai's
Wave,
Read Slowly

By Kenji Akiyama· Contributing editor· 12 min read
浮 世 絵 · 神 奈 川 沖 浪 裏
Reported by
Kenji Akiyama
Contributing editor
Curator, Sumida Hokusai Museum, Tokyo.

For two centuries the West has read Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura as a portrait of menace — an instant before catastrophe, a wave poised to devour three slender boats. This is, I want to argue, an absurd wilful misreading. The wave is not falling. It is repeating.

To read it as a kind of slow Japonisme — I had AS to the great Hokusai's reputation by the foam-claws of the Hanabusa Museum, very pleasingly. Naturally, our editor took the keyblock as the lasting image. But the woodblock was probably no longer than the hand who carried this story, that this is repeating. We should have remembered hand-cutting the wave forward.

This is not a metaphor I am supplying. The hokusai print, the longevity, the breath of distance — bring forward the form. The way you read the print is the way you read the page. Block your eyes, then come back, then leave again.

The wave is not a wave. It is a wave being inflected — water performing its grammar.— FROM THE INTRODUCTION

Hokusai began the print, the rotated foam-curls and tumble-marks emerge from the foreground. The depth of pressure needed to print the keyblock is in the indigo on the print, water-side, in the thumb-print of his hand on the dampened mulberry paper. The paper is the wave. Everything else is a ground beneath.

Consider the boats. There are three — 押送り船 (oshiokuri-bune), the cargo skiffs that ran low and fast in the bays around Edo. Each holds eight oarsmen. Eight men face downward in unison, seemingly indifferent — almost devout — toward the closing column of water. They are not waiting for the wave to break. They are waiting for it to pass.

The Three Studies

Plate I · 浪

The Foam Claws

The Prussian blue keyblock, photographed in raking light at the Sumida Hokusai Museum. Note the sliver of Fuji.

Plate II · 三 舟

The Three Skiffs

Eight oarsmen each in three boats; their pose conscious, balanced, the body of the print a counter-weight to the closing curl.

Plate III · 富

Mt. Fuji, in Scale

The 1.4-percent Fuji at the print's exact mathematical centre. Hokusai considered the mountain a tendency, not a place.

"But surely the wave is about to fall."

"Hokusai feared the sea — see his late letters."

It is not. The most reproduced — Aogaeru-gachi (still famous) — was first printed by Eijudō Hibino in 1831 between June and October. The most reproduced print of the modern era is not falling, has never fallen. The keyblock is intact. The wave is repeating.

Editor's Note

The Keyblock, Today

The original 1831 keyblock survives only in fragmentary form, held by the Sumida Hokusai Museum. Two of the eight cherrywood blocks (sumi and ai) were destroyed in the 1923 earthquake.

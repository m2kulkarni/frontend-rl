浮世絵 · 04SLOW READING

Hokusai's
Wave,
Read Slowly

BY THE EDITORS
Junichirō Mori
Contributing Editor; Edo-period print culture

08 SEPTEMBER 2024 · TOKYO
第一節
I   THE OPTIC

For two centuries the West has read Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura as a portrait of menace — an instant before catastrophe, a wave poised to devour three slender boats. This is, I want to argue, an almost willful misreading. The wave is not falling. It is repeating.

To stand close to a first-state impression — I had 60 in the print room of the Suntory Toyokan Museum, on a grey Wednesday in May — is to feel the paper not as image and not as commentary but as technology. The crests are arrayed in fewer than fifteen colours. The boats are nearly absent.

This is not a metaphor I am importing. The insights — the bracing of the calligrapher's wrist against the linework's vertical, even the brace of the fisherman's foot against the bow's thwart, the very mathematics of the pivot — are all native to print itself.

Consider the boats. There are three — 押送船 — outshockers, fast boats run along the Boso coast in summer to bring fresh fish to the markets at Tsukudajima; their original audience would have known of them; the wave is not their first encounter, only their last.

The Hokusai reading of menace, I suspect, is a translation of a sentimental Romanticism brought home by the first European visitors. A well-read foreign visitor of 1880 would have seen the wave as the first metaphor for the unknowable Orient.

"What I want is property, then." said Van Gogh. "What I want is what makes printing inexpensive."

— Letter to Theo, June 1888

— PRE-LECTURE ARGUMENTS CONTINUED —
"But surely the wave is about to fall."
"Hokusai feared the sea — see his late letters."
It is not. The wave gestures — frozen against the spire of Fuji at the moment of repetition. Hokusai chose the form because that is what an everyday Edo audience would have read; the boats are the punctuation of an act that does not stop.

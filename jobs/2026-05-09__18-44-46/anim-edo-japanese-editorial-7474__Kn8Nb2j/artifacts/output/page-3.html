Naoko Furusawa works barefoot beside a 200-year-old earthenware vat sunk into the floor of a Nishijin machiya. She is the fifth Furusawa to do so. The vat, she explains, has not been emptied since 1834.

PLATE ONE
PLATE I — RAW VAT, MORNING — K. Aoyama, 03/24

That Kuoto can use 200-year-old fern fronds in the indigo bath is unsurprising. The lengthy fermentation of sukumo, the foundation, has needed two species of bacteria; not a thing has hurried it. What is unusual is that the chemistry of the vat — the species of beetles that lay eggs in the surface scum, even — is held to be a function of inheritance. A particular vat, in a particular town, sustained for several generations, becomes the home of a community of microorganisms that gives the cloth a tone unmatchable elsewhere.

A Conversation, Transcribed

TR  Could you describe the morning ritual — what comes first, when you walk in?

NF  I take the cover off, of course. Then I look at the surface and at what is on it. The flower — the white scum — has to be smooth. If it is broken, that means a temperature drop overnight, or that some breath of mine is the night before that I should have known about.

TR  Is the dye an old recipe, or something developed?

NF  Both. The recipe is the same. The development is what changes between us. My mother and I do not produce the same blue. The vat does, but we do not.

TR  You have refused synthetic indigo.

NF  Refused. Yes. There is no place in the city for the synthetic. The Edo dyers refused chemical reds in the 1860s and that was, I believe, the right choice. We do the same.

TR  What about the body — what about the apprenticeship?

NF  An apprentice gets eighteen months on the cover before she touches the vat. By that time her hands have been changed by the smell. Her smell becomes the smell of indigo.

第一節 · I
THE ROAD AS
OBJECT, NOT IMAGE

A bell, in the morning of the third of May, with the bronze plaque of Nihombashi still cold under my palm. I shouldered a backpack, took my first step on the road that would, in a quiet ceremony, become the same line Hiroshige drew in 1832 for his employer Kawatō, and which the press knew as Tōkaidō Gojūsan-tsugi — the Fifty-Three Stations. Four hundred ninety-two kilometers, by foot, with the same notebook, the same indecisions about whether to turn aside for a temple, and the same boredom afterward. By the end I knew, the way the prints knew, that the road was an object before it was an image.

Hiroshige's genius was to draw not the road but the weather of being on it — the moment a porter shifts his load, the second a cup is set down on a stone and not yet picked up again.

I started in Tokyo at 06:42. The next, when I lifted, was Shinagawa, three hours' walk on a cool overcast day. Hiroshige made eight separate sketches at Shinagawa, but I only knew that detail when I returned home.

第二節 · II — THE LAKE, THE PASS, THE PRINT

The fifteenth of the route became Hakone: the steepest climb and the gladdest descent. The pass through volcanic crater leaves the legs uncertain on the way down, and even my walking-stick — bought at Mishima from a daughter of the family who has cut them for ninety years — could not save me a sleeping limb. Up at the lake the inscription on the haiku-stone tells what every prefecture's tourist office now repeats: that this is the lake the woodblocks taught the world to see.

第三節 · III — WHERE THE INK STILL STANDS

At the fifty-second, the woodblock-cutter Tatsuya Suzuki met me at the door of his shed, smelling of resin and hinoki. The blocks his great-grandfather cut for the press are still there, three of them in a black-pine case so tight I had to slide it open with both hands. I asked whether he had cut copies — whether anybody, in a thousand years still to come, would still ink them.

"I had walked four hundred and twenty kilometers and the road was telling me very little. The conversation that mattered, that converged with the prints, was always inside a building."

脚注 · NOTES
A note on the prints in the museum at Mishima: the Suzuki family copies are catalogued as 1834, not 1832. The error was first identified by Sōen, but the same date persists in three Tokyo collections still.
Original keyblocks for stations 18, 22 and 49 are now in private hands abroad — last sold at auction in Bonn, March 2019.

Arrival, Without Ceremony

I had walked four hundred and twelve kilometers to stop in front of an awning where the proprietress was sweeping leaves into a bucket. There was nothing to mark it that did not also unmistakably mark it.

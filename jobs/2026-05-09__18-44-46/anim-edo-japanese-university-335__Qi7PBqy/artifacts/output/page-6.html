Hokurin Daigaku has been a small public university for seventy-three years, with a single campus in Higashiyama and a research culture shaped by the workers it studies — printers, papermakers, dyers, foresters.

— A SHORT HISTORY

From a Higashiyama yobi-tsu to a chartered university.

Hokurin Daigaku began in 1952 as a part-time evening college on the second floor of a silk-merchant's house in northern Kyoto. The first prospectus, run off in fifty copies on a borrowed printer, listed only three classes — kanbun composition, the geography of the Kamo basin, and "the natural history of the Higashiyama bamboo grove."

By 1972 the college had attached a faculty of craft sciences; by 1989 it had moved south to its present 1782 machiya compound on Sanjō-dōri. The 1991 Hachiya bequest of fourteen thousand woodblock prints quietly transformed the institution: from then on Hokurin understood itself as a small university with a national-grade special collection.

The university remains deliberately small. Its admission cycle has not changed in twenty years, its lecture rooms still seat twelve, and its term calendars still observe the closing of the Higashiyama season in early February.

The Hachiya Trust

The endowment that maintains the Tōrin Bunko also funds the partial- and full-tuition waivers held by sixty-six per cent of the undergraduate body. Decisions are made each spring by a committee of three faculty and a fourth member drawn at random from the student council.

Three founding principles

— PRINCIPLE I

Slow Reading

The university believes a difficult page rewards a slow eye, and the seminar is the institution that makes slow reading possible. Class sizes are kept at twelve.

— PRINCIPLE II

Common Hands

Faculty are required to make as well as study; the kiln, the vat, and the loom each are tended weekly by a rotating cast of professors and graduate students.

— PRINCIPLE III

Local Service

Hokurin returns its work to the watershed, the city, and the temples it studies — through pro-bono conservation, river surveys, and gate-side public lectures.

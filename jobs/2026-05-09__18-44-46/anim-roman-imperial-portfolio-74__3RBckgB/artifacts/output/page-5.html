SCRIPTA

WRITING & LECTURES

A chronological register of essays, lectures, and occasional polemics by Lucius Vennius — chiefly on the survival of Roman building grammar in the contemporary city, and on the patient discipline of the mosaicist's hand.

INDEX SCRIPTORUM
forty entries · MMXXIV — MMXXIV
MMXXIV · 2024
FEBRUARY · 202402

"Concrete Without Apology"

A defence of opus caementicium as a material with a moral inheritance, not merely a structural one. The essay traces the lineage from the Markets of Trajan to Pier Luigi Nervi, and argues that the polite refusal of cement has become, in some quarters, an evasion of difficult engineering questions.

JANUARY · 202415

"The Threshold is the Argument"

A short lecture given at the Scuola Normale di Pisa, January 2024. The threshold of a building, Vennius argues, is the only part of an architecture that cannot lie about its intentions. He reads the four-step entry of the Pantheon against the false ground floors of nineteenth-century Roman bank buildings, and finds the latter wanting.

MMXXIII · 2023
DECEMBER · 202312

"On the Coffer, as Structural Honesty"

Originally a footnote in a working letter to a client, this essay answered the long-standing question of whether the modern coffered ceiling is decoration or structure: Vennius argues that, in concrete, it is necessarily structural, and may not be skinned in plaster on a steel frame.

OCTOBER · 202306

"Travertine is a Slow Material"

Vennius's response to the question, repeatedly asked of the studio in 2023, of why a small chapel in Aquileia should take eighteen months to build. The first quarry-cut block at Tivoli has stood half a billion years; he asks why one would attempt a deadline.

JULY · 202319

"As Walks A Roman"

A lecture given at the British School at Rome, on the cadence of streets in the antique city, the average tessera dimension of which is consistently between 8 and 11mm — a discovery, Vennius argues, that has not yet been absorbed by historians of the Roman pavement. Walking, he argues, is the test of a paving.

MMXXII · 2022
NOVEMBER · 202211

"The Pediment Returns"

A 4,500-word essay arguing that the resumption of the classical pediment by certain American post-modernists in the 1970s — disreputable in European architectural circles when it occurred — has aged into something morally permissible, even valuable.

JUNE · 202204

"Against the Frameless Window"

A short polemic, distributed at a private architectural symposium in Bologna and never published, in which Vennius argues that the eighty-millimetre stone reveal is not a stylistic choice but a structural necessity, and ought to be defended at all reviews and against any client.

FEBRUARY · 202222

"Vesuvii, and the Patient Hand"

Vennius's ten-thousand-word account of the four months he spent at the dig of Boscoreale, learning the slow infringement of pumice from a Roman wall — and the lesson that conservation is a form of attentive listening, not of intervention. Read at the Soprintendenza, MMXXIII.

MMXXI · 2021
SEPTEMBER · 202115

"A Catalogue of Verb"

A short, 800-word piece (later much expanded) that became something of a manifesto. Vennius proposes that every Italian young architect should be given a catalogue of active verbs describing what a wall does — bear, retain, deflect, divert, frame — and asked to use no others in justifying a design decision.

MAY · 202128

"Notes from a Closed City"

A meditation, written in the third week of the Roman lockdown of MMXX, on the vacant streets of the Aurelian Walls — the closest thing, Vennius writes, to the experience of antique architecture. Its only contemporary reading is in the silence of the city.

MMXX · 2020
MARCH · 202007

"Why We Still Draw the Order"

In defence of the practical exercise, set every term to first-year students at La Sapienza, in which they must reproduce, by hand and at scale, the Doric order to Vignola's prescription. Vennius argues that no student understands the load-path of a column until he has drawn an entasis.

MMXIX · 2019
AUGUST · 201922

"Vitruvius at the Hardware Store"

A wry essay on the difficulty of buying lime mortar, gold leaf, and 0.35-mm Rotring nibs in a city that, two thousand years ago, would have supplied them on every block. Used as a teaching text by colleagues at the Politecnico, who report it gives mixed feelings to younger architects.

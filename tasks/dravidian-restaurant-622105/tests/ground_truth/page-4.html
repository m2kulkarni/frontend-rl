॥ Mandapam · The Pillared Hall ॥

The Hall & Hours

Step beneath the carved lintel of 47 Kutchery Road and you enter not a dining room but a chatram—a temple-town rest-house where pilgrim and householder alike are served on plantain leaf, beneath thirty-two pillars of teak that remember the gas-lit Mylapore of a century ago. We keep the doors open from before the first conch of dawn until the last bell of evening.

Mandapa

The Pillared HallCarved Teak · Brass · Plantain Leaf

The dining mandapam is enclosed by thirty-two pillars of carved teak, salvaged in the winter of 1971 from a derelict chatram built at Tiruvarur in 1908 by the merchant Naranappa Chettiar. Each pillar bears at its capital a different musician of the Chola court — a mridangam player here, a nagaswaram blower there — and a base of compressed lotus petals worn smooth by ninety winters of pilgrim hands.

At the centre of the hall stands a brass uruli, four feet across, in which jasmine and red lotus float on tepid water from sunrise to last service. The lamps in the four corner niches are filled each evening with sesame oil; the wicks are spun, every Friday, by Mrs. Padmavathy, who has tended this hall since 1979.

Diners are seated on low benches of jackfruit-wood facing the long granite plinth. The leaf is laid tip-to-the-left, washed with rosewater, and the meal commences only when the senior server has rung the small bronze bell beside the kitchen threshold.

The courtyard tables are stone, polished by four generations of elbows; in summer the pandal is rethatched with fresh palmyra. Reservation is suggested for the courtyard during the months of Margazhi and Vaikasi, when the music season and wedding feasts overlap.

The Daily Kolam

Each morning at 05:45, before the rice is set to soak, Mrs. Padmavathy refreshes the threshold kolam in rice flour and red brick powder. The pattern — sixteen lotus petals around a central nelli seed — has not been changed since 1979.

“The kolam is the first hospitality of the house — it greets the rising sun before any guest.”
— Mrs. Padmavathy, head of threshold rites since 1979

House Customs

Notes for the Honoured GuestOf Footwear, Of Photography, Of Children

  • The Inner Threshold Footwear is removed at the inner threshold marked by the brass step. Lockers of carved Burma-teak are provided to the right of the entry; the attendant returns slippers polished, at no charge.
  • The Plantain Leaf Each diner is served upon a fresh leaf cut that morning from the Saidapet groves. The narrow tip points to your left; sweets are placed at the top, savouries below, rice at the centre. Folding the leaf toward yourself at the end of the meal is the customary signal of contentment.
  • Hands & Vessels We serve in the Tanjore manner — by hand, with copper panchapaatra for water and silver tumblers for buttermilk. Spoons are available on request and brought without comment.
  • Photography Photographs of the hall and the food are welcomed. We ask only that the brass uruli, the kolam, and the small shrine to Annapurna in the north-east niche be approached with quiet feet.
  • Children Children are honoured guests; small leaves are laid for them and a half-portion of curd-rice with a single ripe banana is the traditional first course. There is a low cushioned bench at the courtyard end for grandmothers and infants.
  • Quiet Hours From 13:00 to 14:00 the hall observes a soft hush in honour of the noon offering at the Kapaleeshwarar Temple; conversation continues, but the ceiling fans are stilled and the radio is switched off.

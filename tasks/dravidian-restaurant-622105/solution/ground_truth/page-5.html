Chronicle of the House

The Four Hearths

I ✦ The Tiffin Counter

1947 ✦ Sri V. Subramaniam

In the smoke of Independence August, a 23-year-old cook from Kumbakonam set down a single coal-fired kal-chatti on the pavement opposite the eastern gopuram of Sri Kapaleeswarar Temple. He sold three things: pongal in palm-leaf cones at four annas, filter coffee in tumblers borrowed from his landlady, and a sweet akkaravadisal reserved for Friday devotees.

By 1953 the counter had a tin roof, two helpers from his native village, and the name his wife Rajalakshmi had insisted upon — Annapurna Bhavan, after the goddess who feeds without measure. He served standing, never sitting, until 1968.

II ✦ The Move to Kutchery Road

1971 ✦ Smt. Lakshmi Subramaniam

The eldest daughter, Lakshmi, returned from her short marriage in Madurai a widow at twenty-six, and took the ledger from her father's hand the morning of 11 June 1971. Within the year she had leased the ground floor of 14, Kutchery Road — a 1908 agraharam house with a granite-pillared thinnai — and moved every vessel down the street on a single bullock cart.

Her thali was the first in Mylapore to be served on a banana leaf laid over polished brass: eight kuzhambus, two payasams, and a rice mound she insisted be heaped, never flattened. Lunch was 1 rupee 75 paise.

❀ ✦ ❀ ✦ ❀

III ✦ The Chettinad Addition

1989 ✦ The Hearth Doubles

In the summer of 1989, Lakshmi welcomed into her kitchen Smt. Meenakshi Achi of Karaikudi, a Chettiar widow whose family hearth had been broken up. The two women — one Iyer-vegetarian, one Chettiar-omnivore — built a second kitchen behind the first, separated by a granite wall and a sandalwood door that has never since been opened on a Friday.

The Chettinad menu was launched on 14 August 1989 with seventeen dishes; today's card retains eleven of those originals, including the kuzhi paniyaram and the vellai paniyaram, both still ground on the same Karaikudi stone.

❀ ✦ ❀ ✦ ❀

IV ✦ Present Stewardship

2014 onward ✦ Kalyani & Arjun Iyer

Lakshmi's daughter Kalyani — trained as an architect in Bengaluru, returned in 2008 after her mother's stroke — formally took the seat in October 2014. Her son Arjun, who apprenticed three years under Chef Damu and a further fourteen months in the temple kitchens of Sringeri, joined her at the pass in January 2021.

Mother and son together now keep nine cooks, two coffee-roasters, and a sweet-house staff of four. Kalyani still personally tastes every sambar at 11.40 a.m., a ritual unbroken since her grandfather's tin-roofed counter.

A Closing Note from the Kitchen

Of Stone, Of Wood, Of Fire

Every masala served in this house is ground each dawn on a granite ammikkal by hand — turmeric from Erode, coriander from Guntur, the long red chilli of Ramanathapuram. We have refused the electric grinder since 1971; we shall refuse it tomorrow.

Every dosa is cast on one of three cast-iron thavas heated over tamarind and cashew wood. The griddle on the southern hearth was forged in Tiruvannamalai in 1962 and has never been washed with soap. It is wiped, between dosas, with half an onion and a thread of gingelly oil — as Sri Subramaniam wiped his, and his mother before him.

This is not nostalgia. It is method. The stone keeps the heat the cook intends; the wood gives the smoke the rice expects; the iron remembers the hand. We mean to keep all three remembering.

— Kalyani Iyer & Arjun Iyer ✦ Mylapore, Āvaṇi 2024

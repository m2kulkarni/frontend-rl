Gallery III · Spring–Autumn 1402 SH

The Inner Dome

Light, Pattern, and the Sheikh Lotfollah Tradition
14 March — 22 September
After the apex of the Sheikh Lotfollah dome ✦ Isfahan, 1028 AH / 1619 CE

Wall Text · Gallery III

Introduction to the Exhibition

Completed in the year 1028 AH (1619 CE) under the master builder Muḥammad Riḍā ibn Ustād Ḥusayn Bannā Iṣfahānī, the inner dome of the Sheikh Lotfollah mosque on the eastern flank of the Maydān-e Naqsh-e Jahān remains one of the most concentrated demonstrations of geometry and devotional intent in the Safavid corpus. Its surface is governed by a single radiating arabesque — a network of cream lozenges set against an indigo ground, contracting toward the apex where a sunburst of gold tessellation receives the light admitted from the perforated drum. From below, the dome appears to inhale.

This exhibition traces how that single ceiling reorganised the visual logic of interior programs across the seventeenth century. Designers working for Shāh ʿAbbās I, and later for Shāh Ṣafī and Shāh ʿAbbās II, returned again and again to its proposition: that pattern is not applied to architecture but is the means by which architecture admits light. The lozenge net of Sheikh Lotfollah migrates, in the decades after 1619, into the soffits of the Chehel Sotūn pavilion, into the spandrels of the Madrasa-yi Mādar-i Shāh, and onto the lacquered binding-boards that the royal kitābkhāna sent abroad as diplomatic gifts.

Twelve loaned tiles, drawn principally from the Sheikh Lotfollah conservation reserves and from three private waqf collections in Isfahan and Kashan, anchor the gallery. A short technical section presents new pigment analysis carried out jointly by Dār al-Naqsh and the Iran National Heritage Laboratories: it confirms — for the first time on this scale — that the cobalt blue of the dome's interior was sourced almost entirely from the village of Qamsar, south of Kashan, rather than imported via Hormuz as long supposed.

Pigment Analysis

The Cobalt of Qamsar

Spectroscopic study of nine apex fragments (catalogue Nos. III.1, III.2, III.6, III.8, and five non-displayed sherds) was completed in winter 1401 SH at the Iran National Heritage Laboratories under the supervision of Dr. Farhād Behnām. Trace-element ratios — particularly the As/Co and Ni/Co ratios — closely match ore samples from the cobalt-bearing zone above the village of Qamsar, in the highlands south of Kashan.

Earlier scholarship had assumed Safavid cobalt was largely imported via Hormuz from Saxon or Yunnanese sources. The Qamsar attribution forces a revision: by 1619, royal workshops were drawing pigment from a domestic mountain source no more than five days' caravan journey from Isfahan, fired in conjunction with locally calcined tin-lead opacifier.

Method & Display

A Note on the Hang

The twelve loaned tiles are mounted along a single curving wall whose radius approximates the inner profile of the Sheikh Lotfollah dome at one-quarter scale. Visitors enter beneath a low pendentive of plain cream plaster and pass into the gallery as the radius opens above them; tile labels are recessed into the dado so as not to interrupt the field of pattern when seen from the central axis.

Lighting is held at 75 lux on the painted surfaces and warmed slightly toward 2700 K to approximate the late-afternoon condition under which the dome's drum windows admit their fullest illumination. The ambient sound, recorded on site at the morning prayer of 19 Khordād 1401, plays at a level that does not overrun conversation.

Conservation note: tile III.8, the apex fragment, is shown horizontally rather than mounted vertically, in conformity with the loan agreement reached with the Sheikh Lotfollah trust. A pointed-arch viewing aperture frames the visitor's first encounter with the object, recapitulating in miniature the geometry of its original setting.

Curator's Note

By Dr. Shirin Mahdavi · Senior Curator of Architectural Ornament

I have wanted to make this exhibition for fifteen years. The inner dome of Sheikh Lotfollah is, for those of us who grew up walking the Maydān, less a building than a measure — the standard against which one privately judges every later interior, every claim of "Safavid revival," every architect's fond return to lozenge and palmette. To bring twelve of its tiles into a gallery is therefore an act of some delicacy. They were made to be seen at a distance of twenty-eight metres, from below, in the rotational drift of the eye that geometry rewards. Held instead at arm's length, in steady museum light, they ask a different question of us.

What this exhibition proposes is that the dome's afterlife — its diffusion through pavilions, madrasas, manuscript bindings, garden tile-work — was not a matter of motif being copied. It was a matter of a problem being inherited. The problem is how a flat, pressed, fired surface can persuade a viewer that pattern is breathing. Master Muḥammad Riḍā solved it once, in 1619, with a contraction toward the apex and a single warming pass of gold. His successors, working under the more attenuated patronage of the later seventeenth century, returned to that solution as one returns to a teacher's correction: not to repeat it, but to understand what it had asked.

I am grateful to the Sheikh Lotfollah trust, to the families holding the Mīr Findiriskī and Khwāja Naṣīr waqfs, and to my colleagues at the National Heritage Laboratories. The cobalt of Qamsar — long suspected, now confirmed — is, in its way, the quietest revelation of the show.

Dr. Shirin Mahdavi Isfahan, 22 Esfand 1401

The Royal Kitābkhāna— Manuscripts & Albums of the Safavid Court —

Within the painted walls of Tabriz, Qazvin and Isfahan, a single workshop sustained the most ambitious illustrated book of the Islamic world: the Shāhnāma of Shah Tahmasp, begun c. 1522 and pursued for nearly three decades by hands trained from Herat to Shiraz.

— I —The Workshop & the Book

No object survives Safavid taste so completely as the illustrated book. Begun in Tabriz around 1522 at the order of the young Shah Tahmasp I, the so-called Houghton Shāhnāma assembled, over more than two decades, two hundred and fifty-eight miniatures across seven hundred and fifty-nine folios. It is the largest royal painting cycle of pre-modern Iran, and the foundational document of the Safavid kitābkhāna — at once library, scriptorium and academy of ornament.

Tahmasp had been raised in Herat, where the late Timurid master Bihzād still presided over a workshop bequeathed to the Safavids in 1510. When the prince returned to Tabriz in 1522, he carried with him both the Herati conventions — meticulous architectural framing, a flat gold sky, modest figural scale — and the painters trained in them. Joined to the Türkmen sensibility already at work in the western capital, this collision produced what scholars now call the Tabriz school of the second decade: hot in palette, vertiginous in landscape, populated by demons and shahs alike.

— Marginal note, fol. 60v — “The painter Sulṭān Muḥammad has depicted a scene the like of which the revolving heavens have not seen.”

The administrative head of the workshop was the calligrapher Mīr ʿAlī al-Ḥusaynī, but the painted programme answered to two presiding talents: Sulṭān Muḥammad, a Türkmen-trained master whose Court of Gayumars (fol. 20v) anchors the book's opening, and the Herati-trained Āqā Mīrak, who carried Bihzādian decorum into the densest court scenes. By the late 1530s the dual idiom had hardened into a unified Safavid manner — the manner that would reach Qazvin under Tahmasp's nephew Ibrāhīm Mīrzā and, later, the studios of Isfahan.

II. Sulṭān Muḥammad's Brush— from the demon-haunted skies of Tabriz —

If a single painter defines the early kitābkhāna, it is Sulṭān Muḥammad. His Court of Gayumars — the first illustrated folio of the Tahmasp Shāhnāma — depicts the primordial king on his leopard-skin throne, encircled by a court clothed in the very pelts of the leopards they have tamed. The picture's astonishment lies not in its narrative content but in its surface: rocks dissolve into the faces of dīvs, clouds curdle into dragons, and gold ground becomes a vibrating field rather than a backdrop. The eye is given no rest, and is meant to be given none.

Contemporary critics were emphatic. Dūst-Muḥammad, writing his celebrated preface to the Bahrām Mīrzā album in 1544, named Sulṭān Muḥammad nādir al-zamān — “the rarity of the age.” The painter is documented in the kitābkhāna from c. 1505 until at least 1543, by which date he was supervising the illumination of the great Khamsa of Niẓāmī now in the British Library (Or. 2265). His brushwork — described by Dūst-Muḥammad as mū-yi qalam, a hair of a pen — is recognisable across both books in the small flickering highlights that animate every robe and rock.

III. From Herat to Tabriz— the migration of Bihzādian conventions —

The other current in the early Safavid book is the Herati. Bihzād himself, appointed head of the royal library in 1522, may have produced little new work in Tabriz before his death around 1535, but his pupils — Shaykh-Zāda, Mīr Sayyid ʿAlī, and the prodigious Āqā Mīrak — transmitted his vocabulary intact. From Bihzād they inherited the calibrated geometry of architectural settings (a palace courtyard built from tile-panels that echo real Herati practice), the careful spacing of figures so that none overlap, and the trick of cropping a horseman or a servant against the picture's edge to imply continuation beyond the frame.

What changed in Tabriz was scale and temperature. Herat had favoured intimate page-sizes and a cool ground; the Safavid royal page is larger, hotter, and more crowded. Bihzādian compositional discipline survives, but it is overlaid with a Türkmen love of swirling cloud-bands, of vermillion and lapis pressed against one another without modulation. By the time the workshop migrated to Qazvin under Tahmasp's later patronage (the capital moved in 1548), this synthesis had become canonical: it is the manner of the Haft Awrang of Jāmī, completed for Ibrāhīm Mīrzā at Mashhad between 1556 and 1565.

— Dūst-Muḥammad, 1544 — “Whatever has issued from the pen of Āqā Mīrak the eye of admiration has read as a chapter of paradise.”

IV. The Single Page & the Album— Riza-yi Abbasi and the figural study —

By the time of Shah Abbas I (r. 1588–1629), the great cyclical manuscript had given way to a different ambition. The royal kitābkhāna, now installed at Isfahan around the new Maydān-e Naqsh-e Jahān, increasingly produced single sheets — drawings, tinted studies, calligraphic specimens — gathered into the bound albums known as muraqqaʿ. These were objects of connoisseurship, leafed through in private rather than read aloud at court. Their economy was different: a single figure on a single page, framed by gold-flecked margins and a narrow band of Persian verse.

The decisive figure of this turn is Riżā-yi ʿAbbāsī (d. 1635), the painter most closely associated with Shah Abbas's personal taste. His mature manner — long-limbed youths in plum or saffron robes, leaning against a single tuft of grass, drawn with a calligraphic line that varies its weight from gossamer to ink-loaded — abandoned the crowded Shāhnāma stage entirely. A signed sheet of 1598 in our own reserve (see Folio VII opposite) shows the formula at its purest: an unnamed page-boy holds a wine-cup; the rest of the paper is blank but for a sprig of narcissus and Riżā's own signature in a corner. Patron and painter alike were satisfied.

The muraqqaʿ assembled under Abbas — most notably the Gulshan album, dispersed since the eighteenth century but partially reconstructable from fragments in Tehran, Berlin and our own Folio II — is the connoisseur's reply to the cyclical manuscript. Where the Tahmasp Shāhnāma had argued the legitimacy of kingship through a thousand-year story, the Abbasi album argued the legitimacy of taste itself: an ordered juxtaposition of Bihzād, Sulṭān Muḥammad, Mīr ʿAlī's calligraphy, and Riżā's own line. To open it, even now, is to be addressed as a connoisseur. That address — at once intimate and severe — is the kitābkhāna's lasting bequest to us.

The Workshop

Tabriz · 1522

Bihzād installed as kitābdār; the prince Tahmasp commissions a Shāhnāma of unprecedented scale. Painters from Herat and Türkmen Tabriz collaborate side by side under a single roof.

The Album

Tabriz · 1544

Dūst-Muḥammad composes his preface to the Bahrām Mīrzā muraqqaʿ — the earliest known critical history of Persian painting, and the source for nearly every name we still use.

The Single Sheet

Isfahan · c. 1598

Riżā-yi ʿAbbāsī, working at the new capital, signs a tinted drawing of a youth with a cup. The cyclical manuscript yields to the connoisseur's page; collecting itself becomes an art.

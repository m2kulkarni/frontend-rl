Galleries I — IV

The Permanent & Founding Collection

Four halls disposed along the central axis of the museum, devoted to the principal ornamental arts of Safavid Iran, 1501–1736 — assembled from acquisitions, bequests, and long-term deposits of the Naqsh-e Jahān Trust.

An Index of the Halls

The collection of Dār al-Naqsh is arranged along a single ivory axis running from the entrance vestibule to the rear courtyard. Each of its four halls treats one of the foremost ornamental disciplines that flourished under the patronage of Shāh Ismāʿīl, Shāh Ṭahmāsp, and — most particularly — Shāh ʿAbbās the Great, whose remaking of Isfahan in the late sixteenth century gave shape to the period style.

HALL · I

Tilework & Mosaic

kāshī-kārī  ·  کاشی‌کاری

The first hall traces the Safavid revolution in architectural ceramic, from the ascetic banna’i brickwork of Tabriz to the great haft-rang ("seven-colour") fields developed in Isfahan after 1598. Cobalt and turquoise dominate the palette, set against ivory kāshī, with manganese aubergine and a measured oxblood reserved — as in the original buildings — for emphasized inscriptions.

At the centre of the hall stands a haft-rang panel from the Masjid-e Shāh dado, recovered during the 1932 conservation campaign and entered into the collection through the Maydān Trust. Its split-leaf arabesque, signed in the lower border by the workshop of Ustād Muḥammad Riẓā Imāmī, is the touchstone of the gallery.

HALL · II

Manuscript & Album

muraqqaʿ  ·  مرقّع

The second hall presents the painted folio and the bound muraqqaʿ album: the great Safavid invention by which finished paintings, calligraphic exercises, and even European prints were assembled into the patron's private gallery. The room is lit low, the cases shallow, and pages are rotated quarterly to preserve their gold and lapis.

The keystone is a single folio attributed to Riẓā-yi ʿAbbāsī (c. 1605) — a seated youth holding a wine-cup, the contour drawn in a fluid, calligraphic line that became the model for the next two generations of Isfahani painters. The folio entered the Dār al-Naqsh in 1961 as part of the Mostofī bequest, accompanied by four further leaves from the same dispersed album.

HALL · III

Carpet & Textile

qālī-bāfī  ·  قالی‌بافی

Hall III gathers the great court textiles of the seventeenth century: silk velvets and lampas from Yazd and Kashan, embroidered termeh from Isfahan, and the silk-and-silver-thread carpets which Europeans, mistaking their origin, called "Polonaise". The hall is hung in shallow daylight, the most fugitive pieces protected by linen veils.

The principal object is a Polonaise carpet fragment, Kashan, early 17th century, woven on a silk warp with brocaded silver-gilt thread now darkened to a soft grey. It preserves a complete corner cartouche and a portion of the floral lattice; comparable pieces survive in Krakow, Vienna, and the Gulbenkian. Acquired at auction in Geneva in 1974 with funds from the Avicenna Society.

HALL · IV

Architectural Fragment

pāra-ye miʿmārī  ·  پارهٔ معماری

The final hall is the most intimate, devoted to the smaller members of Safavid building: carved stucco muqarnas cells, plaster cartouches from demolished pavilions, fragments of marble inscription, and — at its centre — the wooden bracketry of the urban house. Each piece is mounted at the height it originally occupied, so that the visitor walks beneath them as in a vanished room.

The presiding object is a carved teak column-bracket from a private Isfahani residence, datable on stylistic grounds to c. 1660 and removed before the 1968 demolition of a merchant house in the Jūbāra quarter. Its split-palmette scroll is closely related to the brackets of the ʿĀlī Qāpū's tālār; the piece entered the museum in 1969, the gift of the Khalili family.

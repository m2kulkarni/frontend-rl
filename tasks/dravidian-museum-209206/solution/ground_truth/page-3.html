❁ Essay I · pp. 14–28 Proportional Studies

The Stepped Tier A Reading of the Brihadeeswarar Vimana

By Prof. Kamala Venkataraman · Chair of Dravidian Architecture, University of Madras; Senior Curator, Department of Stone, this Museum.

When Rajaraja I commissioned the Rajarajesvaram in CE 1010, the masons of his court did not begin with a tower; they began with a square. The base of the great vimana at Tanjavur measures 96 feet to a side, and the structure rises 216 feet from plinth to stupi. These two numbers — 96 and 216 — are not arbitrary. Their ratio, 9 : 20.25, encodes a system of nine ascending talas, each of which is itself derived from a ninefold subdivision of the basal square.1 The result is a monument that reads, to the eye, as a single ascending pyramid, and to the measuring rod as a strict harmonic progression.

What the present essay proposes is that the Brihadeeswarar tower is not, as has often been claimed, a unique experiment in Chola gigantism, but rather the most rigorous surviving demonstration of a proportional canon already implicit in the smaller Pallava vimanas at Kanchipuram. The early-tenth-century Naltunai-Isvaram at Punjai, surveyed by this Museum's Field Office in 1987, exhibits the same 9:20.25 elevation in a structure barely 38 feet tall.2 The Tanjavur tower scales the canon; it does not invent it.

“The vimana is not a pile of storeys but a single thought, divided ninefold so that the eye may climb it without effort, and the rod may verify it without remainder.” — from the author's 2019 Annamalai Lectures

The thirteen visible talas of the Tanjavur vimana — counted from the lower adhisthana to the recessed neck below the stupi — are not thirteen equal divisions. The lowest two register as a single doubled storey (the dvitala), each face articulated by paired pilasters and shallow kumbha-panjara niches. Above this, eleven diminishing tiers step inward at a constant rate of 1:1.0825, a figure derived from the cube root of 1.27 — itself the ratio of the basal square's diagonal to its side.3

This recursive geometry has a perceptual consequence: the tower appears, from the floor of the inner courtyard, to recede toward a single vanishing point above the central axis. The mason who specified this ratio — almost certainly Kunjaramallan Rajaraja Perunthachan, named in the foundation inscription on the south wall of the sanctum — was not solving a problem of stability. He was solving a problem of vision.

The capping stupi, an 80-tonne monolith of fine-grained granite quarried at Sittannavasal some 60 kilometres distant, was raised, according to local tradition, along an earthen ramp originating at the village of Sarapallam. The tradition is unverifiable in detail but consistent with the engineering constraints: no contemporary lifting apparatus could have hoisted such a mass vertically. What the ramp left behind, however, is the proportional armature itself, fossilised in stone.

It remains an open question — one this essay does not pretend to settle — whether the ninefold canon was understood by the masons as a ritual diagram (the vastupurusha-mandala of the Mayamata) or as a workshop convenience. The two readings need not be exclusive. In Dravidian practice, as the Periya Puranam reminds us, the ritual and the rule of thumb are frequently the same gesture, performed twice.

❁ Essay II · pp. 29–46 Conservation Report

Polychromy at Madurai Pigment, Plaster, Patronage

By Dr. Iyappan Nadar · Director, Conservation Laboratory, this Museum; formerly Field Conservator, Meenakshi-Sundareswarar Restoration Campaigns 1995–2003.

It is by now common knowledge among South Indian conservators that the painted figures of the Madurai gopurams — twelve thousand of them, by the Pandyan-era inventory of 1623 — are not, in any meaningful sense, “original.” The polychromy visible to a visitor to the Meenakshi temple in 2024 is the result of a continuous, contested, and often contradictory tradition of repainting, the most recent campaign of which concluded in November 2003 under the supervision of the present writer. What is less commonly understood is that the plaster beneath the paint — the lime-and-chukkam ground onto which each generation has applied its pigments — preserves a stratigraphy as legible, in its way, as the foundation inscriptions of Tanjavur.

This essay summarises the findings of the 1995–2003 campaign, which removed, recorded, and replaced the polychromy of the South Gopuram (the Tenkayilai), and offers preliminary remarks on what those findings imply for the patronage history of the temple between the late Nayak period and the consolidation under the Nattukottai Chettiars in 1959.

“Beneath the cobalt-blue Shiva of 1959, beneath the indigo Shiva of 1908, beneath the lapis Shiva of 1814, we found a Shiva painted in the bone-white limewash of the late Nayak ateliers — a figure not coloured but only outlined, awaiting a polychromy that the Carnatic Wars deferred.” — Field Diary, 11 February 1998

The South Gopuram preserves nine distinct paint layers, separated by lime washes of varying thickness. The lowest layer, dated by stylistic and inscriptional means to the reign of Tirumalai Nayak (1623–1659), is in fact monochrome: a thin red-ochre kavi outline against the unpigmented plaster.1 The earliest fully polychrome layer — the second from the bottom — has been dated by accelerator mass spectrometry of an organic binder to 1742 ± 15, that is, to the brief revival of patronage under Vijaya Raghunatha Sethupathi of Ramanathapuram.2

The pigments of this 1742 layer are exceptional. Rather than the locally available red ochres and yellow earths that dominate later campaigns, we identified malachite (copper carbonate) and lapis lazuli (lazurite) — the latter, almost certainly, imported from the Badakhshan mines via the Coromandel trade. Two hundred and seventeen grams of lapis were consumed in the 1742 polychromy of the South Gopuram alone. At the import prices reconstructed from the Dutch Compagnie ledgers preserved at Pulicat, this represents an outlay of approximately 480 pagodas — roughly the annual revenue of three medium-sized temple villages.3

The third layer (1814 ± 8) abandons lapis entirely in favour of Prussian blue, then a recent European import distributed through the Madras presidency. The substitution is technical, economic, and — I would argue — ideological: the deep, cold lazurite of the Sethupathi atelier gives way to a brighter, slightly greenish blue more easily renewed under conditions of contracted patronage. Each subsequent layer continues this drift: toward synthetic pigments, toward faster-drying binders, toward a polychromy meant to be replaced rather than preserved.

The 1959 layer — the campaign of Sri Arumuga Nainar, master painter of the Chettiar consortium — is the first in the sequence to have been documented photographically before application. Black-and-white plates from his studio, deposited with the Museum in 1981, allowed the present campaign to reverse certain of the Nainar atelier's more controversial chromatic choices (notably the saffron Ganesa of the second tier, restored in 2001 to a period-appropriate vermilion).4

The 1995–2003 campaign cost ₹3.4 crore, was funded jointly by the Tamil Nadu Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Department and a private subscription drive among the temple's hereditary patron families, and consumed 11 tonnes of slaked lime, 380 kilograms of pigment, and the labour of 47 painters working in three shifts over eight winter seasons. No lapis lazuli was used. We did, however, set aside a small reserve of malachite — twelve grams — for the next generation.

❁ Essay III · pp. 47–61 Iconographic Survey

The Yali at the Threshold A Survey across Vijayanagara Mandapas

Compiled by the Department of Stone, with contributions from R. Ramaswamy (Hampi), G. Mariyappan (Tadpatri), and the late Prof. S. Ananda Krishnan (Lepakshi, posthumous).

No other creature in the Dravidian repertoire is so widely distributed and so poorly defined as the yali. Lion in the chest, elephant in the brow, horse in the gait, sometimes serpent in the tail — the yali is a composite that resists the taxonomies of the Silpa Sastras, which describe him only obliquely, under the umbrella term vyala. He is, in the surviving record, almost entirely a creature of the threshold: he guards the entry to the mandapa, he leaps from the pillar's lower drum, he flanks the steps that climb to the inner shrine.

This essay does not propose a definition. It proposes, instead, a survey: of 1,447 yali figures recorded across nine major Vijayanagara mandapas between 2014 and 2023, organised by posture, by attendant figure, and by the height at which they appear above the floor of the hall. The dataset, deposited with the Museum's Field Office under accession TMSS/FD/2023/06, is open to scholars on application.

“He is the threshold itself made animal: the moment at which the worshipper, having left the world, has not yet entered the shrine. To pass between the yalis is to be, briefly, neither here nor there.” — S. Ananda Krishnan, lecture notes, Lepakshi, 2019

The earliest yalis in the Vijayanagara corpus, those of the Vitthala temple's Maha-mandapa at Hampi (c. 1422–1446), are relatively restrained: a leonine torso rising from the drum of a granite monolith, the elephant's brow only suggested in the swept-back curve of the ear. By the late sixteenth century, in the work of the Tadpatri ateliers, the yali has acquired a small mounted warrior between his shoulders, and beneath his belly a second, smaller animal — usually an elephant, occasionally a horse — that he tramples or, more often, simply contains. The sculptural intelligence of the Tadpatri masons lies precisely in this nesting: the yali is not merely a guardian but a vessel, a creature whose own interior is populated.

At Lepakshi (c. 1538) the yalis of the kalyana-mandapa achieve an unusual horizontal continuity: the eight pillars of the western range are linked by a single sculpted band in which the yalis face alternately inward and outward, producing a rhythmic pulse that the worshipper, walking past, registers as a kind of slowed heartbeat. The late Prof. Ananda Krishnan called this “the cardiac mandapa,” a phrase the present writers retain with affection if not full conviction.1

Of the 1,447 figures in the survey, 89 percent carry an attendant — a small armed warrior, a dwarf, a coiled serpent — between their shoulders or beneath their forelegs. Eleven percent stand alone. The lone yalis cluster, suggestively, at the outermost thresholds: the entry porches, the southern colonnades, the steps descending to the tank. The yali becomes most singular, that is, where the boundary between sacred and profane is most sharply drawn.

A final note. In every one of the nine mandapas surveyed, the yali's mouth is open. In none is the mouth closed. The Silpa Sastras are silent on this point; local tradition, recorded by R. Ramaswamy at Hampi in 2018, holds that the yali is roaring — not at the worshipper, but at whatever follows him in. We pass between two roars and enter the shrine in the silence between them.

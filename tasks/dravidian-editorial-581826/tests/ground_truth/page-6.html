Correspondence — Letters to the Editor

In reply to "The Roof of Gold: On the Re-Gilding of the Chit Sabha at Chidambaram" (Vol. XII, No. 2)

On the Question of the Plate-Beaters

From Sri P. Subbaraya Dikshitar — Chidambaram, 14 Aippasi 1947

Madam, — I write as one whose family has stood at the threshold of the Chit Sabha for nine generations. The notice given to the gilders of Kanchipuram in your last issue was just; but the writer has been silent upon the role of the temple's own plate-beaters, who have for centuries thinned the gold to the breath required by the rafters.

Without the patient hammer of these households — some of whom number their craft from before the days of Vikrama Chola — no gilder, however accomplished, may finish his work. I would ask the Quarterly to record their names in a future number, lest a craft of nine hundred years pass unnoticed.

— P. Subbaraya Dikshitar

Concerning the Copper Beneath

From Smt. Saroja Ramachandran — Mylapore, 21 Aippasi 1947

Dear Editor, — Sri Ramaswami's essay was a delight to all of us who attended the rite of consecration in Vaikasi last. Yet I observe, with respect, that no mention is made of the under-plate of copper which receives the gold leaf and from which, in the wet years of the late 'thirties, several sheets were observed to have lifted.

The gilders of today, learned as they are in the application of the leaf, have inherited little of the older smiths' knowledge of the bedding metal. Should not the Quarterly, in its commendable mission, also press for the recording of these allied trades before they too vanish into the salt air of our coast?

— Saroja Ramachandran

A Small Correction, Respectfully Offered

From Sri A. Padmanabhan, Retd. Engineer — Tanjavur, 30 Aippasi 1947

Madam, — The fine essay in your last issue states that the present roof of the Chit Sabha bears 21,600 tiles of gold, corresponding to the number of breaths drawn by a man in the day. The figure is well-loved and often repeated; yet the survey conducted in 1929 by the late Mr. Longhurst counted, after the renewal works, 21,612 plates.

I draw attention to this not in any spirit of correction, but because the additional twelve plates — one for each rasi — were laid at the express direction of the then Dikshitar elders, and form a charming addendum to the symbolism. Perhaps your readers will care to know.

— A. Padmanabhan

A Folio from the Mackenzie Collection

Colin Mackenzie's notes from the Vijayanagara survey of 1799, with the ink-line plate of the Vitthala temple's stone chariot — and a re-examination of the draughtsmen, those Telugu brothers Kistnaji and Lakshmaiah, by whose hand the British Empire saw what it claimed to see.

Edited & introduced by P. Sundaresan, Madras Record Office, Egmore

Editor's Commentary

The folio reproduced opposite came into the keeping of the Madras Record Office in the autumn of 1944, returned from the India Office Library at Whitehall as part of a larger transfer of triplicate survey papers. It is one of three nearly identical drawings of the stone chariot at the Vitthala temple, ruined Vijayanagara, executed during Colin Mackenzie's first private survey of the Tungabhadra ruins in October and November of 1799 — six years before he was confirmed Surveyor-General of Madras.

The plate is, by any honest accounting, a beautiful object and a poor record. The wheels are too small; the niches of the plinth too even; the four registers of the shrine compressed by perhaps a fifth. The yali brackets are present but mute, their snarling faces softened into the bracket-stops of an English garden temple. What is missing — what no draughtsman of 1799 was given the means to render — is the fact that the chariot does not stand alone, but is the visual hinge of a long axial composition that begins at the eastern gopuram and resolves in the kalyāna-mandapa beyond. Mackenzie surveyed; he did not see.

“The stone Car is of one block, very curious in its workmanship, drawn (as is said) by Two Horses also of stone. The Brahmins assured me it had once moved upon its wheels, but this I cannot believe.”
— Mackenzie, MS. journal, 14 Octr. 1799

That last sentence — this I cannot believe — is the whole of the matter. The stone wheels of the Vitthala chariot do, of course, turn; the priests were not lying; the temple's traditions of festival processional movement are recorded in epigraphical sources Mackenzie never read because they were not translated. The limit was not the eye but the frame.

It is in this gap — between what was drawn and what was there to be drawn — that the work of the Telugu draughtsmen Kistnaji and Lakshmaiah, brothers from Niduvrolu in the Krishna delta, becomes visible in our own century as the more interesting historical fact. They did the seeing. The signed Mackenzie folios — perhaps four hundred sheets, by Lakshmaiah's own count submitted to the Asiatic Society in 1818 — were drawn either entirely by them or, more often, drawn by them and then laboriously re-inked by a European hand for the despatch copy.

— P.S., Egmore, the 11th of Aippasi.

From the Survey Journal of Colin Mackenzie

Vijaya-nagara · Octr. 14th–17th, 1799 · transcribed verbatim, original spelling preserved

1This day rode out to the ruined Pagoda of Vittala-Sami, situate about a mile to the Northeast of the bazaar of Hampee, the country between being broken with great masses of granite and certain enclosed gardens of plantain belonging to the Mahomedan Killadar.

The Pagoda is of a very superior workmanship, far exceeding any thing seen by me in the Carnatic. 2The principal court is entered by a Gopuram now much decayed, its upper stories fallen; within is a Mantapam of fifty-six pillars, each pillar carved into a cluster of slender colonnettes which, when struck with the hand, return distinct musical tones. The Brahmin guide named one cluster Sa-ri-ga-ma; another, the seven notes of the vīna.

Before the inner sanctuary stands the Stone Car — vide the drawing made this day by Lakshmiah — which is the principal curiosity of the place. 3It is of one block, or so I at first supposed; on closer inspection finding it to be assembled of perhaps fourteen courses of granite so finely jointed as to deceive the eye at twelve paces. The wheels are pierced in the manner of a chakra, and are said by the priests to have formerly turned. This I cannot believe, though the axles bear the wear of motion.

Two stone horses, much defaced by Mahomedan iconoclasm of the year 1565 (so the Brahmins reckon it), draw the car. 4The car itself bears upon its plinth a frieze of the ten avatars; that of the Boar (Varāha) is the most perfectly preserved, the others having lost their upper members.

I caused Kistnaji to take the dimensions, which he records as: length of plinth, fourteen feet two inches; height to the finial, twenty-three feet six; diameter of wheel, three feet eleven. 5These I have verified with my own chain. The drawing is taken from the South-West, the morning light falling oblique upon the East face, which is the principal.

I am told by the head Brahmin, one Vēnkata-rāma Sastri, that an annual festival was formerly held at which the car was drawn around the inner enclosure by ropes — but this practice was discontinued in the time of the Sultan, no festivals being now performed at the place. 6He shew'd me also certain inscriptions in the Cannada character upon the basement of the East Gopuram, of which Lakshmiah took copies to be translated at our return to Bellary.

Kistnaji of Niduvrolu

Senior draughtsman, c. 1796 – 1818

Born to a family of Niyogi Brahmins of the Krishna delta, Kistnaji entered Mackenzie's service in March 1796 at Cumbum, having previously been employed as a karanam-clerk under the Nizam's revenue. He was the elder of the two brothers and the more architecturally exact — his measured drawings of the Tirumala temple, made over four months of 1803, are still the working reference for the conservation works at that site.

He died at Madras in February 1818, aged about forty-six, of fever; his half-finished plan of the Lepakshi mandapa is filed at I.O.L. Mackenzie Drawings, vol. 5, f. 112.

Lakshmaiah, his brother

Translator-draughtsman, 1797 – 1841

The younger brother by some four years, Lakshmaiah survived the Colonel by twenty years and rose, after Mackenzie's death in 1821, to be Native Head of the Survey Office at Madras — the first Indian to hold that station. The Vitthala chariot folio is in his hand throughout: drawing, lettering, and the Telugu marginal note (excised from the despatch copy but visible on the second triplicate) which reads, simply, idi nā cēta vrāyabaḍinadi — this was written by my hand.

His memorial volume, An Account of the Antiquities of Vijayanagara (Madras, 1836), remains uncited in every standard British survey of the ruins.

The Limits of the Frame

A note on antiquarian recording

The Mackenzie folios were drawn for a particular kind of looking: the elevation, the plan, the section, the picturesque view. None of these conventions was suited to a Dravidian temple, whose architectural grammar is processional, axial, and ritually performed — understood by the body that walks the prakara, not by the eye that stations itself at thirty paces South-West.

This is no fault of Kistnaji or Lakshmaiah, who knew perfectly well what they were leaving out. It is the fault of the frame they were given, and of the patrons who never asked them to draw differently.

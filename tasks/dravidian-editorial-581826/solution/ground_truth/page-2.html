Lead Essay · Aippasi 1947

The Granite Vimana
at Tanjavur

On structural logic, the quarry-yards of Tiruchirappalli, and the eighty-tonne corbelled cap-stone that crowns Rajaraja's Brihadeeswarar.

By Dr. Ananthapadmanabhan Sastri · Photographs & Plates by the Author

A meditation on stone, geometry, and the unbroken descent of measure — from the quarry-faces above the Kaveri to the gilded finial sixty-six metres above the prakara floor.

By Ananthapadmanabhan Sastri · read at the Madras Antiquarian Society, 4th Aippasi

To stand within the second prakara of the Brihadeeswarar at Tanjavur, in the cold blue hour before the temple bells, is to recognise that stone — the dense, biotite-flecked granite of the Pachchaimalai outcrops — has here been compelled to perform a feat which neither timber nor brick could survive. The vimana rises in thirteen progressively diminishing tiers from a base of twenty-nine kol square to a finial sixty-six kol above the pavement; and yet there is no central pole, no buttress, no flying brace. The whole, weighing perhaps forty thousand tonnes, balances on its own subdivided geometry.

What follows is offered not as an exhaustive monograph — for that one must still consult the great Madras Epigraphical Reports of 1893 and Pierre Pichard's measured plates — but as a reading of the logic of the structure: the quarry, the corbel, and the mandala that governs both.

I. The Quarrying at Tiruchirappalli

The granite is not local. The alluvial Kaveri delta, on which Tanjavur sits, yields no stone of consequence; one must travel some fifty miles north-west, into the low Pachchaimalai range above Sittannavasal, before the parent rock surfaces. Here, in shelves of charnockite so dark it photographs black, the Chola masons opened quarries that are still legible in 1947 — long parallel grooves cut by iron wedges, the negative print of the temple itself laid into the hillside.

The cap-stone is not a single block, nor was it ever lifted as one. It is a corbelled assembly — a closed dome of mortarless granite — that resolves its own weight into the shoulders of the tier below.

The technique is simple in description and ferocious in execution. A line of holes was driven along the intended fracture; dry teakwood plugs were hammered home and then soaked. Overnight, the swelling timber prised the stone open along a clean plane. Sthapati Kunjaramallan Rajaraja Perunthachan, named in inscription 29 of the south wall, supervised at least three such yards simultaneously between A.D. 1003 and 1009. The blocks — some of them four kol by two by two, a working weight near nine tonnes — were dragged on rolling logs of palmyra to the Kaveri, ferried during monsoon flood, and re-mounted onto sledges for the final crawl into Tanjavur.

One may compute, from the surviving inscriptions of grain-rations to labourers, that the temple absorbed something like 130,000 tonnes of dressed granite over six and a half years. This is not, by ancient-world standards, a vast figure; the Khufu pyramid demanded twenty times as much. What distinguishes the Tanjavur work is not bulk but concentration — the entire mass is gathered onto a footprint smaller than the courtyard of a Madras bungalow.

II. The Tiered Logic

If the plan is a square subdivided into sixty-four equal cells, the elevation is a sequence of squares shrinking by one kol per tier. The eye reads this as taper; the engineer reads it as the systematic reduction of mass at each rising course, so that bending moment at the base is held within the compressive tolerance of charnockite (which Pichard and his successors have measured at upwards of 1,800 kg per square centimetre — far in excess of what the structure ever asks of it).

Each tala is itself a miniature shrine, with cornice (kapota), wall-pilaster (bhitti), and a register of niche-figures (devakoshtha). The figures are not decoration in our anaemic modern sense — they are the formal grammar by which the wall declares itself a wall, and not merely a stack.

Above the thirteenth tier sits the griva, the neck-course, an octagonal drum into which the four cardinal nasikas open. And above that, the famous shikhara: the corbelled dome.

III. The Cap-Stone of Eighty Tonnes

Popular tradition — repeated even in Fergusson — holds that the apex stone of the Brihadeeswarar weighs eighty tonnes and was rolled into position up an earthen ramp four miles long, beginning at the village of Sarapallam ('the scaffold-hollow'), where the ramp's lower end is still pointed out by cultivators. Local memory of an event nine centuries old must be treated with affectionate scepticism, but the figure of eighty tonnes is, on measurement, very nearly correct: the visible stupi-stone, an octagonal cushion of about 7.7 kol across and 3.8 deep, computes to between 78 and 81 tonnes, depending on the granite's specific gravity at the quarry.

What ascends is not a monolith but a convergence: every block above the neck-course leans inward by a calculated angle, and each is locked, dry, by the weight of the next.

It is here that the most beautiful misconception of all must be dispelled. The cap-stone is not a single block, nor was it ever lifted as one. It is a corbelled assembly — a closed dome of mortarless granite — that resolves its own weight into the shoulders of the tier below. The 'eighty-tonne' figure refers to the entire converging mass of the shikhara, finial included; the largest single block within it weighs between twenty-five and thirty tonnes, well within the capacity of an inclined ramp and a thousand bonded labourers, working at the calm pace of three or four kol per day.

The corbelling is the whole secret. Each course steps inward; each course is held by the dead-load of the course above; and the closure is sealed by a smaller octagonal slab — the kalasa-bearer — onto which the gilt copper finial was finally fitted. There is no keystone. There is no mortar. There is only weight, geometry, and the perfect indifference of granite to time.

IV. The Continuing Practice

One walks out of the Brihadeeswarar through the Keralantakan tiruvasal, into the noon glare of the outer prakara, and is met — even now, in 1947 — by men who know exactly how this thing was made. The sthapati-lineages of Kumbakonam and Mamallapuram, whose hands still cut the ritual shilpa for new shrines from Tiruvaiyaru to Singapore, retain in their silpa-nool manuscripts the same proportional canon that guided Kunjaramallan a thousand years ago. The kol is still in use; the pada-vinyasa is still drawn in chalk on the dressing-floor before the first cut. The vimana of Tanjavur is not, in this sense, a ruin or a relic. It is the most successful surviving example of an active tradition.

That this tradition was, at its height, capable of placing a corbelled granite dome two hundred feet in the air, dry-laid, on a square footprint of ninety-six feet, is something the architectural West has still scarcely understood. To understand it, one must do as Rajaraja's masons did: begin with the square, divide it into sixty-four, and trust the geometry to carry the weight.

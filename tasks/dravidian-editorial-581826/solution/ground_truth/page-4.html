Sthapatis of the Mamallapuram School

Three masters of the Government College of Architecture and Sculpture, in their own words on proportion, ornament, and the consecration of the kalasha.

From the Editor's Notebook · Mamallapuram, Aippasi

"The vimana stands because the sthapati has measured it twice — once with the rod, once with the heart." So says the workshop proverb on the shore at Mahabalipuram, where for thirteen centuries the granite has been read as scripture. We spent three days in the courtyards of the Government College of Architecture and Sculpture, watching three of its quietest masters at their measuring rods.

I.  On Proportion · Talamāna

M. Ganapathi Sthapati

Senior Instructor, b. Pillaiyarpatti 1898 · Forty-one years at the chisel · Interviewed in the courtyard of the College, 14 Aippasi 1947, by R. Krishnamurthy

Of the three men who hold the iron rod at Mamallapuram, Ganapathi is the one who speaks of measure. To him a vimana is not a structure but a body, and the navatāla — the ninefold canon — is the spine that runs through it from kumbha to kalasha.

"Without proportion," he says, settling on the stone bench beneath the kadamba tree, "there is no temple. There is only stone arranged in a heap. The śāstra is not a prison; it is the breath by which the granite stands erect."

His rod, the daṇḍa, is cut to twenty-four aṅgulas — finger-widths — and from this single instrument he derives the height of every moulding in the prastara, the spacing of the pilasters along the wall, even the curvature of the śikhara above. "The Mānasāra speaks of nine talas for the principal deity," he explains, "but the Mayamata permits ten for the standing Vishnu. We follow the local lineage, the paramparā of Tiruvānaikkāval, where Karuvūr Devar set down the proportions in the eleventh century."

Asked whether the modern engineer's foot-rule has displaced the daṇḍa, Ganapathi laughs without bitterness. "The Public Works Department came in 1932 to measure the Shore Temple. They said the southern wall was 'out of true' by an inch and a half. I told them: that inch and a half is the breath of the wall. Without it, the stone cannot sing."

"The śāstra is not a prison; it is the breath by which the granite stands erect."

His students — there are twelve in the present cohort, three of them from Ceylon — begin each morning with the recitation of the talamāna verses before they are permitted to lift a chisel. "First the mouth must know the measure," he insists. "Then the hand."

He charges nothing for instruction beyond what the College pays him — Rs. 84 per mensem, unchanged since 1939 — and accepts no private commissions. The vimana, he says, belongs to the village; the sthapati only lends his hand.

II.  On Ornament · The Makara-Toraṇa

R. Selvanathan

Carving Master, b. Swāmimalai 1907 · Specialist in soft-stone toraṇa work · Interviewed in his shed beside the Lighthouse Mandapam, 16 Aippasi 1947

Selvanathan is the youngest of the three but the most sought after. His makaras — the crocodile-elephant beings whose curling tails frame every doorway in southern stone — have travelled by bullock cart and steamer to seventeen new temples between Tirunelveli and Singapore in the past fifteen years.

The toraṇa, he begins by drawing in chalk on the floor, is not an arch. "An arch carries weight. A toraṇa carries meaning. The two makaras at its base are the gates of the world; from their open mouths issues the foliate scroll — the patra-latā — which the Tamil texts call the kodi-karai, the creeper-line."

He reaches for a half-finished panel in pinkish Krishnagiri sandstone. "See here," he says, running his thumb along a curl, "the eye of the makara must be set deeper than the snout by exactly three aṅgulas. Otherwise the whole face goes flat. The yāli on the side comes only at the third tier; below the third tier no yāli, only the lotus and the kīrtimukha."

His method is patient: a single toraṇa of moderate scale, three feet by two, takes him forty-two days. He charges Rs. 320 inclusive of the stone, less if the temple-trust is poor. "The Pālaiyam temple at Kāraikkudi paid me in rice and lamp-oil for nine months in 1942. I do not regret it. The lintel is there, and it will outlive the receipt."

"An arch carries weight. A toraṇa carries meaning."

He keeps no apprentices of his own — "the College gives me four boys for two months at a time, which is enough to spoil good stone" — but his elder son, aged nineteen, has begun to rough out the kumbha bases. "In ten years, perhaps, his makara will not embarrass me."

On the question of innovation, Selvanathan is firm. "Some sthapatis from Bangalore now carve the makara with the head of a horse. This is fashion, not śāstra. The makara is the makara. If you change its face, you change the gate of the world."

III.  On Consecration · The Kalaśa

K. Vaidyanathan

Acharya-Sthapati, b. Tiruvārur 1889 · Trained under his maternal uncle, the late S. Subbaraya Sthapati of Kumbakonam · Interviewed at the Vaikuntha Perumal compound, 17 Aippasi 1947

Vaidyanathan does not carve. He places. The kalaśa — the finial pot at the apex of the vimana — is the last act of a temple's making, and for fifty-three years the lifting and setting of the bronze pot has been his alone, performed in the small hours before dawn.

"The kalaśa is the temple's head," he says. "Without it the deity cannot be installed. I have set seventy-three pots in my life. I expect to set six more, perhaps seven, before the hand grows weak."

The rite, he explains, is not merely structural. The pot — typically of pañchaloha, the five-metal alloy, weighing between seven and eleven kilos — is filled before the lifting. "Nine grains: rice, sesame, mustard, black gram, green gram, coriander, cumin, fenugreek, and one pinch of gold-dust if the trust can afford it. Then the seven rivers, drawn the day before from the temple tank. Then the mantra, recited by the brahmin priest, but the placing — the placing is the sthapati's."

He describes the sequence with the calm of a man reciting a remembered hymn. "First the padma-pīṭha, the lotus seat carved into the topmost course. Then the kumbha — the swelling — set true to the plumb-line. Then the kanṭha, the neck. Then the bījā-pūra, the seed-fruit, which is the small inverted bell. Above all, the chūḍa, the crest. Each must align within one yava — one barley-grain — of the central axis. If it leans, the rains will know."

"The kalaśa is the temple's head. Without it the deity cannot be installed."

He charges no fee for the consecration itself — "one cannot sell the moment of crowning" — but accepts the customary gift of new cloth, a measure of rice, and the use of a bullock cart for the return journey. The College has tried for nine years to record his procedure on paper. He has refused. "When I die my nephew will know it. He has watched me set forty-one pots. That is the document."

Asked what he would say to the young men reading this journal in Madras, he is silent for some time. Then: "Tell them the granite is patient. The rod is patient. The kalaśa above all is patient. Only the sthapati must be in a hurry — to learn, before the older men are gone."

Glossary of Forty
A reader's hand-list of Dravidian temple terms used throughout this issue.
Vimāna
The sanctum tower; the pyramidal superstructure above the garbha-griha.
Garbha-griha
The "womb-chamber"; the inner sanctum housing the principal deity.
Ardha-maṇḍapa
The half-hall directly fronting the sanctum.
Mahā-maṇḍapa
The great pillared hall preceding the ardha-maṇḍapa.
Gopuram
The towered gateway in the prakara wall.
Prakāra
A circumambulatory enclosure-wall; temples may have up to seven.
Prastara
The cornice-entablature above the wall, below the śikhara.
Adhiṣṭhāna
The moulded plinth or basement of the vimana.
Pāda / Stambha
The pilaster or pillar of the wall-order.
Kuḍu
The horseshoe-arched dormer ornament on the prastara.
Hāra
The garland of miniature shrines around each tier.
Śikhara
The crowning member of the vimana, dome-like in the south.
Stūpī
The pinnacle finial, often capped by the kalaśa.
Kalaśa
The pot-finial; the temple's "head," ritually consecrated.
Padma
Lotus moulding or motif; the seat of divinity.
Kumbha
The pot or vase form, both moulding and finial element.
Yāli
The leonine-elephantine guardian beast at pillar bases.
Makara
Aquatic-crocodilian being framing toraṇas.
Toraṇa
The ornamental arch above doorways and niches.
Kīrtimukha
The "face of glory"; lion-mask above arches.
Talamāna
The canon of proportion measured in tālas (face-lengths).
Aṅgula
Finger-width; the basic unit of measure.
Yava
Barley-grain; sub-unit, eight to the aṅgula.
Daṇḍa
The sthapati's measuring rod.
Sthapati
The master temple-architect, carver, and ritual-builder.
Śilpin
The journeyman carver under a sthapati.
Ācārya
Senior teacher; honorific for the master.
Patra-latā
The foliate scroll issuing from a makara's mouth.
Kodi-karai
Tamil for the creeper-line of ornament.
Pañchaloha
Five-metal alloy used for finials and icons.
Nāgara
The northern style; for contrast only.
Drāviḍa
The southern, pyramidal-roofed style.
Vesara
The hybrid Deccan style.
Mānasāra
Foremost canonical śilpa-text.
Mayamata
The Tamil manual ascribed to the demon-architect Maya.
Garbha-pātra
The relic-pot interred at the foundation.
Bali-pīṭha
The offering-stone before the dhvaja-stambha.
Dhvaja-stambha
The flag-staff in the temple's axial line.
Mūla-mūrti
The fixed principal icon of the sanctum.
Utsava-mūrti
The processional bronze icon.

Field Report · Six Days at Tiruvarangam

The Outer Prakaras of Srirangam

A walk through the seven concentric enclosures of the Ranganatha temple during Panguni Uttiram — taking the measure of an unfinished Rajagopuram, the rearing yalis of the Sesharaya Mandapa, and a kolam refreshed before each dawn.

By Sarojini Krishnaswamy  ·  Visiting Correspondent, Tiruchirappalli District  ·  Phalguna–Chaitra, Saka 1869

From the bullock-cart road north of the Kollidam, the great horizontal city of Tiruvarangam announces itself first as a sequence of pale walls receding into haze, and only afterward as gopurams. One counts seven enclosures, twenty-one towers, and — the bus driver insists, gravely — a single uncompleted ambition four centuries old.

I arrived at Srirangam on the second day of Panguni, with letters of introduction from the editor of this Quarterly addressed to Sri R. Doraiswami Iyengar, the senior archaka of the Sesharaya shrine. The festival of Panguni Uttiram was four days off; the streets within the fourth prakara were already being washed at dusk by a relay of boys with copper pots, and the painted lines of the kolams at every threshold were thicker than usual, as though the women of the household meant the design to last till dawn even if no one were to walk the lane.

The temple is famously laid out in seven concentric rectangles, the outermost almost a mile across, the innermost no larger than a courtyard. To enter is to pass, in turn, through walls and bazaars, walls and houses, walls and pillared corridors, walls and a hall of jewellers, until at last the granite plinth of the Ranganatha vimana lifts itself up under its gilded roof. There are twenty-one gopurams in all, of which one — the Rajagopuram in the southern wall of the seventh prakara — has only this past decade come to its consecrated height of two hundred and thirty-six feet, the work of the present jeeyar after four hundred years of arrest.

I. The Unfinished Tower, or What Was Finished in 1987

The Rajagopuram, when I last passed beneath it in 1934, was a brick stump some seventy-two feet high, faced raggedly with stone at the base and capped with grass. Old guidebooks of the South Indian Railway used the photograph as a picturesque ruin. The brickwork dated, by the inscription on the southern jamb, from the reign of Achyuta Deva Raya of Vijayanagara (c. 1539); the stoppage was attributed locally to the sack of Hampi nineteen years later, after which neither funds nor sanction ever returned to the project.

It is now — though the painted plaster is still drying — the tallest temple gopuram in South India. The sthapatis of the Ahobila Mutt have raised eleven new tiers above the Vijayanagara stump, faithful in proportion to the original four, and have populated the niches with some four thousand stucco figures. I climbed, by permission, to the level of the sixth tier. From that height the seven prakaras are a single map: one sees plainly that the eastern half of the outermost wall is residential, with its own bazaars and streets named for chariots, while the western half is dense with mandapas. The roof of the Ranganatha shrine, gold-leafed and shaped like a recumbent vimana, sits low at the centre as if the towers had agreed in advance to defer to it.

The mason Ramanujan, who has worked here twenty-eight years, told me: "The old courses are different stone — harder, darker. We have matched the carving but not the colour. In a hundred years the rains will agree." He had no quarrel with the joining; he was only describing it. — Field interview, third day, beneath the southern stair

II. The Sesharaya Mandapa & Its Reared Yalis

Of the seventeenth-century mandapas Srirangam holds in such number, the Sesharaya — sometimes called the Hall of a Thousand Pillars, though the actual count is nine hundred and forty — is the most formidable. It stands in the fourth prakara, north-east of the Ranganatha shrine, and was endowed by the Nayaka governors of Madurai in the years between 1635 and 1652. Its capitals are not the calm padma-cushions of the Chola era but the rearing yali: a leonine-equine composite, jaws agape, forelegs lifted clear of the shaft, with armed warriors astride or beneath.

What is striking, on close reading, is that no two yalis are identical. Of the eighty-eight in the central nave, I counted eleven distinct postures. Some carry a deer in the jaws; some swallow an elephant whole, the trunk and tail emerging from opposite sides of the maw. The heaviest blocks — three of them weigh upwards of nine tons each — are dressed from a single piece of granite, the rider and beast and base extending continuously down to the kumbha-base as though grown rather than carved.

Sri Doraiswami Iyengar walked me along the western colonnade after the noon offering. The light at that hour is not generous; it falls in narrow stripes between the piers, and the yalis are mostly shadow. He pointed out a peculiarity I had not seen in earlier visits: along the lower register of three pillars in the southern bay, the chisel-marks of the Madurai workshop have been overwritten in the eighteenth century by a smaller, finer hand — apparently a restoration after the French occupation of 1751. The newer carving is half a finger shallower than the older, and the mason has signed his work with a small lotus glyph at the base.

III. The Kolam at the Vellai Gopuram

Each morning at four, before the temple opens for the viswarupa darshan, an old woman named Kamalathammal Pillai — she gives her age as seventy-three — sweeps the threshold of the Vellai Gopuram, the white tower in the third prakara, and lays out a fresh kolam in rice-flour. She has done this, by her own account, since 1899, the year of her marriage. Her mother-in-law did it before her, and her mother-in-law's mother before that.

The design is not arbitrary. It is a thirty-six-dot kolam in the pulli tradition, a single unbroken line that loops around all the dots and returns to its start without crossing itself. Drawn correctly, it takes about eleven minutes; drawn perfectly, perhaps fourteen. It is erased by feet within the hour. I asked her why she does not, as some now do, use a paste that would last two days.

"If it lasts two days, who will draw the second day's? My granddaughter must learn. The line is not for the gopuram. The gopuram is two hundred years older than my house. The line is for tomorrow morning." — Smt. K. Pillai, fifth day, before sunrise

IV. A Working Log of the Six Days

Day I — Phalguna 26
Arrival from Tiruchirappalli by 5 p.m. bus. Lodging at the Vaishnava Choultry, Mada Veedhi north. Walked the seventh prakara at dusk; counted four functioning bazaars and one defunct (the salt market, closed 1942).
Day II — Phalguna 27
Climb of the Rajagopuram by introduction. Photographed (with Mr. V. Subramaniam, six plates, half-plate camera) the southern face from the third tier. Notes on stucco palette: ochre, vermilion, peacock-blue, white. Gilding limited to the kalasa-finials.
Day III — Phalguna 28
Sesharaya Mandapa, full survey of the central nave. Identified eleven yali postures. Inscriptions partially legible on three plinths; copies taken in pencil rubbing. Lunch with Sri Doraiswami Iyengar.
Day IV — Phalguna 29
Vellai Gopuram and Akhilandeswari shrine. Interview with Smt. K. Pillai, 4:20 a.m. Pradakshina of the fourth prakara, noting twenty-three subsidiary shrines.
Day V — Phalguna 30
Panguni Uttiram festival. Garuda Sevai procession through the third prakara, attended by an estimated forty thousand devotees. Photography forbidden after 6 a.m. inside the second prakara.
Day VI — Chaitra 1
Closing inspections of the unfinished northern Karpaga Vinayaka gopuram (only the basement and one tier extant; brick, undated, abandoned). Departure by the 4:15 p.m. mail to Madras.

V. A Note on What Is Living

One leaves Srirangam suspecting that the seven prakaras are less an architectural diagram than a calendar. The outer rings are the noisy present, where buses arrive and rice is sold and the salt market is or is not still open. The inner rings are deeper time: the seventeenth-century yalis, the twelfth-century shrines, the granite plinth of the central vimana whose lowest courses precede record. Between these two — between the present and the deep — runs the daily, ephemeral work: the kolam at the threshold, the camphor lit before the deity, the line of rice-flour drawn each dawn, walked over by noon, drawn again. It is this middle register, and not the towers, that holds the place together.

To photograph the gopurams, one needs only a camera. To photograph the kolam one would need to come back at four every morning for fifty years, and one would still have less than half of the work.

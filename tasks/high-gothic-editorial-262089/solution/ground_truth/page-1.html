Letter from the Editor

On the Autumn Pilgrimage to Amiens

Aldous Renier, Editor — written from the desk by the choir, 29 September

We left Paris on the feast of Saint Matthew, six of us in two carriages, and reached the west front of Amiens just as the rain began. There is a moment, peculiar to the autumn light of Picardy, when the limestone goes from grey to the colour of weak tea, and the prophets in the lower register begin, for an instant, to look like men one might have known. The cathedral is impossibly tall — 42.30 metres at the vault, a fact one repeats out of nervousness — and the impulse, on entering, is to forgive every mason who ever erred at Beauvais. They were, after all, trying to do this.

This issue is given to that upward line. Émilie Vasseur examines the singular cobalt of Chartres, and what we know — and refuse to know — about its sand. Henri Cottard returns to Beauvais and the choir that fell in 1284, and proposes, with characteristic mildness, that the limit of Gothic height was not engineering but liturgy. And Sister Marguerite of the Abbaye de Solesmes reads the west front of Reims as one would read a page of carefully ruled prose. Three plates, drawn this summer in Senlis and Laon, are folded in at the centre.

We have, as ever, set the journal in Fraunces and Source Sans, printed in two colours on a Heidelberg cylinder press in the rue du Cloître, and bound by hand. The plates are tipped in. If you find a quire out of order, the fault is mine.

— A. R.

Essay I · Glass

The Cobalt of Chartres

by Émilie Vasseur

The blue of the twelfth-century lancets at Chartres has been chemically reproduced and is, by every test, identical to its modern counterparts — and yet it is not the same. On a sand from the Loire, on the cooling of crucibles, and on what a window is for.

Essay II · Stone

Beauvais and the Limit of Height

by Henri Cottard

The choir of Saint-Pierre rose to 48.20 metres, fell in 1284, was rebuilt, and in 1573 the spire fell again. The usual reading is structural; the better reading, perhaps, is liturgical. A reflection on what cathedrals refused to do, and why.

Essay III · Sculpture

Reading the West Front at Reims

by Sr. Marguerite of Solesmes

A façade is a sentence. The smiling angel of the central portal is its conjunction. On the grammar of jamb-figures, on the patience of the master of 1252, and on what it means to read a building from left to right at the speed of a procession.

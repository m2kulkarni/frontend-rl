Issue Forty‑Seven  ·  The Correspondence Pages

Letters & Correspondence

Seven readers respond to Nasrin Daryabandari's Summer essay on the cuerda seca tile, “The Drawn Line that Holds the Fire” — with replies from the Letters Editor.

Autumn 1444 ◆ Received at the Maydan Office, Isfahan ◆ Ed. K. Esfahani

Few essays this past year have produced a postbag like Daryabandari's. We print here a selection — abridged where necessary, and with the writers' permission — of seven letters that arrived between the 4th of Mihr and the 21st of Aban. Editorial replies, set in turquoise, are by the Letters Editor, K. Esfahani.

Letter I

On the Two Firings, and a Quibble with the Author

Dr. Farzaneh Khosravi  ·  University of Tehran

Madame Daryabandari's account of the cuerda seca technique is the clearest I have read in Persian or English in a decade — and yet it perpetuates the comfortable myth of the “single firing.” In the kilns at Naqsh‑e Jahan, as the unpublished accounts of Master Ali‑Reza Vaziri (d. 1031 H.) make plain, the manganese‑and‑grease line was first set at a low pass of perhaps 620°, and the polychrome glazes brought up only afterwards to the proper 940°. To collapse the two events is to misunderstand the entire economy of the workshop.

I would direct your readers, further, to the fragments lifted from the Madrasa‑ye Chahar Bagh during the 1387 conservation, where the manganese line is visibly proud of the field — proof, surely, of a separate, earlier pass.

With respectful disagreement, — F. Khosravi, Tehran
From the Editor

Dr. Khosravi's letter sent us back to the Vaziri papers, which we had not consulted as carefully as we should have. The author concedes the point in part: a preparatory pass at low temperature did occur, though she maintains the term “single firing” remains apt for the polychrome event. We will print her own response in the Winter issue.

— K. Esfahani, Letters Editor
Letter II

A Conservator's Note from the Lagoon

Andrea Pellegrini  ·  Venice

Dear editors — I read the Summer issue on the lagoon vaporetto between San Zaccaria and the Lido, and arrived at my studio convinced that I had been cleaning Safavid tiles wrongly for fifteen years. The author's insistence that the “black line” is in fact a fired wax‑and‑manganese channel, not a mere drawn boundary, has changed how I approach the eight panels we hold from the Bagh‑e Fin commission of 1003 H.

One small correction: the EDTA solution at 2.4% which she mentions is, in our experience, far too aggressive on the turquoise oxide. We use 0.8% with a thickening agent of agar‑agar, and have never lost the tone.

Cordialmente, — A. Pellegrini, Calle dei Frati 318
From the Editor

The agar‑agar method is, we are told, now standard at the Doge's workshops; the author acknowledges her figure was drawn from a 1979 Tehran manual and is in need of revision.

— K. Esfahani
Letter III

“The Drawn Line that Holds the Fire” — and the Carpet

Houshang Ardalan  ·  Kashan

Sir — your Summer cover is handsome, but the parallel the author draws between the cuerda seca line and the warp of the silk carpet is, I fear, too easy. The carpet's warp is a structural necessity; the manganese line is decorative consequence. To equate them is to flatter the tile‑maker at the weaver's expense, and we Kashani weavers have suffered such flattery quite enough this century.

Yours in good humor, — H. Ardalan, Kashan Bazaar
Letter IV

From a Reader in Yazd, Who Asks Not to Be Named

Anonymous  ·  Yazd

I write because the essay was placed on the dashboard of my taxi by a passenger who left it behind, and I read it slowly over four nights between fares. I am not a scholar. But I will tell you that my grandfather, who set tiles at the Amir Chakhmaq complex in 1342, used to say the black line was not drawn at all — it was “stopped,” the way one stops water at a sluice. The glaze, he said, ran toward the line and was held there, like a horse at a rein.

I do not know if this is true. I send it because it is what I remember.

— A reader, Yazd, 12 Aban
From the Editor

This is the sort of letter for which the Correspondence pages exist. The image of water held at a sluice is, in fact, exactly what the resist line does; the author has asked us to thank the writer, whose grandfather we now wish to know more of.

— K. Esfahani
Letter V

On the Pricing of the Summer Issue

Mehrdad Sotoudeh  ·  Shiraz

To the publisher: 240,000 rial for a single issue is a great deal of money in the provinces, particularly when the Tehran kiosks receive the same number for 215,000. I do not begrudge you the cost of the gold ink on the cover, but I would ask that the regional supplement be priced honestly. I have been a subscriber since Issue 04 and would like to remain one through Issue 100.

— M. Sotoudeh, Shiraz
From the Editor

Mr. Sotoudeh is correct, and the discrepancy was a freight error on our part. From the Winter issue forward, the cover price is fixed at 220,000 rial nationally. Renew at the office on Chahar Bagh and we will discount your next year by an issue, with apologies.

— K. Esfahani
Letter VI

A Dissent on Ottoman Comparisons

Prof. Mehmet Tanyeri  ·  Mimar Sinan University, Istanbul

I write as an admirer of the journal, but to register a polite dissent. The author's footnote 14, which assigns the “invention” of the cuerda seca line to the Isfahani workshops of the late 16th century, overlooks Iznik experiments at least two decades earlier. We have a tile dated 982 H. in the Çinili Köşk that uses an identical manganese‑and‑grease channel. Priority is a small thing; precision is not.

With every collegial respect, — M. Tanyeri, Istanbul
From the Editor

The Iznik priority claim is one we have heard before and shall hear again. The author's position, as she put it to me by telephone, is that the techniques developed in parallel and that the question of priority is, at this distance, both unanswerable and uninteresting. We print Prof. Tanyeri's letter so that our readers may judge for themselves.

— K. Esfahani
Letter VII   —   the last

On Reading Aloud

Soraya Mostofi (age 14)  ·  Tabriz

I read the Summer issue to my grandmother, who is blind. She is from Isfahan and worked as a girl in a tile workshop near the Khaju Bridge. When I came to the part about the line that holds the fire, she put her hand on mine and said, “Yes — that is exactly what the master used to say to us: hold the fire, hold the fire.” She has not spoken of the workshop in many years. I wanted you to know that your magazine made her speak of it.

Thank you, — S. Mostofi, Tabriz
From the Editor

Miss Mostofi — there is no reply we can write that would improve your letter. We are sending you, and your grandmother, the next four issues without charge, and we should be very glad to have her workshop's name, when she is ready to share it.

— K. Esfahani, with the staff of Maydan

Essay · No. IV · Notes from the Workshop

Reza Abbasi at the Royal Library

On the loose-line single figures that made a court painter into the most copied draughtsman of seventeenth-century Iran — and on what the reed pen knows that the brush forgets.

By Leila Vahdat  ·  Photographs from the Topkapi & Gulistan Albums

The reed pen, when it has been cut correctly, holds about three lines of weight in a single stroke. Reza Abbasi, working in the kitabkhana of Shah Abbas I in the early years of the seventeenth century, asked it to hold five — a thinning into hair at the throat, a swelling into ribbon at the sash, a doubling back at the hem, and then, somewhere near the slipper, a release so quick it leaves only a comma on the paper. The figures who carry these lines do not stand on ground. They lean against margins. They read, drink, embrace one another, doze; occasionally they look up, mildly, as if the viewer had just walked into a room where they had been waiting.

To approach Reza Abbasi (active c. 1565–1635) through the album leaves now divided between the Topkapi Sarayi in Istanbul and the Gulistan Palace in Tehran is to watch a tradition learn a new economy. The Safavid manuscript, by 1600, was a saturated form: gold-flecked margins, lapis grounds, miniaturized armies in the hundreds. What Reza did, with the support of a court that had recently moved its capital to Isfahan and was rebuilding itself around the Maydan-i Naqsh-i Jahan, was to subtract.

I. The Single Figure

Before Reza, the album page in Iran was understood largely as a vehicle for poetry, calligraphy, and narrative compression. The single-figure study existed — Bihzad had drawn dervishes, and the Tabriz painters knew how to set a youth against a flowering branch — but it was a study, a workshop exercise, not the finished thing. Reza took the workshop exercise and refused to dress it. The figure became the page.

The result is a body of work that reads, even now, as deeply private. A young scribe in the Gulistan album leans his weight onto one elbow, holding a folded letter; a dervish on a Topkapi leaf bends forward to listen to nothing. The line that describes them is unhurried. It is a line that has decided what it knows. The painter Mu'in Musavvir, Reza's most diligent student, would later annotate his own copies in the margin: "after the master, in the manner of his later years."

The figure became the page, and the page learned to breathe at the edges where, once, gold had been required to fill.

II. Two Dismissals, One Return

Reza's biography in the court chronicles is unusually well-attested for an Iranian painter of his generation. The Tarikh-i 'Alamara-yi 'Abbasi of Iskandar Beg Munshi places his entry to the royal kitabkhana at 1587, when he would have been a young man of perhaps twenty-two; by 1603, he had been dismissed for keeping company that the chronicler describes, with diplomatic restraint, as “wrestlers and other low people.” He returned to court favour in 1610, and remained there, with a stipend and his own atelier, until his death in 1635.

The dismissal years are, on the present evidence, the most interesting. They produced the loose-line drawings the Topkapi album preserves: figures sketched in coffeehouses, in baths, leaning out of unimportant doorways. The paper is thinner, often re-margined later. There is sometimes no patron's signature at all, only the painter's own — raqamahu Reza, “Reza wrote it.”

What returned in 1610 was not the same painter. The Gulistan album leaves from the 1620s — Youth Reading among them — show a Reza who has decided, finally, what to keep from the workshop years and what to keep from the court. He keeps the silence of the single figure. He keeps the gold. He lets the line continue to do most of the work.

III. What the Pen Knows

It is tempting, looking at the Topkapi sheets, to read Reza as a kind of proto-modernist — the Safavid Schiele, the man who saw the body before the costume. The temptation should be resisted. Reza was a court painter under a patron who had made image-making a department of state. The looseness is not rebellion; it is fluency. A line that has been drawn ten thousand times can afford to look as if it had been drawn once.

What the reed pen knows, in his hand, is when to stop. The brush, by contrast, is an instrument of accumulation: it likes more colour, more gold, more ground. Reza's late single figures use the brush only where the pen has already finished — a wash of turquoise at the lining of a cloak, a single stroke of saffron for the inside of a sleeve. The page beneath remains cream, and breathes.

This is the lesson Isfahan's tilework masters were learning at exactly the same hour, three streets away, on the dome of the Sheikh Lotfollah: that ornament concentrates at the edge of the field, and that the field, left alone, will hold.

A Brief Timeline

  1. 1587 Entry to the Royal LibraryAdmitted to the kitabkhana of Shah Abbas I in Qazvin, before the capital's removal to Isfahan. Stipend recorded by Iskandar Beg Munshi.
  2. 1603 DismissalRemoved from court favour after associating, per the Tarikh-i 'Alamara, with wrestlers and street performers. Continues to draw; signs work raqamahu Reza.
  3. 1610 ReturnReinstated as principal painter to the court. Begins the Gulistan album leaves; trains Mu'in Musavvir and Afzal al-Husaini.
  4. 1635 Death in IsfahanBuried, by some accounts, near the Chahar Bagh. The atelier continues under his students for two further generations.

— Compiled from Iskandar Beg Munshi —

Marginalia

Of the 124 securely-attributed Reza Abbasi sheets, 47 are held at the Topkapi (H. 2155, H. 2161), 38 at the Gulistan, and the remainder dispersed between Tehran, Boston, and the British Museum.

The painter signed in three hands across his career; the latest, used after 1620, adds the honorific Aqa.

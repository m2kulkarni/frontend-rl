Feature Essay  ·  Architecture & Cosmology

The Painted Heaven
Inside the Sheikh Lotfollah Dome

A reading of the radiating arabesque medallion, completed in 1619 on the eastern flank of the Naqsh-e Jahan, as a diagram of cosmological order — and an inquiry into the four centuries of looking that have followed.

By Hossein Mirzai ◆ Autumn Issue, 1444 A.H. ◆ Approx. 2,400 words

Stand on the threshold of the Sheikh Lotfollah at the second hour after sunrise on a clear October morning, and the dome above you will perform a kind of sleight of hand. A blade of light, admitted through one of the sixteen latticed clerestory windows that ring the drum, falls across the upper surface of the cobalt and ochre arabesque, igniting a single radiating medallion at the apex. The dome is not painted, of course; it is faced in cuerda-seca tile, fired in seven colours, and laid by the master Muhammad Reza Isfahani between 1011 and 1028 A.H. Yet the moving disc of light upon those tiles is so precise, so apparently choreographed, that visitors for four centuries have called it the Painted Heaven, and have insisted, against the evidence of carpentry and pigment, that the ceiling itself is alive.

To stand inside the prayer hall — which is not, in any conventional sense, a congregational mosque at all, but a private oratory built for the women of Shah Abbas's harem and the saintly father-in-law from whom it takes its name — is to enter a room that is simultaneously a building and a diagram. The plan is a square of roughly nineteen metres on a side; the dome above is set on a drum which itself sits on eight concave squinches; the total interior height, measured from the carpet to the apex of the inner shell, is approximately thirty-two metres. These numbers matter, because the building's effect depends upon them. The arabesque medallion at the apex is exactly four metres in diameter; from it twenty-six radiating rays of stylised lotus and palmette descend, broadening as they fall, until they meet the springing of the dome and dissolve into the cobalt field of the spandrels.

What I want to argue in the pages that follow is that this radiating medallion, far from being a decorative flourish, is a working diagram of the Safavid cosmology — that it encodes, with the discipline of a treatise, the sequence by which the One descends into the Many, the simple into the manifold, the unmoving into the moved. To read it as ornament is to read the Mathnawi of Rumi as a rhyming dictionary: technically possible, fundamentally absurd.

I. The Geometry of Descent

◆ ◆ ◆

Begin, then, at the centre. The medallion's innermost figure is a sixteen-pointed star, drawn in gold on a ground of deep lapis. The number is not arbitrary: sixteen is the count of the clerestory windows in the drum below; sixteen is the count, too, of the sub-medallions that ring the apex at the second order. The star is contained within a circle of pearled gold — a single line of microscopic dots, no bigger than poppy-seeds, which a workman of the royal kiln must have laid one at a time with a horsehair brush.

Around this central disc the arabesque begins to multiply. The first ring is a band of paired shamseh — sun-rosettes — alternating with the long-stemmed lotus that the Persians inherited from the Mongol painters of Tabriz. The second ring is a meander of split-palmette and saz-leaf, drawn in turquoise on white. The third — and here the work begins to turn vertiginous — is a continuous arabesque of vegetal scrolls in which no two are identical, although the entire ring is bilaterally symmetrical to a tolerance of a few millimetres. The fourth ring contains the cartouches of the calligrapher Ali Reza Abbasi: a band of thuluth in white on cobalt, repeating the ninety-nine names.

By the time the eye reaches the seventh ring it has lost count, and this is precisely the intended effect. The medallion is constructed so that its complexity outpaces attention. One is meant to fail to enumerate it, and in failing to be returned, by an emotional rather than an intellectual route, to the simplicity of the central star.

Arthur Upham Pope, writing in the third volume of his Survey of Persian Art in 1938, identified this strategy as "controlled excess" and traced its philosophical lineage to the school of Mulla Sadra at Shiraz. Sadra, Pope argued, taught that the divine was apprehended not through a clear vision of the One but through the failure of the senses before the Many — that the gradient of being was itself a kind of veil, and that to walk the gradient was to draw nearer to the source. Whether or not Muhammad Reza had read Sadra is unclear; the master tile-layer left no theoretical writings, and the few archival fragments we possess from the royal workshop of Isfahan suggest a craftsman more concerned with kiln temperatures than with metaphysics. Yet the dome speaks, and what it says is Sadrian.

II. The Painted Hours

◆ ◆ ◆

There is a second reading to be made, and it concerns time. The sixteen sub-medallions of the second order are not, as a casual viewer might assume, identical; each is differentiated by a slight variation in the colour of its inner field. Beginning at the qibla axis and proceeding clockwise, the first medallion is faced in deep cobalt; the next, slightly lighter; the next, lighter still, until at the eighth — which lies directly opposite the first, on the western axis — the field has shaded into pale ivory. From the ninth, the sequence reverses: the colours deepen, returning to cobalt at the sixteenth.

This is a clock. Or rather, it is the diagram of a clock, since the medallions do not move and there is no hand to read them. The sequence describes the progression of the sun across the day, from the cobalt of pre-dawn through the ivory of noon and back to the cobalt of evening; and because the windows of the drum are arranged to admit light onto each medallion in turn — slowly, over the course of the daylight hours — the dome appears to tell the time, lighting first one medallion, then the next, in a slow procession that takes approximately eleven hours and forty-eight minutes to complete on the equinox.

I have spent a number of mornings in the empty oratory with a notebook and a pocket watch, attempting to verify this sequence. The match is not perfect — the windows are obstructed at certain seasons by the screen of the bazaar opposite, and the medallions on the southwestern arc are catching less light in 2024 than they did in 1939, owing to the construction of a four-storey bank — but the basic intention is unmistakable. Muhammad Reza laid a sundial onto the inner surface of his dome, and the dial is read by the building reading itself.

III. The Restoration as Reading

◆ ◆ ◆

The dome we see today is not, in any strict sense, the dome of 1619. Two major restoration campaigns — in 1932, under the supervision of André Godard at the newly founded Iranian Department of Antiquities, and again in 1978, on the eve of the Revolution, under the master tile-maker Ostad Hossein Lorzadeh — have lifted, replaced, and re-laid perhaps eighteen percent of the original tilework. The boundaries of these interventions can be read by anyone who knows where to look: the 1932 campaign favoured a turquoise of slightly higher chroma than the original, while the 1978 campaign, working under conditions of considerable difficulty, sometimes substituted a moulded relief for a hand-cut cuerda-seca piece.

What strikes me, returning to the dome each year, is not these failures but the strange continuity that the building has maintained through them. A craft tradition is not a fossil. The restorations of 1932 and 1978 are themselves now part of the dome's history; they belong to the medallion as surely as do the original tiles of Muhammad Reza. To call for a "true" Sheikh Lotfollah, scrubbed of its successive restorations, is to mistake the nature of an object that has only ever existed in continuous, attentive, slightly imperfect renewal. The Painted Heaven is painted, and re-painted, and will be painted again.

IV. A Diagram of the World

◆ ◆ ◆

Let me return, in closing, to the central star. It is, as I have said, sixteen-pointed, gold on lapis, surrounded by a ring of pearls. It is also, if one looks long enough, a diagram of every other ring in the medallion, condensed. Its sixteen points correspond to the sixteen sub-medallions of the second order; the lozenges between its points correspond to the sixteen panels of thuluth calligraphy in the fourth ring; the pearled border corresponds to the band of shamseh in the first. The medallion is, in effect, a fractal: each scale of organisation contains, in compressed form, the structure of every other.

This is what I mean when I say that the dome is a diagram of cosmological order. The Safavid theologians of the Isfahan school — Mir Damad and his student Mulla Sadra most prominently — held that the cosmos was not a thing made once and finished, but a continuous procession of self-similar emanations from a single source, each level of being containing, in compressed form, the structure of the level above. The Painted Heaven is that doctrine made visible. It is a sermon delivered in tile, by a man who probably could not have written the sermon in prose, but who could lay it, ring by ring, into a dome that has been delivering it without interruption for four hundred and five years.

One leaves the building, on a clear October morning, with the strong impression that the dome has been watching one back. This is not, I think, a sentimental illusion. It is the appropriate response to a room that was designed to look at its visitor, and that has had four centuries to perfect the gaze.

Companion Note

The Two Restorations

1932 — The Godard Campaign. Begun in the spring of 1310 (Solar Hijri), under the directorship of the French archaeologist André Godard at the request of Reza Shah's antiquities ministry, the first systematic restoration of the dome lifted approximately 1,840 tiles from the upper third of the inner shell. Godard's team, drawing on photographs commissioned a year earlier by the Pope expedition, replaced losses with hand-cut cuerda-seca tiles fired at the kiln of Ustad Khalil at Naain. The campaign closed in the autumn of 1313 (1934 C.E.), and was documented in a slim folio printed in 200 copies, of which fewer than thirty survive.

1978 — The Lorzadeh Campaign. The second great intervention was undertaken between Tir and Azar of 1357 (June–November 1978), in the closing months of the Pahlavi period, under the master tile-layer Ostad Hossein Lorzadeh — already in his eighty-third year, and the last surviving apprentice of the Qajar workshops of Tehran. Working at speed, and against a deteriorating political climate, Lorzadeh re-laid a section of the southwestern spandrel and stabilised the medallion's gold leaf with a fixative of his own devising. The campaign closed abruptly in late Azar; the scaffolding was struck six weeks before the Revolution.

A third campaign, long anticipated, has been delayed since 2017. The dome, as I write, is sound — but the bank opposite continues to rise.

— H. M., Isfahan, Mehr 1402

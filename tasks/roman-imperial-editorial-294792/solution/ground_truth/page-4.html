Letters & Marginalia

Five readers respond to “The Honest Slanderer: Suetonius and the Truth of the Caesars” from our previous fascicule. Editorial replies set in italics, signed L.A.P.

From Issue VIII ❦ November MMXXIII ❦ Pages 42–61

From the Editor's Desk

No essay we have published has produced a heavier post-bag than Dr. M. Tellier's broadside in our autumn number, “The Honest Slanderer” (Issue VIII, pp. 12–34). The argument — that Suetonius is to be trusted precisely because he traffics in gossip, and that the De Vita Caesarum is the most candid administrative document we possess from the Flavian chancery — has provoked five letters worth printing, and many we have, with reluctance, not.

What follows is a corrected and lightly edited selection. We are, as always, in our readers' debt.

— L.A.P.

Correspondence

Letter the First

On the Question of Sources

Princeton, New Jersey

To the Editor — Dr. Tellier writes with the enthusiasm of a convert, and converts are seldom careful. To call Suetonius an "honest slanderer" is to grant him an honesty he does not seek and a slander too dignified for the rumour-collecting we actually find in his pages. The biographer of the Caesars was, by his own admission in the dedication to Septicius Clarus, a magistrate's secretary; he had access, briefly, to the imperial archives under Hadrian. The interesting question is not whether he is candid but whether he is comprehensive — and on that count the verdict has been settled since Wallace-Hadrill's monograph of 1983.

What troubles me in your contributor's argument is the implicit equation of access with truth. Suetonius reproduces the acta of the Senate when it suits the rhythm of his portrait and ignores them when it does not. The famous chapter on Tiberius at Capri (Tib. 43–45) is not a document; it is a literary set-piece that owes more to Tacitus's Annals 6 than to any chancery file. To treat it as administrative testimony is a category error.

That said, Dr. Tellier is right to insist that the gossip is not noise. Gossip is the sound a court makes when it cannot speak directly. I would ask only that we listen to it as gossip, and not pretend it is something graver.

Prof. Adriana Cole Department of Classics, Princeton University
The Editor Replies

Professor Cole's distinction between access and truth is the one we should have drawn ourselves. Dr. Tellier, who has read this letter in proof, concedes the point on Tib. 43–45 and asks us to record his agreement; he maintains, however, that the chancery hand is visible elsewhere — most clearly in the Vespasian, where the financial detail is too granular for invention. We shall return to the question in a future number.

— L.A.P.
Letter the Second

A Provincial Defence

Aix-en-Provence

Madame, Sir — I read Dr. Tellier's piece on the train from Marseille to Arles, and I confess I closed the journal feeling defended. Those of us who labour in the provinces — the literal Roman ones, I mean: Narbonensis, Lusitania, Mauretania Caesariensis — are accustomed to being told that Suetonius is unserious. But the lives of Galba and Otho contain almost the only continuous narrative we have of the events of A.D. 68–69 outside Tacitus, and the Galba in particular preserves a Spanish provincial perspective that the senatorial sources flatten.

Where I would gently differ with your contributor is in his treatment of the omens. Dr. Tellier dismisses the prodigies as "courtly weather-reporting." This is too tidy. In the epigraphic record from Tarraco I have catalogued seventeen inscriptions between 64 and 70 referring to portents — bulls speaking, statues sweating, the sea retreating at Carteia. Suetonius did not invent this material; he transcribed a register that was, for the people who lived under it, the news.

To strip the omens from the Lives is to read a newspaper with the front page torn off.

Dr. Yusuf el-Amrani Centre Camille Jullian, Aix-Marseille Université
The Editor Replies

Dr. el-Amrani's seventeen inscriptions are an argument we should very much like to see at length, and we have written to him by the same post to invite an essay on the prodigy-record from Tarraco for our summer number. The point about the front page is well taken: Suetonius's omens are not décor but data, and the modern reader's discomfort with them is the reader's problem, not the historian's.

— L.A.P.
Letter the Third

From a General Reader

Edinburgh

Dear Editor — I am not a classicist. I am a retired civil servant who reads your journal with the slow pleasure of someone who has time. I read Dr. Tellier's essay twice and then went and bought the Robert Graves translation of Suetonius from a shop on West Port for £8.50, and I have been reading Tiberius in bed for a fortnight.

What strikes me, as a reader and not a scholar, is how modern Suetonius feels — not because the gossip is modern, but because the structure is. He gives you the official career, then the private life, then the death, then the omens. It is the Wikipedia infobox. It is the obituary in The Times. He invented a form we still use.

I write only to say: please do not let your specialists talk us out of him. Some of us read him for pleasure, and the pleasure is also a kind of evidence.

Mr. Hamish Lindsay subscriber since MMXVI
The Editor Replies

Mr. Lindsay's observation about the infobox is, in fact, exactly the argument made by John Henderson in a very technical paper of 2014, which we shall not inflict upon him. We are delighted he found his Graves for £8.50; the same edition is presently £24 in our nearest London shop. The pleasure he describes is the reason any of this matters.

— L.A.P.
Letter the Fourth

A Translator's Quarrel

Toronto, Ontario

Sir — I have been translating the Caligula for a small press in Halifax (publication, deo volente, in spring 2025), and I write to register a small but not trivial complaint. Dr. Tellier quotes Cal. 22 — "oblitus hominem" — as "forgetting himself a man." This is the Loeb's reading, and it is wrong. The phrase belongs to a longer participial construction, and the verb is better rendered "having put aside his humanity," which carries the legal sense of oblivisci in second-century Latin: not absent-mindedness but deliberate renunciation.

The distinction matters because Dr. Tellier's whole reading of Caligula's theatrum rests on the idea that the emperor was confused about his own nature. Suetonius's Latin says the opposite. The emperor knew exactly what he was doing. That is what makes the chapter terrifying.

I am perfectly willing to be wrong, and would welcome correction from a reader with better Latin than mine.

Ms. Eleni Vasilakis independent translator
The Editor Replies

We have submitted Ms. Vasilakis's reading to Dr. Tellier and to two further Latinists. The vote is two-to-one in her favour, with the dissenter (whose name we withhold) noting that the Loeb's solution has stood since 1914 and that there is something to be said for tradition. We agree with Ms. Vasilakis. Caligula knew. We look forward to the Halifax edition.

— L.A.P.
Letter the Fifth

In Defence of Tacitus

Cambridge, England

To the Editor — There is a phrase Dr. Tellier uses, near the end of his essay, that I have not been able to dislodge from my mind: "Tacitus is the great moralist; Suetonius is the great accountant." It is a fine sentence and entirely wrong.

Tacitus is not a moralist in the sense your contributor implies — that is, a thunderer in the Carlyle mode. Read the openings of Annals 1 and Histories 1 side by side: the moralism is structural, not rhetorical. Tacitus tells you what kind of book this is going to be by the syntax of the first sentence, and then he keeps that promise. Suetonius, by contrast, tells you nothing in advance and reserves the right to contradict himself. This is not the difference between a moralist and an accountant. It is the difference between a historian who has a thesis and a biographer who has a filing system.

Both are valuable. But let us not pretend the filing system is more honest than the thesis simply because it appears more neutral. Neutrality, in historiography, is a posture, not a virtue.

Mr. Daniel Okafor postgraduate, Faculty of Classics, Cambridge
The Editor Replies

Mr. Okafor's distinction between rhetorical and structural moralism is exactly the one Dr. Tellier should have made and did not. We confess ourselves persuaded. The accountant metaphor was, in our view too, a little too pleased with itself. We have asked Mr. Okafor to write us a thousand words on the first sentence of the Annals, and we hope to print it in the next number.

— L.A.P.

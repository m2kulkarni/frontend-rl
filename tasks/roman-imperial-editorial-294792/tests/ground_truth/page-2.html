Concrete & Empire Opus Caementicium as Political Argument

On the political grammar of Roman pozzolana — how a grey paste of volcanic ash, lime, and seawater carried the weight of an empire and rewrote the architectural sentence of the Mediterranean.

By Marcus Reiner
Senior Editor at Large · Filed from Rome, the XIV. Kalends of November
Editor's Note

Mr. Reiner's essay is the second in our continuing series, The Long Republic, which examines the material vocabularies of imperial argument.

§ I. The Markets at Mid-Morning

Stand on the Via Quattro Novembre at half past ten on a clear October morning, with the wind off the Quirinal moving the umbrella pines, and the Markets of Trajan present themselves not as ruin but as argument. Six tiers of brick-faced concrete climb the hillside above the forum, vaulted, recessed, hollow, populated still — after nineteen centuries — by the geometry of commerce. The architect, the Damascene Apollodorus, did not build a monument. He built a sentence, and its grammar was concrete.

I have come here often, and I have come here this morning, because I want to insist on something that the Italian guidebooks treat as a footnote: that the material we glibly call Roman concrete — properly opus caementicium — is not a technology in the modern sense, not a product, not even, exactly, a recipe. It is a political argument, advanced over four centuries, in grey paste and aggregate, by a state that had decided early on that the built environment was a legitimate instrument of governance. The Markets are the argument's clearest paragraph.

Consider what is being said here. A hillside on the lip of the Imperial Forum, awkward, irregular, geologically unloved, has been carved into 150 tabernae — shops, offices, administrative cells — arranged across an artificial topography that no temple-and-column architecture could have produced. The vaults are shallow, the corridors curve to follow the slope, the great hall on the upper level (the so-called Aula Traiana) spans 8 metres clear and roofs itself with cross-vaults that would not be matched in stone for a thousand years. None of this is decorative. All of it is structural prose.

Concrete did for Rome what the alphabet had done for Phoenicia: it lowered, by an order of magnitude, the cost of being articulate. — M. R.

To grasp why this matters one must first abandon the modern association of concrete with the cheap, the hasty, the brutal. The Romans did not think of caementicium in those terms. Vitruvius, writing in the 20s b.c. in the second book of De Architectura, treats the material with the reverence a vintner gives to a soil. He describes its components — lime burnt from the limestone of the Sabine hills, sand of three named qualities, and above all the pulvis puteolanus, the powder of Puteoli — with the precision of a man who knows that a single substitution will collapse the wall.

§ II. Pozzolana, or, the Volcano as Civil Servant

Some 220 kilometres south of Rome, on the western flank of the Bay of Naples, the earth has spent the past forty thousand years vomiting glass. The Phlegraean Fields are a volcanic complex of low intensity and high persistence: not the catastrophic eruptions of Vesuvius but a slow, multimillennial spitting of fine, silicic ash. That ash, oxidised and weathered, lying in beds tens of metres deep around Pozzuoli — the Roman Puteoli — is pozzolana. The Romans did not invent it. The Romans noticed it.

What they noticed, and what no other Mediterranean people had thought to systematise, is that pozzolana mixed with slaked lime undergoes a pozzolanic reaction: the amorphous silica and alumina in the ash bind chemically with the calcium hydroxide of the lime to form calcium-aluminium-silicate hydrates, a cementitious gel that hardens not by drying but by chemistry. The reaction proceeds underwater. It accelerates in the presence of seawater. It continues, slowly, for centuries. The harbour mole at Caesarea Maritima, poured by Herod's engineers in the 20s b.c. using imported Italian pozzolana, is — as a consortium led by Marie Jackson at Berkeley demonstrated in 2017 — still gaining strength two thousand years later.

This is the part of the story that the engineers like to tell, and it is the right story, but it is not yet the political story. The political story is that pozzolana, unlike marble, unlike granite, unlike the prestigious imported stones with which the Republic had decorated itself, was not a luxury good. It was a bulk commodity, and the Romans organised its trade as a state concern.

The numbers, where we have them, are striking. By the reign of Trajan a single navis lapidaria out of Puteoli could carry 350 tonnes of pozzolana to Ostia in a four-day passage. The Lex Puteolana of 105 b.c. — preserved on a bronze tablet now in the Naples museum — fixes the price of a modius of pulvis at 4 asses, which after correction for the second-century inflation comes to roughly the cost of a day's bread for a labourer. A cubic metre of finished concrete, ready to pour, cost the Roman state about 80 sestertii in materials and roughly the same again in labour. For comparison: a single column drum of Carystian marble for the Pantheon cost the imperial fisc 3,200 sestertii at the quarry, before transport.

The Republic spoke in marble. The Empire learned to speak in paste — and discovered that it could write longer sentences.

Cheap material, abundant material, transportable material — and a material that hardened in water. The Roman state, which in the late Republic had begun to understand itself as a Mediterranean rather than an Italian project, suddenly possessed a substance that could build harbours, breakwaters, bridge piers, aqueduct channels, military camps, and basilicas, in any province where lime was available and pozzolana could be shipped. The fleet, the legions, and the cement barges constituted a single logistical organism.

Vitruvius understood this. In a passage that is normally read as antiquarian curiosity (II.6.1) he writes: Est etiam genus pulveris quod naturaliter efficit res admirandas. Nascitur in regionibus Baianis et in agris municipiorum quae sunt circa Vesuvium montem. "There is a kind of powder that naturally produces marvellous results. It is found in the regions of Baia and in the territories of the municipalities around Vesuvius." But he then adds, with a politician's instinct, that this powder, mixed with lime and rubble, hardens "not only in ordinary buildings, but also in piers built into the sea." Vitruvius is not describing a material. He is describing a strategic asset.

§ III. The Logistics of Caracalla

If the Markets of Trajan are the argument's clearest paragraph, the Baths of Caracalla, dedicated in a.d. 216, are its longest and most exhausting sentence. They are also the place where the political reading of opus caementicium becomes inescapable.

The bathing complex covers eleven hectares. The central block alone — the caldarium, tepidarium, frigidarium, and natatio — required, by the careful estimate of Janet DeLaine in her 1997 monograph for the British School at Rome, approximately 100,000 cubic metres of concrete. The vaults of the central hall span 24 metres clear. The ceiling of the frigidarium, before its sixth-century collapse, rose 33 metres above the marble pavement. To pour these vaults the imperial works department mobilised, between roughly a.d. 211 and 216, what DeLaine reconstructs as a workforce of around 9,000 men working in continuous shifts: lime-burners in the Sabine quarries, bargemen on the Tiber, pozzolana-carriers at Puteoli, scaffolders, brick-makers at the figlinae of the Salarian estates, mortar-mixers, vault-formers, and the small army of skilled structores who actually placed the caementa — the rubble lumps of tufa, brick, and travertine — into the wet mortar.

Read the supply chain backwards. The bricks bear stamps: CIL XV preserves more than two thousand of them from the Caracallan strata, naming the brickworks of the Domitii Afri at the third milestone of the Via Salaria, and the figlinae of Sulpicius Maximus on the Tiber's right bank. The lime came from the Sabine hills above Tivoli, where the limestone of the Aniene valley produces an unusually pure quicklime when burnt at 900°C — a temperature the Romans achieved with oak charcoal in beehive kilns, four of which have been excavated at the site of Bagni di Tivoli since 1981. The pozzolana, for the great vaults, was specifically the pulvis puteolanus of the Phlegraean Fields, shipped via the maritime route Misenum–Ostia and barged up the Tiber to the portus below the Aventine, then carted overland to the building site.

This is not the supply chain of a private patron, however wealthy. This is the supply chain of a state. The Severan dynasty maintained, through the office of the curator operum publicorum, a permanent cadre of imperial freedmen and military engineers whose job was the procurement of building materials at provincial scale. The same office that supplied Caracalla's vaults supplied, in those same years, the legionary fortresses on the Rhine and the harbour repairs at Leptis Magna. Opus caementicium had become an instrument of imperial coordination — a way of converting the tax revenues of Egypt and the Spanish silver mines into vaults, basilicas, and bath-houses that could be experienced bodily by the urban plebs.

The bath-house was the bread and circus made architecture: a daily, somatic encounter between the citizen and the fiscal apparatus that had bought him his hot water.

And the citizen, walking through the great vaulted halls under their now-vanished mosaic ceilings, was meant to feel exactly what the architect intended him to feel. Concrete vaults, unlike trabeated stone, can be enormous. They can be hollow. They can admit light from clerestories that no post-and-lintel system would tolerate. They can roof a space without a single internal column. The frigidarium of Caracalla was, in a.d. 220, the largest covered interior in the world, and it had been built — and the citizen knew it had been built — for him, free of charge, by the Emperor.

§ IV. The Argument from Vitruvius

It is worth returning, before we close, to the strange figure of Vitruvius. He is our only sustained ancient theorist of architecture, and he is — as every reader of him soon discovers — a peculiar guide. He is conservative where we expect him to be radical; he praises Greek precedent at the moment when his contemporaries were inventing a new vocabulary; he treats opus caementicium as a technique rather than as the revolution we now know it to be.

But read him politically and the picture clarifies. De Architectura was dedicated to Augustus around 25 b.c., in the years immediately following the settlement at Actium. Augustus had inherited a city, in his own famous boast, of brick, and intended to leave it of marble. Vitruvius's job, in the treatise, is to articulate a building programme that can match this ambition without — and this is the crucial point — abandoning the structural advantages that the Republic's engineers had developed over the previous century.

The compromise he proposes is the one we now recognise as classical Roman architecture: a marble or stuccoed surface applied over a concrete core. The Pantheon, finished a century and a half later, is the masterpiece of the formula. From outside it presents a Greek temple front; from inside it is a single concrete dome, 43.3 metres in diameter, lightened by graduated aggregates (travertine in the foundations, tufa lower down, scoria and pumice at the crown), poured around a wooden centring that must itself have been one of the great carpentry projects of the second century.

The Pantheon, like the Markets, like the Baths, is an argument. It says: the state can do this. The state, and only the state, can do this. No private patron in Rome, however rich, could have mobilised the pozzolana fleets, the lime kilns, the bronze-clad oculus, the Egyptian granite columns, the imperial brickworks, the shipwrights and the carpenters, in a single coordinated act. Concrete made the state visible. The Pantheon's coffered dome is, in this sense, a balance sheet, rendered in volume.

§ V. Coda — The Long Shadow

The technique faded. Pozzolana continued to be used through Late Antiquity, in the great fifth-century basilicas of Ravenna and Constantinople, but the supply chains decayed with the western empire's tax base. By the time of Theodoric the figlinae of the Salarian estates had largely closed; the Tiber barges no longer ran; the lime kilns of Tivoli were burning church marble for agricultural lime. The recipe was not lost, exactly — Justinian's engineers used it at Hagia Sophia in 537 — but the political organism that had made it cheap, abundant, and ubiquitous had ceased to exist.

It returned slowly. The eighteenth-century engineers of the Bourbon court at Naples, faced with the harbour problems at Mergellina, rediscovered pozzolana in the Phlegraean beds and began to publish on it. John Smeaton, building the third Eddystone lighthouse in 1756, used Italian pozzolana in his lime mortar after reading Vitruvius in a 1684 Latin edition. Joseph Aspdin's Portland cement of 1824 is, in a sense, the industrial revolution's attempt to manufacture pozzolana from materials that did not require a volcano next door.

But the political argument that the Romans made — that a state can express its legitimacy through the systematic, fiscal, infrastructural production of public space — has had a more chequered modern history. The Markets of Trajan are now a museum. The Baths of Caracalla are an opera house in the summer and a romantic ruin the rest of the year. The Pantheon, alone of the great concrete monuments, still functions as it was meant to: as a covered public space, free of charge, where any citizen of any standing may walk in off the street and stand under the sky.

That, in the end, is the argument that opus caementicium was always making. Concrete is the cheapest way the state has ever found to give its citizens a roof. The Romans understood this, and built accordingly, for four hundred years. Whether anyone since has fully understood it — whether we, in our age of cement and steel, understand it now — is the question this essay has not quite dared to answer, and to which I shall return, the editors permitting, in the spring.

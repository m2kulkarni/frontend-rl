The Oculus
and the Sky

On the geometry of the Pantheon, the engineering of weather, and the architectural piety of an empire that built circles to contain the gods.

By Helena Vossius
Senior Fellow, Schola Architecturae · Bologna

At noon on the twenty-first of April — the traditional dies natalis of Rome — a coin of light falls through the oculus of the Pantheon and crosses the threshold of the doorway with the precision of a sundial. Hadrian's masons knew this, and so did Apollodorus before them. The building is not, as the guidebooks say, a hemisphere balanced on a drum. It is a single sphere, half-buried in the earth, its lower meridian held by walls thick enough to be mistaken for masonry, but in truth performing the geometry of restraint.

To stand beneath the dome is to stand inside an idea — and the idea is older than its concrete. This issue assembles eight essays on what Rome built, what Rome wrote, and what Rome continues to ask of us.

— From the Editor's Desk —

Why Rome Still
Legislates the Imagination

We do not study Rome because it answers our questions. We study Rome because it teaches us how to ask them — slowly, in long sentences, with an instinct for ruin and a stubborn faith that what is built well outlasts the men who built it.

This issue, our forty-eighth, is offered in that spirit. The amphitheatres are silent now; the legions are dust. But the grammar of public life — the colonnade, the codex, the carved inscription that begins SENATUS POPULUSQUE — has not yet finished speaking.

Lucius Aurelian Pratt Editor-in-Chief · Acta Diurna · Kalends of October

Emperor of Rome

Ruling the Ancient Roman World

Mary Beard  ·  Profile Books, 2023  ·  512 pp.  ·  £30 / $35

Reviewed by Theodora Vancecontributing critic, Faculty of Classics, Magdalen College, Oxford

For thirty years Mary Beard has been the most public custodian of the Roman past in the English-speaking world, and for thirty years she has resisted the temptation, common to her trade, to write a book about emperors as if emperors were what mattered most. Emperor of Rome, her tenth full-length volume and the long-promised companion to SPQR, is therefore not the book one might expect: it is not a parade of Caesars from Augustus to Alexander Severus, marching obediently from page to page in their numismatic profiles. It is, instead, a book about the idea of the emperor — the role, the routine, the dinner menu, the bureaucratic shadow he cast over a world that very rarely saw him in person.

The conceit is announced on the first page and held with the discipline of a senatorial debate: Beard refuses chronology. Domitian and Trajan share a paragraph; Hadrian materialises beside Elagabalus; Caligula is summoned not for his vices but for the question of how a Roman emperor ate his lunch. This is, on the face of it, a perverse choice. It is also the book's chief glory. By dissolving the dynasty into themes — accession, palace, work, dinner, journey, succession — she allows the reader to see what the conventional biography conceals: that the imperial office, for all its theatrical individuation, was a remarkably stable bundle of obligations performed by interchangeable men, most of whom would have been astonished to be remembered at all.

"The emperor was less a person than a problem of logistics — a man who had to be everywhere, eat with everyone, and answer everybody's mail."

Beard is at her best on the paperwork. The chapter on epistulae — the imperial correspondence — is worth the price of admission. Drawing on Pliny's letters from Bithynia, on Hadrian's responses to provincials in Asia Minor, on the wooden tablets from Vindolanda and the Egyptian papyri from Oxyrhynchus, she reconstructs an emperor who was, more than anything, a man drowning in petitions. The figure who emerges is not Suetonius's monster nor Tacitus's hypocrite, but a slightly harassed administrator who has been asked, again, whether a small town in Pontus may build a new bath-house, and who replies, with a patience that reads almost like exhaustion, that they may.

This is the Beard manner: the deflation of grandeur not by sneering but by the patient accumulation of detail. When she turns to the emperor's table — and she devotes a long, marvellous chapter to it — the result is neither Petronian satire nor moralising lecture, but something closer to ethnography. Who carved the meat? Who tasted the wine first? Where did the slaves stand, and what did they hear? Beard's answer is that the imperial dinner was less a feast than a stage on which the entire political order of the empire was rehearsed, nightly, in front of an audience of perhaps thirty.

Not everything succeeds. The chapter on imperial women — Livia, Agrippina, Julia Domna, the formidable Severan matriarchs — feels compressed, almost dutiful, as though Beard is conscious of having said much of it before in Women & Power. The treatment of the Tetrarchy is brisk to the point of impatience, and readers hoping for a sustained reckoning with the Christian emperors will need to look elsewhere; Constantine appears mostly as an awkward coda. Beard is candid about these omissions — she announces in the introduction that her emperor is the emperor of the first three centuries, not the late-antique autocrat of the fourth — but the announcement does not quite excuse the imbalance.

What it does excuse, and gloriously so, is the book's refusal to be a textbook. Emperor of Rome is the work of a scholar who has stopped trying to convince anyone of anything and has instead settled into the harder business of looking carefully. The footnotes are a pleasure in themselves: a running, slightly mischievous commentary in which Beard quarrels with Ronald Syme, agrees reluctantly with Fergus Millar, and confesses, on page 387, that she has changed her mind about Commodus. One does not often read a classicist confessing in print to having changed her mind. It is the kind of intellectual courtesy that makes the book feel less like a monument than a long, generous conversation.

Profile Books have produced the volume with their customary care: the colour plates are well chosen (the marble portrait of Caracalla from Berlin, scowling out of the page like a bouncer, is worth lingering over), the maps are legible, the index is — rare praise — actually useful. At £30 for 512 pages of intelligent, unhurried, frequently funny prose, it is the best classical bargain of the season. Buy it; lend it cautiously; expect not to get it back.

If SPQR was Beard's argument with the long arc of Roman history, Emperor of Rome is her argument with the men who sat, briefly and uncomfortably, at its summit. The argument, in the end, is that they were not as interesting as we have been told, and that this is precisely what makes them worth our attention. It is a quietly subversive thesis, advanced with the authority of someone who has earned the right to be quiet. The shelf of books on the Caesars is very long. This one belongs near the top of it.

Shorter Notices

Three volumes recently arrived in our offices in Bloomsbury — read, weighed, and committed to the record.

Poetry · Translation

The Silvae

Publius Papinius Statius

translated by D. R. Shackleton Bailey, revised by Christopher A. Parrott

Loeb Classical Library 206 · Harvard University Press, 2024 · 528 pp. · $30

Statius's occasional poems — the consolations, the wedding hymn for Stella, the manic poem on the Via Domitiana — have always lived in the long shadow of the Thebaid. Parrott's revision of Shackleton Bailey is sober where it ought to be and sparkling where Statius is, restoring to the sequence its flickering, almost Baroque, instability of tone.

— Notice by L. WhitcombeSenior Lecturer, King's College London

Cloth · $30.00

Monograph · Frontier Studies

The Limes Germanicus

Stone, Earth, and the Edge of Empire, AD 83–260

Hartmut F. Reinhardt

Cambridge University Press, 2024 · 386 pp. · £75

Reinhardt synthesises three decades of German rescue archaeology — from the Saalburg to the Odenwald line — into a single, lucid argument: the limes was less a wall than a permeable apparatus of customs posts, watchtowers, and negotiated permissions. Indispensable; the maps alone repay the price.

— Notice by F. MarcellusRoman Frontier Project, Newcastle

Cloth · £75.00

Exhibition Catalogue

Faces of the Republic

Roman Portraiture from the Late Republic to Augustus

edited by Anne Marie Nielsen & Jan Stubbe Østergaard

Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek, Copenhagen, 2024 · 312 pp. · DKK 395

The Glyptotek's portraits — those magnificent, lined, sceptical Republican heads — deserve a catalogue that matches them. This one almost does. Nielsen's introductory essay on verism is exemplary; the photography, alas, occasionally flatters where it should interrogate. Still, an essential souvenir of an essential collection.

— Notice by H. AureliusCurator, Antikensammlung, Berlin

Paperback · DKK 395

I · Provenance & Apprenticeship

I was born in the agraharam of Tiruvaiyaru in the year 1956, on the bank of the Kaveri, within walking distance of the Pancha-nadeeswarar shrine whose pillared corridors taught me to count before I could read. My father, V. Ramaswamy Sthapati, was the seventh generation of carvers in our line; in 1968, when I was twelve, he placed a chisel in my hand and a slab of Krishnashila in front of me, and said only that the stone already knew its capital — my task was to remove what was not the capital.

For ten years I worked beside him, first dressing rough blocks, then drawing the tāla-māna grids onto stone in red ochre, and at last cutting the lower mouldings of the adhiṣṭhāna under his correction. By 1976 he had me carving full pilaster capitals for the renovation of the Sri Adi Kumbeswarar temple at Kumbakonam, and on his seventieth birthday in 1978 he gave me his measuring rod, his copy of the Mānasāra-Śilpa-Śāstra annotated in his own hand, and the small workshop on Nageswaran North Street, where I have remained.

II · The Studio & Its Compass

The Kumbakonam atelier accepts work in three departments: the cutting of new temple stone for kumbhābhiṣekam-bound shrines; the measured drawing of existing structures for restoration; and the consultation of agama-prescribed proportion for trustees, architects, and the Hindu Religious & Charitable Endowments Department of Tamil Nadu. I work to the orthodox Dravida canon as transmitted through the Mayamatam and the Kāśyapa-Śilpa, and refuse, politely, the polished granite of the export-temple style.

III · Method

Every commission begins on paper, never on stone. A measured plan is drawn at 1:48 in ink and lampblack on linen tracing cloth; the elevations are set out by the tāla system, the unit being the height of the principal deity divided into nine equal parts. Only when the trustee, the chief priest, and I have signed the sheet in vermilion does the stone enter the yard. The chisels themselves are forged for us each season at a smithy in Swamimalai, and are dedicated, with the first cut of the year, to Vishvakarma.

The atelier does not employ power tools beyond a single diamond saw used for the rough quartering of blocks. A capital, once roughed, is finished entirely with the hand chisel and the wooden mallet — twenty-two grades of cut, from the bhañjana (breaker) to the niśāna (polisher) — over a period of between three and eleven weeks, depending on order.

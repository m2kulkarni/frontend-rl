Curator's Essay

Of Quarries and Empire

on the white veins of Luna and the violet flame of Phrygia

Rome did not invent marble; she inherited it, conquered it, and at last commanded it. By the reign of Trajan the imperial city had become a ledger of stone — every column shaft a receipt drawn upon a province, every revetment a quiet boast of reach. To walk the Forum of Trajan was to walk through an atlas underfoot: the cold, sugar-grained whiteness of Luna at one's feet, the bruised purple of Phrygian pavonazzetto at one's shoulder, the honeyed giallo antico of Numidia warming the apse. The visitor who today crosses our threshold is asked to read these stones not as ornament but as policy.

The quarries at Luna — modern Carrara — were already old when Augustus turned to them in earnest. Their marble is fine, even-tempered, and capable of holding both the muscular detail of an imperial portrait and the long flat planes of an inscribed cornice. It became, in time, the default skin of public Rome. Yet what astonishes is not its abundance but its discipline: blocks were cut to standard sizes at the quarry face, numbered, dated by consular year, and shipped in vast rafts down the Tyrrhenian coast. Several such inscribed blocks, recovered from the Tiber silt in 1934, are presented in the second station of this exhibition.

Phrygian marble — pavonazzetto, the "little peacock" — tells a different story. Its violet veining, threading through cream like ink in milk, came at greater cost from the quarries above Dokimeion in central Anatolia. The Senate's commission books, partially preserved on the Marble Plan, record the journey of a single shaft of pavonazzetto from quarry to dedication: nineteen months, four ships, and the labour of some four hundred men. When you stand before the great half-column in our cryptoporticus, you stand at the end of that journey.

This exhibition gathers six stations along an unbroken line of argument: that Roman marble was at once a material, a logistics, and a language. The objects assembled here have been drawn from our own cellars, from the storerooms of the Soprintendenza, and on generous loan from Naples, Aquileia, and the Capitoline. Several have not been shown publicly since the Mostra Augustea of 1937. Walk slowly. Read the stone.

— Dr. Livia SoranzoCurator of Imperial Sculpture, Antiquarium Traianum

Station I

The Equestrian Trajan

a reconstruction in plaster, bronze, and conjecture

I

Equus Traiani

Reconstruction at 1:1 scale · plaster, gilt bronze leaf, painted timber armature · 2024

Nothing of the original equestrian monument that once dominated the centre of the Forum of Trajan survives — nothing, that is, but its absence, recorded in the worn rectangle of paving and in a passage of Ammianus Marcellinus describing the awe of the emperor Constantius II in the year 357. Our reconstruction, undertaken in collaboration with the École française de Rome and the Politecnico di Milano, takes that absence as its starting point.

Working from the impression in the stylobate, from the diminutive equestrian on the reverse of a sestertius of 112 CE, and from the sister monument to Marcus Aurelius preserved on the Capitoline, the team has produced an armature reconstruction in plaster and gilt leaf. The piece stands 5.4 metres at the crest of the rider's head. It is deliberately unfinished at the flanks — the visitor is invited to see the iron of conjecture beneath the gilding of certainty.

An adjacent vitrine presents the surviving fragment of the bronze rein-loop, recovered in 1872 during the demolition of medieval housing on the Via Alessandrina, together with three of the original anchoring clamps from the plinth.

Period · 112 CE (original) Material · plaster, gilt leaf Location · Sala I, ground floor

Station II

The Cast Room

reliefs of the column, unwound

II

Columna Traiani · the Helical Frieze

Plaster casts after the Column of Trajan · taken 1861 (Napoleon III) and 1939 (Mussolini campaign) · 38 panels presented

Forty metres of carved marble spiral up the shaft of the Column of Trajan in the open air of the Forum, where they have weathered for nineteen centuries and are today legible in only the broadest strokes. In our cast room, the spiral has been cut, unwound, and pinned flat against the long east wall, allowing the visitor to read the Dacian campaigns as one reads a scroll, from left to right, from the crossing of the Danube to the suicide of Decebalus.

The casts on display are drawn from two historic campaigns: the moulds commissioned by Napoleon III in 1861, now in the collection of the Cast Court at the Museo della Civiltà Romana, and the cleaner, more crisply pulled set of 1939. Where the two diverge — and they diverge often, in the depth of a fold, in the angle of a sword — the curatorial team has placed both side by side, with the difference annotated.

This is the only place in Rome where the entire frieze can be read at eye level. The room is intentionally low-lit; raking light from the south wall is calibrated to approximate the late-afternoon angle the original sculptors are thought to have anticipated.

Casts · 38 panels Wall length · 42 m Location · Sala II, cryptoporticus

Station III

The Hadrianic Medallions

eight tondi of the hunt and the sacrifice

III

Tondi Hadrianei

Eight circular reliefs · Luna marble · ca. 130–138 CE · on loan from the Soprintendenza Capitolina

Eight medallions, each just over two metres in diameter, are presented in a circuit around the upper register of the Sala dell'Imperatore. They were carved during the reign of Hadrian and reused, with the head of Antinous variously recut, on the Arch of Constantine in the early fourth century. Of the original twelve, eight survive in a state legible enough for display; the four lost tondi are indicated on the wall by their carved mouldings alone.

The subjects alternate strictly: a hunt, a sacrifice, a hunt, a sacrifice. A boar falls; an altar smokes; a lion is run down; libation is poured. Read together they construct a portrait of imperial virtue as a closed cycle — violence offered to the gods, the gods returning the favour to the hunt. The face of the emperor, where it survives, has been recut at least once, and in two of the medallions the Hadrianic features are still visible beneath a Constantinian overlay. A polarised-light photograph of each tondo, displayed beside it, makes the palimpsest plain.

Diameter · 2.05 m Material · Luna marble Location · Sala III, mezzanine

Station IV

Antinous in Porphyry

the imperial purple, in the body of a youth

IV

Antinous Porphyreticus

Bust of Antinous in Egyptian porphyry · ca. 134 CE · recovered Hadrian's Villa, Tivoli, 1769

Egyptian porphyry — the imperial purple of stone — was reserved, by edict of the second century, for the use of the emperor alone. Its quarry, at Mons Porphyrites in the eastern desert, was worked under direct military supervision; its export was a matter of imperial paperwork. To carve a private subject in porphyry was to lay claim to the colour of the throne itself. That Hadrian commissioned a porphyry portrait of his beloved Antinous, drowned in the Nile in October 130, is therefore not an act of taste but of theology.

The bust before you was excavated in 1769 from the lower terraces of Hadrian's Villa at Tivoli and entered the papal collections by way of the Albani sale of 1798. It is one of only three surviving porphyry portraits of Antinous; of the three, this is the most complete. The sculptor — almost certainly Aphrodisian — has worked against the grain of the stone, coaxing from its hardness the soft inward gaze that the literature attributes to the subject. The lower drapery, recut in white marble in the seventeenth century, has been left in place: a Roman head, a Baroque body, and an honest seam between them.

Height · 78 cm Material · porphyry & marble Location · Sala IV, central niche

Station V

The Inscriptions Corridor

forty-one stones, forty-one voices

V

Tituli Fori · the long corridor

Forty-one inscribed blocks and fragments · Luna and Pentelic marble · 27 BCE – 408 CE

The visitor now passes through a long, narrow gallery — forty metres in length, lit only from above — in which forty-one inscribed stones from the Imperial Forums are mounted at reading height along both walls. The earliest, a fragment of a dedicatory architrave from the Forum of Augustus, dates to 2 BCE. The latest, a verse epitaph reused as a doorstep in the Forum of Nerva, was carved in 408 CE on the eve of Alaric's first siege.

Each stone is presented with its Latin in raised gold capitals, an English translation in low pompeian-red small caps below, and — crucially — a pencilled note from the epigrapher recording where the cut is uncertain, where a chisel has slipped, where a stone-cutter has misspelled the name of his emperor. Several of the inscriptions have not previously been published; the catalogue raisonné, edited by Prof. Marco Castelli (Sapienza), is available at the entrance to the corridor.

Visitors are asked to read aloud, quietly, when alone. The corridor's acoustic — narrow, hard, unrelieved — is itself a Roman invention, and the inscriptions were made to be voiced.

Stones · 41 Span · 2 BCE — 408 CE Location · Corridoio Epigrafico

Station VI · Coda

A Meditation on Spolia

the second life of imperial stone

Marble is patient. The same block that lined the apse of Trajan's Basilica Ulpia became, in the eighth century, an altar in a Lateran chapel; in the twelfth, the lintel of a Cosmati doorway; in the seventeenth, the pavement of a Barberini stair. The stone outlasts the emperor, the pope, the cardinal, and at last the museum. To speak of "spolia" — the spoils of one age reused in the architecture of the next — is to speak of an honesty that the modern world, with its love of intact objects, has not always shared.

The exhibition closes in a single circular room. At its centre stands a fragment of column shaft in giallo antico, found in 2017 beneath the foundations of a baroque palazzo on the Via Giulia. The shaft, originally cut for the Forum of Caesar, was reused as a wellhead in the eleventh century, then split lengthwise in the sixteenth to serve as a doorframe, then buried in the eighteenth when the doorframe in turn was replaced. The four lives of this single stone are presented as a single object, with the seams visible, the recut surfaces unrestored.

We hope you will stand with it for a moment before leaving. There is no audio guide for this room. There is no label longer than the stone itself. The exhibition's argument — that Roman marble was a material, a logistics, and a language — comes here to its quiet final form: that it was, and remains, also a kind of memory.

Lapis loquitur · etiam fractus. The stone speaks · even broken.

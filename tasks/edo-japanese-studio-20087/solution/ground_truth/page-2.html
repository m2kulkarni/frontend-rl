工房の由来 The Workshop

Five generations of joiners have worked in the same single-bay machiya on Aburanokōji-dōri, planing hinoki by lamplight and couching kōzo paper at the long stone trough behind the kiln. What follows is a brief account of the house — its founders, its tools, and the men who keep it.

光琳之印

沿革 Five Generations, Two Centuries

From the founding by Tachibana Kōrin in the fourth year of Bunsei to the present day — a record kept on the rear cedar post of the workshop, planed flat once each generation.

  1. Bunsei — The Founding

    1821 · First Master: Tachibana Kōrin (1788–1849)

    Kōrin, a journeyman joiner from Ōmi, leased the rear bay of a charcoal merchant's machiya on Aburanokōji-dōri and hung his shingle as a maker of shōji frames and tea-room fittings. The original cedar workbench, now black with two centuries of oil, remains in daily use.

  2. Tenpō — The Paper Trough

    1837 · Second Master: Tachibana Saburōbei

    Saburōbei married into the Mizuno family of Echizen paper-makers and brought the first kōzo vat into the workshop yard. From this year forward the house has made its own paper for shōji and fusuma, never buying in finished sheet.

  3. Ansei — Fire & Rebuilding

    1858 · Third Master: Tachibana Kōzaburō

    The Great Ansei fire took the front of the machiya but spared the rear workshop. Kōzaburō rebuilt the dōzō storehouse using timbers salvaged from a dismantled Honganji sub-temple; three of those beams hold up the present-day plane room.

  4. Meiji — The Transition

    1889 · Fourth Master: Tachibana Yoshinosuke

    With the abolition of the daimyō and the loss of temple patronage, Yoshinosuke turned the studio toward private machiya commissions and quiet collaborations with the Urasenke tea school. The ledger from this period — kept in sumi on washi — is held in the Kyoto Municipal Archive.

  5. Heisei–Reiwa — The Present

    1991– · Fifth Master: Tachibana Sōshichi (b. 1962)

    Sōshichi inherited the studio from his father in the third year of Heisei. Under his hand the workshop has restored fittings for forty-seven machiya, two sub-temples of Daitoku-ji, and the tea-house at Shinnyo-dō. He keeps three principal joiners and two paper apprentices.

材と手 Of Hand-Planed Hinoki & Echizen Paper

We work, as we have always worked, in Kiso hinoki taken from forests above 1,000 metres and air-dried beneath the workshop eaves for no fewer than five winters before a plane touches it. The grain is read at the bench by the present master before cutting; nothing is sawn that has not first been read. A finished shōji frame leaves the studio with the last passage of the kanna still visible across its face — a faint, almost-not-there texture the eye knows before the hand does.

Our paper comes, as it has since 1837, from the Mizuno vat in Imadate, Echizen — kōzo cooked with wood-ash lye, beaten by foot, and couched by hand onto cypress boards. We pay ¥4,800 the sheet and receive perhaps thirty sheets in a good winter. No bleach, no sulphite, no machine fibre. Sheet by sheet it is mounted onto the frame in our rear room, where the humidity is held at sixty percent by a pan of water on the brazier.

— A note on lacquer:
Black urushi for fittings comes from Jōbōji in Iwate; we buy twice yearly, in the third and the ninth month, from the Sasaki house. Three coats, each cured ten days, never less.

職人 The Master & the Three Joiners

The studio carries five hands at present: the master, three principal joiners, and one paper apprentice in her fourth year.

Tachibana Sōshichi

b. 1962, Kyoto · fifth master · since Heisei 3 (1991)

Master Joiner

Sōshichi began at his father's bench at fourteen and was named fifth master in 1991, the year the great cedar at the rear of the plot was cut for the workshop's last full-rebuild of its plane room. He reads timber by hand and ear, sights frames against the north light only, and signs no piece he has not himself finished at the kanna.

Beyond the workshop he has lectured twice at the Kyoto Institute of Technology and serves as advising joiner to the Hachijō Onshi machiya restoration trust. He neither uses power tools nor forbids them; the apprentices may, the master will not.

Onishi Tatsuya

b. 1971 · joined 1994 · principal joiner

Trained at Takenaka Daiku-no-Gakkō. Onishi-san leads the shōji and ranma work; his lattice for the Sanbō-in commission of 2018 remains the studio's most-photographed piece. He keeps the tool-cabinet ledger.

Hayashi Mitsuko

b. 1979 · joined 2003 · paper & lacquer

The first woman to take a full apprenticeship under the Tachibana name. Hayashi-san now runs the paper trough and the urushi room; the dark lustre on every fusuma leaving the studio is laid down by her hand alone.

Kuroda Jirōta

b. 1984 · joined 2009 · joinery & framing

Came to the studio from a temple-carpentry house in Nara. Kuroda-san handles the heavy joinery — door frames, transom beams, the shoji-bone underwork — and is the studio's reader of standing trees on buying trips to Kiso each February.

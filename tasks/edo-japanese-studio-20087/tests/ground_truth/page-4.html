A studio is, before anything, the relationships it keeps with the forests, rivers, and lacquer-tappers that supply it. What follows is an account of where our timber, paper, and urushi originate — and the seasonal logic by which each is worked.

壹   SOURCINGthree houses, three rivers

The provenance of
a single shutter. 一枚の戸の出自

A finished shoji weighs perhaps four kilograms. Inside that weight are the patience of three families and the quiet of three rivers. Kōrin Mokkō works with the same houses our predecessors used — in some cases for over forty years — and we receive their material on the rhythm they choose, not on the rhythm of our commissions.

We send no purchase orders. A telephone call in late autumn from the Iwano family signals that the year's kōzo bark has been steamed; a postcard in February from Yoshino tells us which hinoki billets have finally surrendered their winter water. We plan around these small letters, not around our calendar.

What follows are the three houses with which the studio's work is most closely entangled. None advertise. All of them are listed in the ledgers of the Imperial Household Agency at one moment or another in the past century.

Yoshino Hinoki — Nakai Mokuzai

Yoshino-gun, Nara · merchants since 1842 · sixth generation

Our hinoki cypress comes exclusively from the Nakai timber yard on the Yoshino-gawa, where Nakai Tatsuya selects standing trees of 180 years or more. Billets are air-dried under cedar shingles for a minimum of four winters. We pay roughly ¥48,000 per shaku-jime; the figure has not changed since 2017.

Echizen Washi — Iwano-ke

Imadate, Fukui · paper-makers since 1751 · ninth generation

The Iwano family — currently led by Iwano Ichibei IX, a Living National Treasure — supply our hōsho and torinoko papers. Each sheet is hand-couched from kōzo grown on the Okamoto-gawa floodplain, beaten on stone, and dried on cedar boards under late-autumn sun. We receive 300 sheets annually, delivered the third week of November.

Jōbōji Urushi — Satō Kenji

Ninohe, Iwate · single tapper · seasonal yield 1.6 kg

Satō-san is one of fewer than thirty urushi-kaki remaining in Japan. He works alone on a hillside above the Anebetsu stream, tapping perhaps 250 trees between June and October. We receive his entire summer yield — about 1.6 kilograms — at ¥38,000 per 100 grams. It is enough for eleven commissions a year, no more.

A year folded into
four kinds of work. 四つの節

The studio's calendar follows the materials, not the client. A commission accepted in March may not deliver until the following December — because urushi requires summer humidity, paper requires the dry clarity of October, and joinery requires winter.

From June through early September, when Kyoto's humidity stays above 75 percent for weeks at a time, we apply urushi. Lacquer cures by absorbing water, not by losing it; the muggy stillness of the rainy season is a slow tide that pulls each coat into hardness over six to ten days. We work in a cedar-lined cabinet — the furo — set behind the workshop's north wall, where temperature stays near 24°C.

“We do not finish a piece. We arrange the conditions in which the piece, over a year, finishes itself.” — Tanabe Kōrin III, in conversation, Spring 2019

Autumn belongs to paper. Sheets from the Iwano family arrive flat-packed in cedar boxes during the third week of November. We mount them onto kumiko frames in our smallest room, where the air is dry and still, using nori glue cooked from three-year-aged wheat starch. A single shoji panel takes a full afternoon to mount, and must rest forty-eight hours under boards before it is touched again.

Winter — December through February — is for joinery. The hinoki has by now lost the last of its summer moisture; the workshop holds steady at 8°C; chisels stay sharp for hours rather than minutes. This is when kumiko lattices are cut and assembled, and when the ari-tsugi dovetail-less joints which give our cabinets their reputation are pared into being.

The Kumiko Lattice 組子 ・ あさのは

Hinoki strips, 3mm × 9mm · pattern: asa-no-ha

The asa-no-ha — hemp-leaf — pattern shown here resolves to a six-pointed star whose every line is precisely 30 millimetres. Each intersection is a half-lap pared with a 15mm shōji-nomi. A 600 × 400 panel contains 184 such cuts.

貳

The Ari-tsugi Joint 蟻継ぎ ・ 留型

Hinoki on hinoki · no glue, no metal · tolerance 0.05mm

Our ari-tsugi differs from the European dovetail in that it receives no glue and is hidden on three faces. The joint draws tighter as the seasons compress the hinoki — a property that rewards the hand that cut it within twelve months.

參

Layered Shoji Frame 障子 ・ 重ね

Outer stile, inner kumiko, hosho paper · cross-section

Three layers, each contributing one quality: the stile gives rigidity, the kumiko gives geometry, the paper gives light. A standard 1820 × 910 panel comprises 88 kumiko members and a single uncut sheet of Iwano hōsho.

Notes from the bench. 仕事帳より

We keep a ledger — bound annually in indigo cotton — in which every commission is matched against the materials it consumed. The page reproduced opposite is taken from the entry for a machiya restoration in Nishijin, completed in March 2024. It records, in the master's hand, the small economy by which the building was put back together.

Three values are noted for each material: its source, its weight or count, and its cost in monme — an obsolete unit we retain because it forces us to weigh things rather than count them. A single shoji frame, by this reckoning, is somewhere between 460 and 510 monme of work.

We publish nothing of these ledgers ordinarily. The page below appears here only because the machiya it describes — a nineteenth-century weaver's house on Senbon-dōri — is itself now a public concern, restored on behalf of the Kyoto Heritage Trust.

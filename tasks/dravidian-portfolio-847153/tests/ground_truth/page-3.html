On the Mānasāra &
the Mayamata

Four short readings drawn from a working draftsman's notebooks — circulated among fellow śilpins, students of the Tanjore Heritage Society, and any patron willing to follow a chisel into a paragraph. Composed between Margazhi 2018 and Aippasi 2023, in Kumbakonam, on the bank of the Kāveri.

R. Kalyanasundaram, Sthapati  ·  Atelier on Sannidhi Street
— Four Essays · Read at the Tanjore Heritage Society —
— Essay the First · I —

Tiers and the Tāla-māna of the Vimāna

Every gopuram I have measured at Darasuram, at Gangaikonda, at our own Sārangapāni — insists on the same quiet arithmetic. The Mānasāra, in its thirty-fifth chapter, does not romance the eye; it disciplines it. A vimāna of seven tiers is not seven things stacked. It is one body, divided by the tāla, the span of the principal deity within. The śikhara is the head; the griva, the throat; the prastara, the shoulder. To draw the elevation honestly, one must first know the height of the mūla-bera, then divide.

I keep a brass scale in the studio, cut to nine talas, used since my grandfather Kandasāmi Sthapati's time. It tells me when a draughted tier is half a finger too tall — a small lie that, repeated upward, becomes the squat, unhappy gopurams one sees being put up around the new agraharams.

The second part of the essay attempts a small reconciliation of the Mānasāra and the Kāmikāgama on the question of the karṇa-kūṭa: a square, or — as the Kāmikāgama permits — a rounded, bell-shouldered version. The drawings are folio 14 to 19 of my notebook of 2018.

— Essay the Second · II —

On the Padma Capital

Of all the elements I have been asked to carve, the padma capital is the one most often misunderstood by those who order it and most often loved by those who carve it. The Mayamata, in its fifteenth chapter, gives the padma five mouldings: the kumuda below, the gala, the padma proper, the abacus, and the phalaka. The mistake — repeated in the new pillared maṇḍapams of the 1990s — is to thicken the gala until the padma rests on a barrel rather than a neck.

A lotus, after all, opens above water, not above stone. The neck must remain slender. I have, since 2002, refused commissions where the patron insists on a "stout" gala for reasons of perceived strength. The strength of a column is in its proportion, not its girth — a thing every village mason understood before the cement contractors arrived.

— Essay the Third · III —

Notes Toward a Modern Kolam Grammar

Kolam is, properly, an architectural rite, not only a courtyard ornament. It is the act of laying down a grid before the sun is up, and then folding a single thread of rice flour around that grid until it closes. The dot grid — pulli — is a plan; the looped line — neli — is the elevation. A śilpin who cannot draw a five-by-five sikku kolam without lifting the chalk should not, in my opinion, be permitted to drive a chisel.

What I propose in these pages is a small notation: a way of writing kolam patterns the way we write talas in music, so a young apprentice can learn twenty-eight foundational figures by heart in one season rather than three. My wife, Pārvati, has been quietly doing this for forty years; I am only writing down what she already knows.

Grammar in three parts: the dot, the loop, the closure. Twenty-eight plates, drawn at the studio table during the rains of 2021. The fourteenth plate — the brahma-mudichi — is, I think, the most useful for floor-plan composition.

— Essay the Fourth · IV —

The Disappearing Vocabulary of the Yali

A yali is not a lion. It is not, as some recent guidebooks claim, a "mythical composite beast." A yali is a grammatical creature — leonine forequarters, elephantine snout, a tail that resolves into a leaf. Each variant carries a name: simha-vyāla, gaja-vyāla, aśva-vyāla. At Madurai's Pudu Mandapam I counted seventeen distinct vyāla types in 1994; on a return visit in 2023, the names had thinned to two. Guides simply say "yali."

The vocabulary disappears because the work disappears. When a temple renovation contracts the carving of a thousand-pillar maṇḍapam to a firm in Bengaluru, the bid wins on tonnage of granite, not on competence in the beast. The result is a corridor of identical, hollow-eyed cats. I have refused such tenders four times, most recently in Shrāvaṇa 2022.

The essay closes with a glossary of forty-one yali types, with a hand-drawn plate of each — compiled with my apprentice Selvaganesh, on smoke-finished paper, between the festivals of Aippasi 2022 and Tai 2023.

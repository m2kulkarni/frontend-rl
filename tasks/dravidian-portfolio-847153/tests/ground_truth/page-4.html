§ I · Paramparā

The Lineage of the Hand

R. Kalyanasundaram Sthapati was born in Tiruvarur in the Tamil month of Maasi, 1958, the third son of T. Ramanatha Sthapati (1932–2004) and Lakshmi Ammal. He took the chisel at the age of eleven, learning first the squaring of the granite block and the cutting of the plinth-mouldings, then advancing — under his father's exacting watch — to the carving of the kumbha and the lower register of the dvārapāla figure.

His father, T. Ramanatha, had himself been apprenticed to V. Subramanya Sthapati of Swamimalai, working through the great post-Independence revival on commissions for the Endowments Department at Tiruvaiyaru, Tirupperundurai, and (in 1971) the rebuilt Adhiṣṭhāna of the Pancha-vaṭi shrine at Mannargudi. The instruments now on the workshop wall — the heavy takku, the long-shafted brass plumb, the forty-eight uli of varying gauge — descend in unbroken ownership from that bench.

From 1976 to 1981, R. Kalyanasundaram studied at the Government College of Architecture and Sculpture, Mamallapuram, under Prof. M. Muthiah Sthapati and the late Dr. V. Ganapati Sthapati. The diploma in his cabinet records distinctions in iconometry (Tālamāna), in stone-dressing (Śilā-saṁskāra), and in the drafting of the vimāna elevation. He returned to Kumbakonam in the spring of 1986 and founded the present atelier in a rented shed behind the Sarangapani temple tank; the present premises at Sannidhi Street were taken in 1994.

A Note on Tempering

The chisels are re-tempered every year in the lull before the festival season — the months of Aippasi and Kārttikai — at the Vēlu smithy on the Tiruvidaimarudur road. The smith, Mr. Vēlu Asari, is the third generation of his family to do this work for our bench.

The hardness is judged by ear: the chisel is held by the haft and the bevel struck against an old anvil-corner. The note must ring clear and high, without a rattle, before the master will accept it back to the rack.

The Theodolite, 1998 A used Sokkia DT-5, purchased for ₹ 14,000 from the estate of a retired PWD surveyor. It is the only twentieth-century instrument the studio has admitted to the bench, and it is used only on site, never in the shed.
§ IV · The Day at the Bench

From Dawn-bath to Lamp-lighting

The studio keeps the old hours. Work begins at the second hour after sunrise, after the master has bathed and made his offering at the Sarangapani shrine. It ends with the lighting of the brass lamp at the threshold.

No chisel is sounded on the festival days of Vinayaka Chaturthi, Mahā-Śivarātri, the nine days of Navarātri, the day of Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī, and on the new-moon (amāvāsyai) of every month.

  1. 05:30Master's pūjā at the workshop shrine; lamp lit before the granite Ganesha.
  2. 06:30Apprentices sweep the yard, lay out the day's chisels, fill the water-pot.
  3. 07:00First sitting at the bench. Heavy roughing-out work.
  4. 10:30Tea, with jaggery, brought by Padmavati Ammal.
  5. 12:30The midday meal, taken in the courtyard on banana-leaf.
  6. 13:30Drafting-board work; quiet hours, the master at his table.
  7. 15:30Second sitting at the bench. Fine-finishing only, no hammering.
  8. 18:00Tools racked, sand swept, threshold-lamp lit. Day closed.

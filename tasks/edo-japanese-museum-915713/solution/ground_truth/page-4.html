Chapter Four · Architecture

The Machiya
at 218 Aneyakōji-dōri

Our home is itself an exhibit: a late-Edo townhouse, narrow at the street and long to the back, restored over thirty-eight months between 2014 and 2017 under master carpenter Tanaka Kōji of the Sukiya-daiku guild.

What follows is a short essay on the building — the earthen passage, the inner courtyard, the lattice façade, and the tatami modules that quietly govern every room.

An Essay on the Building
町家
Machiya

A merchant townhouse particular to Kyoto: narrow façade taxed by frontage, long interior, and a deep central axis that admits light, air, and weather.

Address

Nakagyō-ku, Kyoto 604-8091

Restoration Lead

Tanaka Kōji, Sukiya-daiku Guild of Kyoto. Five carpenters, two plasterers, one roof-tiler from Awaji.

Status

Designated Tangible Cultural Property of the City of Kyoto, 2018.

The house at 218 Aneyakōji-dōri was raised in 1851 by a wholesaler of Tango silk named Sumiya Tōsuke, on a plot of just over five and a half ken at the street. For more than a century afterward it served first as a warehouse, then as a dry-goods shop, then — for a long, quiet stretch — as a private home. When the museum acquired the building in early 2014 it was tilted by twelve centimeters along its north wall, its earthen passage half-filled with a 1960s plywood floor, and its interior garden converted into a parking space for a single small car.

The work of restoration, led by master carpenter Tanaka Kōji of the Sukiya-daiku guild, took thirty-eight months. It was not a reconstruction. Roughly seventy percent of the original framing — most of the hinoki cypress posts, the great daikoku-bashira at the entrance, and the blackened beams above the kitchen — was retained, repaired by the sashimono technique of cutting in a new section of timber along the grain so that the joint reads as a scar rather than a replacement.

What we mended, we wanted to be visible. A building that pretends not to have aged is a building that has been killed and embalmed. — Tanaka Kōji, restoration notebook, 2016

The four organising elements of the plan are the tōriniwa, the tsuboniwa, the koshi, and the tatami module. None of these is decorative. Each does a quiet structural or environmental job, and together they describe what the architectural historian Itō Teiji called the "logic of the deep lot." The visitor walking from the front lattice to the rear veranda passes through approximately twenty-seven meters of building and three distinct climates: street, passage, and garden — without crossing a single threshold of stone.

The galleries you visit today are arranged within this structure, not laid over it. A vitrine in the second zashiki sits exactly where the family altar once stood; the print-rotation room at the rear was the kura, the fireproof storehouse, and still has its original mud-rendered walls forty-eight centimeters thick. We have left them unpainted.

Four Elements,
One Plan

A Kyoto machiya is not a sequence of rooms but a sequence of conditions. Each of these four organising features still performs its original environmental work in the museum today — they are not exhibits, but the reasons the building stands, ventilates, and lights itself.

— 01 —

The Tōriniwa

通り庭 · earthen passage

A bare-earth corridor runs the entire 27-meter depth of the house, from the front shop to the rear garden. Originally the kitchen, well, and oven sat along its length; cooking smoke escaped through a high lantern roof called the hibukuro. Today the floor remains tataki — slaked-lime, clay, and bittern, hand-pounded over four days in March 2016.

Length 27 m · Width 1.8 m
— 02 —

The Tsuboniwa

坪庭 · interior garden

A small enclosed garden — exactly one tsubo, or 3.3 square meters — opens at the building's midpoint. It is the engine of the house: cool evening air falls into it, warm air rises out, and the long plan is ventilated end-to-end. Ours holds a single Sawara cypress, two stones from the bed of the Kamo river, and one moss-covered stone basin carved in 1888.

3.3 m² · One Sawara cypress
— 03 —

The Koshi Lattice

格子 · façade lattice

The street face is screened by a vertical wooden lattice — the koshi — whose precise spacing once advertised the household's trade. A silk merchant used the so-called itoya-gōshi, with battens four bu apart. Our restoration follows that pattern: 12 millimeters of cedar in 18-millimeter rhythm, throwing a pleated shadow into the front room from late afternoon onward.

Cedar · 1,108 individual battens
— 04 —

The Tatami Module

畳割 · the floor mat as unit

Every interior dimension descends from the Kyōma tatami: 955 by 1,910 millimeters. Posts step in mat-widths; ceilings rise in half-mat modules; sliding doors close on mat seams. To stand in the second zashiki is to stand inside a building whose every measurement is the height and reach of a single seated body, repeated.

Kyōma · 1.82 m² per mat
§ Materials

Cypress, paper,
and tile.

The restoration limited itself to materials that would have been available to the original builders within fifty kilometers of the site. The few modern interventions — stainless steel ties at three roof junctions, a lime-based vapor barrier behind the kura wall — are recorded in a small ledger kept open in the entry.

Hinoki Cypress

檜 · structural posts

Six original daikoku-bashira and twenty-two secondary posts in hinoki from Kiso, milled square at 12 sun (≈360 mm). New material was sourced from the Kitayama forests north of the city, dried for eighteen months before fitting.

Kiso & Kitayama, 2014

Washi Paper

和紙 · shoji screens

Forty-one shoji panels glazed in handmade Kurotani washi from Ayabe, fifty kilometers north. The paper is replaced each December and again before the rainy season; spent panels are composted in the rear garden.

Kurotani, Ayabe

Kawara Roof Tile

瓦 · clay roof tile

2,840 sangawara tiles fired by the Tanaka kiln on Awaji island — not related to the carpenter — to the deep slate-grey ibushi finish characteristic of Kyoto rooflines. Twelve original Edo-era tiles were retained at the ridge as witnesses.

Awaji-shima, 2016

A note on the engawa, opened by season.

At the rear of the building, between the last tatami room and the tsuboniwa, runs a narrow wooden veranda — the engawa — fifty-eight centimeters wide and seven meters long. It is neither inside nor outside: a slatted hinoki deck under a deep eave, edged by a row of removable shoji on one side and the open garden on the other.

The engawa is opened to visitors only when the weather permits the house to be ventilated end-to-end without harming the works on paper in our collection. In practice this means a few weeks each spring, the cooler mornings of high summer, and the dry weeks of autumn. The schedule below is the calendar we publish each January.

Sit, if you can, on the cinnabar cushion at the south end. From there the building is exactly what its builders intended: a long corridor of shadow, ending in a square of garden light.

A house of
quiet things.花園江戸生活博物館

Since 1968, the Hanazono Museum has gathered, studied, and shown the daily life of Edo — its tools, its prints, its wood and lacquer — under the eaves of a working Kyoto townhouse. We are small by design. We hold roughly four thousand objects, publish one journal a year, and open six rooms to the public.

§ I — Institutional History

Six decades, three thresholds.

The museum's history can be read in three movements: a private gift, a public charter, and a return to the wood-and-paper architecture for which the collection was originally made.

  1. 1962The Donation

    The Yoshida Family Donation

    On the death of the textile merchant Yoshida Heizaemon V, his widow Kimi and their son Tatsuo presented to the city of Kyoto some 1,840 household objects, woodblock prints, and account books accumulated by four generations of the Yoshida house in Nishijin. The collection had been kept, until then, in the family's storehouse on Higashi-no-tōin Street.

  2. 1968Incorporation

    The Museum is Chartered

    Under founding director Yoshida Tatsuo (1921–2003), the Hanazono Museum of Edo Life was incorporated as a public-interest foundation on 14 March 1968. The first galleries occupied two rented floors above a kimono shop on Sanjō-dōri; admission was set at ¥80, and the museum opened with 312 catalogued items on view.

  3. 1974Research Begins

    First Issue of Edo Seikatsu Kenkyū

    The museum's annual journal of research into Edo-period daily life is published for the first time in October 1974, with contributions from Yoshida Tatsuo, Hayashi Kōichirō of Kyoto University, and the textile historian Nagai Sumiko.

  4. 1991Expansion

    Acquisition of the Sano Print Archive

    A bequest from the print collector Sano Yoshirō adds 622 single-sheet ukiyo-e and twelve complete series, including a near-complete Kisokaidō by Hiroshige and Eisen — the spine of every print exhibition the museum has mounted since.

  5. 2017The Move

    Removal to the Aneyakōji Machiya

    After a four-year restoration overseen by the architect Tanahashi Jirō, the museum reopens on 1 October 2017 in a 1908 merchant's townhouse on Aneyakōji-dōri. The new premises return the collection to the kind of building it was made for: low rooms, paper light, polished cedar floors.

Chief Curator

Dr. Saitō Mariko

齋藤 真理子 — appointed 2019

Educated at Waseda and the École Pratique des Hautes Études, Saitō joined the museum in 2008 as associate curator of prints and was named chief curator in 2019. Her monograph on the publisher Tsutaya Jūzaburō (Iwanami Shoten, 2014) remains a standard reference.

She leads a curatorial team of seven and chairs the editorial committee of Edo Seikatsu Kenkyū.

§ III — Research & Publications

A small scholarly programme, run from the back room.

Hanazono is a research museum first. Each year the curatorial team selects two or three sustained lines of inquiry — currently, the economy of single-sheet print publishing in Bunsei-era Edo, and the textile dyer's account book as historical source. Findings are reported, in long form, in the museum's annual journal.

The reading room on the second floor of the machiya holds approximately 6,200 reference volumes and is open to qualified researchers by appointment, Wednesday through Saturday. Visiting fellowships are offered each year to two early-career scholars, typically in residence from October through March.

Edo Seikatsu Kenkyū江戸生活研究 · Studies in Edo Daily Life

Published every November since 1974, the journal carries between eight and twelve peer-reviewed articles, a curatorial dossier, and short notes on recent acquisitions. Volume 50 is in preparation for autumn this year.

I · The ProjectAn inland road, drawn in sixty-nine breaths

Between 1835 and the early years of the Tenpō reform, the publisher Iseya Rihei — operating under the studio name Kinjudō from his shop on the eastern side of Nihonbashi — issued the most ambitious print series of the late Edo period. Kisokaidō rokujū-kyū tsugi, the Sixty-Nine Stations of the Kisokaidō, traces the inland highway that connected the shōgun's capital to Kyōto, climbing through the Kiso valley and the mountain passes of Shinano province before descending into Ōmi.

Where the better-known Tōkaidō hugged the Pacific coast, the Kisokaidō cut into the heart of the country: snowbound, cedar-shaded, and travelled in winter by daimyō processions returning to Edo under the system of sankin-kōtai. Keisai Eisen began the series with twenty-four sheets but withdrew, possibly under pressure from the censors or from Iseya himself; Utagawa Hiroshige completed the remaining forty-six between 1837 and 1838.

"The road bends and the snow comes down — Eisen sees it as an obstacle, Hiroshige as the very subject of the picture." — Saitō M., wall text, station 38 (Suhara)

II · The Post-Station SystemSixty-nine inns, two domains, and a shogunal ledger

Each shukuba on the Kisokaidō was a fully administered settlement, required by the Bakufu to maintain a fixed number of post-horses (typically twenty-five), porters, and an official lodging for daimyō travel known as the honjin. The road was patrolled at the Usui and Kiso-Fukushima barriers, where women, firearms and severed heads were checked against written permits — a fact that quietly governs many of the prints' compositions, in which figures cluster, wait, or are turned back at gates.

Hiroshige's images were not topographical surveys but mnemonic objects. Travellers carried them, collectors arranged them in albums, and the publisher reissued popular sheets from worn blocks. Several of the impressions in this exhibition show the soft, slightly sunken outlines of late-state printing — evidence not of failure but of the series' commercial life.

III · Iseya RiheiThe publisher and his block-cutters

Iseya Rihei is named on every sheet in the series, his seal — a small kin in a gourd — pressed into the lower margin beside the censor's kiwame. He was not a wealthy publisher by the standards of Tsutaya Jūzaburō's heirs, but he commanded a workshop large enough to commission a full-colour nishiki-e series of nearly seventy designs. Recent attribution work by the Tōkyō National Research Institute identifies the block-cutter Yokokawa Takejirō as responsible for at least eleven of the figural blocks; the colour blocks were almost certainly cut by a separate hand.

The collaboration was unusual. Eisen and Hiroshige worked for different print networks, and the decision to publish them under one title — rather than two competing series — suggests that Iseya was hedging: when one artist faltered, the other could carry the road.

"Look closely at Oiwake: the snow is not white pigment but the bare paper, masked by ten separate impressions. The block-cutter's restraint is the print's true colour."

IV · Multi-Block Nishiki-eTen passes through the press

A full-colour nishiki-e from the late 1830s required between eight and fifteen separate carved cherrywood blocks, each registering a single colour or tonal pass against a pair of kentō notches cut into every block's edge. The deepest impressions in the Kisokaidō series — Hiroshige's Kiso no Yamanaka, in particular — demand at least twelve passes: a key block, two greys, two indigos including the imported bero-ai Prussian blue, a pale grey-blue gradation (bokashi) for the mountain mist, two greens, ochre, vermilion for the figures' robes, a black overprint for the foreground branches, and a final embossing pass (karazuri) for the snow on the rooftops.

Prussian blue, available in Japan from around 1829, is the chemical signature of the series. Its arrival permitted Hiroshige to render water and atmosphere with a tonal range previously possible only in monochrome ink painting, and the Kisokaidō prints exploit it constantly: the river at Nojiri, the dusk over Yawata, the haze in the Wada pass.

V · A Note on the HangingReading order

The fifteen impressions on view are not arranged in geographical sequence. We have grouped them instead by atmosphere: arrivals and departures along the east wall, weather across the long north wall, and the four night scenes at the far end of the gallery, lit at 50 lux. A printed station-by-station gazetteer is available at the entrance for visitors who wish to walk the road in its proper order.

Conservation note

Six-week rotation

Edo woodblock prints are exquisitely sensitive to light. To limit cumulative exposure to no more than 50,000 lux-hours per impression per year, the fifteen sheets in this exhibition are rotated on a strict six-week schedule, after which they return to dark storage at 18 °C and 50 % RH for a minimum of three years.

Visitors planning to see specific impressions are encouraged to consult the schedule below, or to telephone the Prints & Drawings desk on 075-441-2208.

Gallery IV · Permanent Installation · Opened 14 March 2014

The Reconstructed Machiya 町家

A full-scale Kyoto merchant house, raised plank by plank within the museum's east wing under the direction of miyadaiku Tsujimura Kenji of the Kyōto Daiku-gumi guild.

Visitors enter beneath an indigo noren, cross a tamped-earth doma, and walk the long passage that links street to garden — the same procession a draper's apprentice would have made in 1843.

Carpenter's Note · 一

Provenance & Method

A house joined with neither nail nor glue, raised in two seasons of cold work.

Construction began on 3 February 2013 and concluded on the spring equinox of the following year. The structure occupies a footprint of 6.4 metres frontage by 18.2 metres depth — the so-called unagi-no-nedoko, or "eel's bedroom" proportion — drawn from a draper's house surveyed by Kyoto City in the Sanjō-Aburanokōji district before its demolition in 2009.

Tsujimura-sensei, fifth in his line of miyadaiku, refused all metal fasteners save the copper nails of the gable. The fifty-three timbers of the principal frame are joined by watari-ago and kanawa-tsugi — laps and scarfs cut by hand, fitted dry, drawn together with oak pegs. Visitors who linger beneath the entrance beam will find the carpenter's sumitsubo line still inked across its underside.

The reconstruction was funded in part by a grant of ¥48,200,000 from the Bunka-chō Cultural Affairs Agency and by private subscription of the Yanaka Merchants' Association.

Five movements through the house.

From the street's noise to the silence of the inner garden, the machiya is an instrument tuned to the day. Read each chamber in sequence.

— 一 —

暖簾

Noren · The Indigo Threshold

Three split panels of indigo-dyed cotton hang from the lintel. The character tsuji (辻), in cinnabar, marks the family. By Edo custom the noren is lifted at sunrise and taken down at the close of business; ours is lowered each evening at 16:30.

Dyed by Kondō Setsurō of Kyoto, registered Indigo Master, vat no. 3.

Read the dyer's account
— 二 —

土間

Doma · The Earthen Floor

A tamped floor of tataki — slaked lime, river clay, and bittern, beaten by hand for nine days. Cool in summer, warm beneath straw sandals in winter; this is the working room where customers stood and accounts were settled at the raised chōba desk.

The kamado stove, three-mouthed, was rebuilt from a fragment held by the Kyōto Mingei archive.

Read about tataki recipes
— 三 —

通り庭

Tōriniwa · The Passage

Eighteen metres of doma run unbroken from front door to garden, passing beneath a soaring hibukuro smoke-well that draws cooking heat up through the rafters. Above, the great matsu tie-beam is a single timber 9.6 metres in length, sourced from a fallen pine in Tamba.

Here the visitor sees the structural truth of the house — every post, every brace, every wedge.

Read the framing diagram
— 四 —

座敷

Zashiki · The Tatami Room

Six tatami of Kumamoto igusa rush, laid in the auspicious shū-gi arrangement. The tokonoma alcove holds a rotating hanging scroll; in October, an autograph poem by Yosa Buson dated 1773. The shōji are paper from Echizen, replaced each November.

Sit, if the docent permits, and listen for the temple bell at 17:00.

Read the scroll rotation
— 五 —

坪庭

Tsuboniwa · The Inner Court

A garden of three tsubo — roughly ten square metres — set between zashiki and storehouse. A single chōzubachi water basin, hewn from Kurama stone, catches the rain that falls through the open roof. Around it: a clipped nanten, a stone lantern of the oribe type, and three azaleas that bloom in the second week of May.

The garden's purpose is climatic before it is contemplative: it draws cool air through the deep house on summer afternoons, and lets the winter sun fall onto the engawa floorboards. It is, in the carpenter's words, the lung of the machiya.

Bill of Materials · 二

Where the timber, paper, and rush were sourced.

Every material was chosen by Tsujimura-sensei against the surviving fabric of the original Sanjō house. Provenance is documented in the carpenter's day-book, on display in the museum library.

MaterialOriginNoteQuantity
Hinoki 檜 Kiso Valley, Nagano 300-year-old cypress for posts, beams, and the entrance threshold. Felled under permit of the Imperial Household Forestry Office, autumn 2011, then air-dried for fourteen months. 53 pcs.
Sugi 杉 Yoshino, Nara Cedar for ceiling boards and the second-floor flooring. Quarter-sawn to 12mm; finished with raw urushi lacquer, two coats. 218 m²
Washi 和紙 Echizen, Fukui Hand-laid kōzo paper for shōji and fusuma, made by Iwano Heizaburō (Living National Treasure, ninth generation). Replaced each November before the cold winds. 96 sheets
Igusa 藺草 Yatsushiro, Kumamoto Soft rush for tatami matting, woven on hand-drawn looms by the Maruichi cooperative. Six tatami in the zashiki, four in the okunoma; rotated every six months. 10 mats
Tsuchi-kabe 土壁 Jurakudai clay, Kyoto Earthen plaster — clay, sand, chopped straw — applied over a bamboo-lath frame in seven coats, the finish coat tinted with iron oxide to the ochre tone of an Edo merchant's wall. 142 m²
Kawara 瓦 Awaji Island, Hyōgo Smoked-grey roof tiles in the sangawara pattern. Each tile fired for 72 hours by the Tarumi kiln; the ridge tile carries the carpenter's stamp. 2,840 tiles

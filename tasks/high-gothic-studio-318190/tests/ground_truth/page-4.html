The Discipline
of the Long Look

On the measured survey, the ethics of intervention, and what we owe to fabric older than ourselves.

Marginalia

I. Of the measured survey

le relevé · before the pencil dares the plan

Before any intervention is drawn, a building must be heard. The measured survey is the discipline by which we listen — a slow inventory of stone, mortar, joint, and crack, conducted in sketchbook and total-station and, increasingly, in point-cloud. At Atelier Saint-Sévrin we hold to the rule that no proposal may be lodged with a Direction régionale des affaires culturelles before the survey has been bound, signed, and stamped — and before, separately, every elevation has been redrawn by hand at 1:50.

The hand redrawing is not nostalgia. It is the only way we know to discover what the laser has not registered: the faint chamfer that betrays a recut jamb of 1689; the splay of a buttress weeping iron from a Victorian cramp; the moment a fourteenth-century mason changed his mind and altered a course-line to clear a putlog. The instrument records geometry. The pencil records intention. A scheme that proceeds from one without the other will, sooner or later, fail the building.

Our standard package for a parochial commission — Saint-Léger d'Essômes was typical — runs to roughly fourteen weeks: two on archive, three on access scaffold, six on the measured drawings themselves, three on the condition report. We charge it at cost. We have never recovered a survey fee on a project that did not also commission the work, and we accept this as the price of doing the discipline honestly.

"As much as is necessary; as little as possible. The rest is vanity dressed as scholarship."

II. The ethics of intervention

les chartes · the inherited contract

A studio that works on listed fabric does not invent its ethics — it inherits them. We work, in practice, beneath three documents and within the spirit of a fourth.

  • 1964Venice Charter. Replacements must integrate harmoniously with the whole, but at the same time be distinguishable from the original.
  • 1994Nara Document. Authenticity is plural, culture-bound, and includes the immaterial: workmanship, use, spirit.
  • 2003ICOMOS Principles, Stone. On compatibility of repair mortars; on the bias toward minimum intervention.
  • 2013Burra Charter. Cultural significance as the test of every decision.

The Venice Charter is the spine of our daily reasoning. Article 9 — that any addition "indispensable for reasons of aesthetic or technical order" must bear the mark of its own time — is the article we cite most often to clients who ask us to "make it match." We do not make it match. We make it answer. The Nara Document, three decades younger, gave us licence to think about authenticity beyond the substance of stone: the authenticity of liturgical use at a still-functioning church is not interchangeable with the authenticity of a museum-piece chancel, and our schemes are calibrated accordingly.

The harder question, in our experience, is not whether to intervene. It is the choice, when intervention is unavoidable, between like-for-like replacement in matching limestone and an honest contemporary insertion legible as the work of our century. Both can be defended within the charters. The decision falls on the building — on whether the lost element belonged to a continuous, repeatable language (a vault rib, a tracery mullion) or stood as a singular gesture (a pinnacle, a finial, a tympanum). For the first, like-for-like, in stone of demonstrably compatible petrography, cut by hand to the surveyed profile. For the second, almost always, the contemporary insertion — or no insertion at all.

III. The third question

to reinstate, to insert, or to leave

There is a third option which the literature treats too lightly: to do nothing. To stabilise, to protect, and to leave the lacuna visible as a lacuna. Ruskin would have called this the Lamp of Memory. We call it, more prosaically, the null intervention. It is the option we recommend more often than clients expect, and it is the option whose defence consumes the largest share of our written reports. A missing pinnacle is not always a wound; sometimes it is a record. A studio that will not read the difference has no business holding the pencil.

What follows, in the column to the right, are three decisions made on three projects between 2019 and 2024. They are not exemplary. They are simply the working of the method on three buildings that asked three different questions.

Case study · i

The Chancel-Step at Noyon

2019–2021 · Cathédrale Notre-Dame de Noyon · liturgical fabric

The principal step into the chancel had been refaced four times since 1804, each campaign cutting back further into the worn 13th-century tread. By 2018 the stone retained perhaps thirty per cent original substance and the lip stood 38 mm proud of the sanctuary pavement — a trip hazard at every Mass.

We surveyed the step in eleven sections. Eight retained sufficient mediaeval fabric to be conserved in place with a lime-shelter coat. Three did not. We replaced these in Saint-Maximin fine, cut by hand, set with a hot-mixed NHL 3.5 mortar, and dated discreetly on the underside — asn · mmxx.

Decision Like-for-like in three sections; conservation in eight. The new stone is identifiable to the eye that knows where to look, and invisible to the eye that does not. Cost: €184,000.

Case study · ii

Glazing at the Cluny Pavilion

2022–2023 · Pavillon de Cluny, Paris Ve · contemporary insertion

A small ancillary pavilion of the abbatial enclosure, much altered, had lost its 14th-century traceried lights to a 1928 reglazing in steel and obscured cathedral. The fabric of the tracery survived; the glass did not, and no documentary record of the original cartoons existed.

We rejected pastiche. There was no scholarship to support a "restored" glazing scheme, and a faux-mediaeval quarry-and-grisaille would have lent false antiquity to a window with no surviving evidence of one. We commissioned the glassmaker Anne-Sophie Bauer to design a contemporary scheme of mouth-blown clear cylinder, leaded in a rhythm derived from the surveyed tracery but legible to any visitor as work of 2023.

Decision Honest contemporary insertion under Venice Article 9. The mediaeval stone tracery reads first; the glass second; the date of the glass, third. Cost: €312,000.

Case study · iii

The Missing Pinnacle, Saint-Léger

2023–2024 · Saint-Léger d'Essômes-sur-Marne · question of reinstatement

The south-east buttress of the chevet has stood without its pinnacle since at least 1789, when a parish account records "la pyramide jetée bas par la foudre." A 1654 elevation by Israël Silvestre shows it intact; nothing else does. The parish council asked us to rebuild it.

We declined. The single oblique image is insufficient evidence for a 4.2 m carved element; the buttress has stood without it for 235 years, and the silhouette of the chevet as it now reads — truncated, austere — has itself entered the historical record. We proposed instead a campaign of consolidation to the buttress head, a discreet bronze plaque set into the pavement below recording the lost feature, and the deposit of our research file in the Archives départementales at Laon.

Decision Null intervention; the lacuna preserved as record. The parish accepted in March 2024. Cost: €41,000.

The Course of a Survey

Fourteen weeks, in five movements, from first key turned in the lock to bound report on the diocesan desk.

i.

Archive

Diocesan archives, departmental archives, parish accounts, photographic record. Every prior campaign documented, dated, and located on plan.

Wks 1–2
ii.

Access

Independent scaffold, drone overflight where the canopy permits, lift access to vault haunches. Photogrammetric capture at 4 mm GSD.

Wks 3–5
iii.

The Hand Drawing

Every elevation, plan, and section redrawn at 1:50 in graphite on Canson 200 g/m². Crack maps, mortar maps, intervention maps as overlays.

Wks 6–11
iv.

Diagnosis

Petrographic samples to the laboratory at LRMH, Champs-sur-Marne. Mortar analyses; salt content; biological colonisation. Structural review.

Wks 12–13
v.

The Report

Bound dossier — roughly 180 pages — lodged with DRAC, the diocese, and our archive. Recommendations always offered against the null option.

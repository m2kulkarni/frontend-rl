The Practice

பணிமனை — workshop, lineage, and the long apprenticeship of form
Sthapati Workshop has, since 2009, kept rooms above a mango-merchant's godown on East Masi Street, two streets north of the Meenakshi gopuram. The studio was founded as a partnership between an architect-sthapati and a press-trained graphic designer; its work moves between temple commissions, contemporary residences, gallery design, and printed matter on craft.

The practice is small by intent — two principals, six full-time associates, and a roving panel of master-craftsmen who join projects as the brief demands. We accept roughly four architectural commissions and three publications a year, and we keep a single physical workshop where stone, plaster, and proof-press all share the same long monsoon-shuttered hall.

What follows is a literal account of where the practice came from and the three rules by which we measure a drawing before it leaves the studio. It is not a manifesto. It is the description we send to clients who ask, reasonably, what they should expect.

Lineage

How the Studio Came to Be

M. Arumugam was born in 1974 in the temple town of Mahabalipuram, where his grandfather Sthapati K. Murugesan kept a working yard a hundred and twenty paces from the Shore Temple. Murugesan-thatha had himself trained under V. Ganapati Sthapati's father and belonged to what the family still calls, with some pride and some pedantry, the Mahabalipuram school — the line that holds Manasara and Mayamata in the same hand as a chisel. Arumugam apprenticed with him from the age of nine. He carved his first kumbha (pot-finial) in 1986 and his first complete dvarapala in 1991.

In 1993 he sat the entrance examination for CEPT Ahmedabad, and over the next five years read architecture under Kurula Varkey and Riyaz Tayyibji. The mismatch — between a sthapati's plumb-line and a studio crit — was its own education. He wrote his thesis on the proportional system of the Tirumalai Nayak Mahal, and graduated in 1998. Three years with Brinda Somaya in Mumbai followed, then two with Mohammad Shaheer on landscape projects in Delhi and Sri Lanka. He returned to Madurai in 2007 to consult on the conservation of a Chettinad mansion at Kanadukathan; he never quite left.

Lakshmi Raghavan was born in 1979 in Pondicherry. She read graphic design at NID Ahmedabad between 1999 and 2003, where she specialised in book typography. She spent four years at the Aurobindo Press, setting and printing the press's revised editions of Savitri and the Letters on Yoga; she still keeps a Vandercook proof-press, hand-shipped from Pondicherry, in the studio's back room. In 2008 she joined Tara Books in Chennai for two seasons, and was the designer-of-record on the Gond folio The Night Life of Trees.

She and Arumugam met in 2008 at a printing seminar in Sivakasi, on the question of whether a temple-craft folio could be set in twelve-point Lato without losing its temper. The argument went on for three years. In 2011 they took the first floor above C. Subbiah & Sons, Mango Merchants, and registered Sthapati Workshop as a partnership firm under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932.

The studio's first commission was a small kalyana mandapam for a family in Karaikudi — a roofed open hall, sixty feet by thirty, with twelve granite pillars and a coffered timber ceiling. It was finished in 2013 for ₹46 lakh including stonework. It still stands, and is still in use; the family rents it out for weddings at ₹85,000 a day. We visit it twice a year to check the joinery.

Since then the practice has grown to its current size and no further. We have turned down two offers to open a Bangalore office and one to open a Singapore one. The work we are interested in cannot be done from far away: it requires that someone walk to the stone-yard on a Wednesday afternoon to argue about the depth of a fillet.

The Three Principles

Tala · Ornament · Color · Light

தளம் · அலங்காரம் · நிறம் · ஒளி — the order in which we draw
I.

Tala Before
Ornament

Proportion is settled before any moulding is permitted to enter the drawing. We work in tala-units (the standing measure of the principal figure or column) and resolve the bay, the storey, and the silhouette in plain line. If the building is wrong at this stage, no ornament will rescue it; if it is right, ornament becomes a service rather than a disguise. We hold this rule even on print — the grid is set before the rule, the rule before the flourish.

தளம் முதல்
II.

Ornament Before
Color

Ornament is drawn, carved and sized in monochrome before any pigment is approved. Where a kolam, a palmette, or a kapota cornice is to appear, it is first proved in graphite or slip-cast plaster at full size on the wall. Only after the relief reads correctly under raking light does the studio admit color. This is the rule that stops a building from becoming a poster of itself.

அலங்காரம் முன்
III.

Color Before
Light

Color is fixed before the lighting plan is drawn. Pigments are chosen for how they read under the actual sun of the actual site — Madurai's noon is not Bangalore's noon — and the fixtures are then designed to defer to those colors, not to overwrite them. Electric light, in our work, is asked to do the modest work of letting the day's color survive into the evening. It is never asked to invent a mood.

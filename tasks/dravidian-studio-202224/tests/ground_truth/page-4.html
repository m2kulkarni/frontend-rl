Workshop & Crafts

Three rooms, three rhythms, one inheritance. The granite yard at Pillaiyarpatti, the colour studio on West Masi Street, and the digital atelier above the courtyard all answer to the same temple plan — only the medium changes.

Three Rooms, One Plan

Stone Yard

Twelve kilometres east of Madurai, behind the Karpaga Vinayagar temple at Pillaiyarpatti, our yard receives raw black granite (karunkal) from quarries at Kanjamalai. Blocks are roughed out by four masons under Sthapati K. Muthukumarasamy.

Open Mon–Sat · 06:00–13:00

Colour Studio

Above the studio courtyard on West Masi Street, three muller-tables grind oxide and earth pigments into linseed and shellac binders. We follow Vijayanagara-Nayaka recipes catalogued by V. Ranganathan in his 1974 monograph for the Madurai Kamaraj University.

By appointment · ₹600 site fee

Digital Atelier

A converted thinnai loft, six workstations, two plotters. Mandala plans are reconstructed in Grasshopper from the Mayamata and Mānasāra texts, then output as 1:50 templates for the masons below.

Internal use · 24-hour access

Quarry

Kanjamalai · Pillaiyarpatti Yard

The block is chosen at dawn, when the angle of light reveals the grain (nāḷam) running through the stone. A 1.8-tonne column shaft for the Trichy gopuram restoration takes nine days to rough-cut by hand: feathers and wedges driven into a chalk line, then split clean along the bedding plane.

We refuse machine-sawn faces for any visible surface. The slight unevenness of a hand-split face holds shadow the way a polished slab cannot.

Splitting Sequence — Feather & Wedge

“The stone tells you where to strike. We only listen — the wedge does the speaking.”

K. Muthukumarasamy · Master Mason, 41 years

— ०२ · Essay Two —

Carving

Pillaiyarpatti Carving Shed

From rough block to finished image is a journey of seven tools and an average of 280 working hours for a half-life-size figure. The progression — uli, kuruvāḷ, sīṟi, vaṭṭam, varai, polishing-bone, beeswax — is fixed by guild custom.

Each carver works to a chalked grid taken directly from the Mānasāra's nine-tāla canon: head, neck, chest, navel, knee, ankle, all proportioned to the same fundamental unit drawn from the figure's own face.

Nine-Tāla Proportional Grid

“The chisel removes nothing. It simply shows what was already inside the stone.”

R. Selvi · Carver, Lead Sculptor

— ०३ · Essay Three —

Polychrome

Colour Studio · West Masi Street

Six pigments form the basis of the studio's palette: vermilion (iṅgilikam) from cinnabar imported via Tuticorin; yellow ochre from Sittannavasal; lamp-black from sesame-oil soot; lapis-derived peacock-blue; gold-leaf from Vellore; and the chalk-white ground (geru) made from local kaolin.

Pigments are mulled by hand on Cuddapah-stone slabs for forty-five minutes per batch. Linseed oil is cold-pressed in Sivaganga; shellac flakes are sourced from Jhalda. No commercial binders enter the studio.

Studio Palette · 6 Pigments

“A vermilion that has been mulled for twenty minutes is a different colour from one mulled for forty-five. The eye knows, even when the mind cannot say why.”

Lakshmi Subbaraman · Colourist & Pigment Master

— ०४ · Essay Four —

Kolam

Digital Atelier · Sthapati Loft

The kolam — the daily threshold-drawing of Tamil households — is, structurally, a parametric system. A grid of puḷḷi (dots) is set out, then a continuous line is drawn looping each dot exactly once. The studio uses these rules to generate large-scale floor plans for exhibition pavilions and temple-precinct paving.

Our 2023 pavilion at the Chettinad Heritage Festival used a 21×21 sikku-kolam grid expanded to 14 metres, hand-laid in white granite chip set into red oxide.

Sikku-Kolam · 7×7 Generative Grid

“The kolam is a programme. The hand executes it, but the rule is older than any of us — older even than the threshold it crosses.”

Padma Anantharaman · Kolam Designer & Geometer

On Apprenticeship

— Pārampariyam · Through the Hand —

The studio takes two trainees per year, selected through the Tamil Nadu State Craft Council's annual examination held each March at the Government Museum, Chennai. Candidates must be between 18 and 26 years of age and demonstrate basic competence in either freehand drawing, stone-tool use, or pigment-grinding.

Apprentices receive a monthly stipend of ₹18,500, full board at our Madurai residence, and rotate through all three workshops over a 24-month cycle. They graduate with a stone or polychrome panel of their own design, exhibited at the studio's January open-house.

The next intake closes on 15 February 2025. Application forms are available at the council office, Egmore, or by post on request.

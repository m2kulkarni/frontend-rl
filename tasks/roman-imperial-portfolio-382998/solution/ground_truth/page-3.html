Villa Cassia

A writer's house on the Janiculum — four hundred and eighty square metres of travertine, lime, and disciplined shadow, completed for the Cassia family in the autumn of MMXXII.

I · Programme

A Writer's House

Villa Cassia was commissioned in the spring of 2019 by Marco and Livia Cassia — he a translator of late antique poetry, she an editor at the Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale — as a permanent residence and working library for a household of two adults, one adolescent daughter, and an irregular but devoted procession of visiting scholars.

The brief, drafted across seven evenings at the family's apartment in Trastevere, asked for four conditions: a room dense enough with books to muffle the city; a courtyard open enough to admit weather without inviting it indoors; a kitchen in which a long table could be set for nine; and a threshold from which the Tiber, or at least its idea, could still be read. The whole was to be sufficient for thirty years of writing, and to be unembarrassed by the Janiculum's older neighbours.

The total programme runs to 480 m² on two storeys plus a half-buried service plinth, and was delivered for a construction cost of €2,140 per square metre — costly for the materials, frugal for the patience.

II · Site

Olive Grove on the Janiculum

The plot, 1,840 m² in extent, descends in three loose terraces from a service lane off the Via di San Pancrazio toward the eastern lip of the hill. The upper terrace held seventeen olives of perhaps a century's age; eleven were preserved, two transplanted, and four — already in slow decline — felled and milled into thresholds and library shelving. A small cisterna dating to the late nineteenth century, brick-vaulted and bone-dry, was retained beneath the new kitchen.

From the upper garden the Tiber is not directly visible — Rome's chestnut canopy will not allow it — but a single oblique sight-line was negotiated by the cutting of one bay in the eastern garden wall. Through this aperture, framed by lime-rendered jambs, one sees the river only as light: a band of pale movement at the base of the city.

III · Plan

Cross-Axial Disposition

The plan resolves to two intersecting axes. The north–south axis carries the visitor: from the street through a narrow fauces, into a tetrastyle atrium with an impluvium of black Belgian limestone, across the tablinum-library, and out to a rear peristyle garden whose far wall is pierced by the Tiber aperture described above. The east–west axis is the household's own: kitchen and dining hall to the west; bedrooms, bath, and a small private oratory to the east.

The atrium is roofed by four Tuscan columns of unfluted Tivoli travertine, 3.20 m on centre. The peristyle garden carries six columns of the same order at a tighter rhythm, framing four mature pomegranates and a single laurel grafted from a tree at Hadrian's villa in Tivoli.

V · Materials

Stone, Lime, Pigment

Materials were specified to a strict economy of four. Tivoli travertine from the Cave Bernardini quarry — selected for its dense, even bedding and its resistance to the hill's winter rain — forms columns, lintels, thresholds, and the impluvium kerb. The principal floors are opus signinum, mixed on site at a 3:1 lime-to-crushed-terracotta ratio and finished with a marble-dust burnish. Internal walls are lime-washed plaster over coarse arriccio, kept the colour of clean linen throughout — except the western wall of the library, which is rendered in lime-washed stucco tinted to a deep Pompeian red after a fragment recovered at the Villa of the Mysteries. Joinery is in fumed Roman oak, drawn from the four felled olives and from a single windfallen oak from the Castelli.

VI · Closing Note

Otivm cvm Dignitate

Cut by hand into a single course of Tivoli travertine above the principal door, the inscription is borrowed from Cicero's Pro Sestio. It was the only ornament the Cassia family asked for by name. Three letters deep, gilded only in the first O, it is meant — like the house — to be unhurried.

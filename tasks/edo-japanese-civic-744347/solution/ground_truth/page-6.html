A Note on Our Origins

Being a faithful account of the Bureau's establishment under the early Tokugawa, its reorganization after the conflagration of Meireki, and the lineage of its commissioners through the eighteenth century.

Chapter 一 — Foundation

Establishment under the Early Tokugawa

Genna ・ Kan'ei ・ 1618 — 1631

The office now styled the Bureau of Wards & Watercourses (Machikata Suidō Bugyōsho) traces its first warrant to the fourth year of Genna (1618), when the second shōgun, Tokugawa Hidetada, appointed Itakura Katsushige as the senior commissioner for the eastern wards of the new capital. At its founding the bureau supervised but thirty-six machi south of Nihonbashi; within a decade its remit had grown to include the conduits drawing water from the Kanda and Tama uplands.

The first formal regulations — the Machi-bure jūrokkajō, the Sixteen Articles of the Wards — were promulgated in Kan'ei eight (1631). They fixed the hours of the gates, the fees for night-watchmen (three mon per household per month), and the duty of every chōnin to keep a barrel of standing water against the threat of fire.

From the outset the bureau sat under the dual authority of the rōjū council and the city magistrate — an arrangement preserved, with minor revisions, until the present day.

Chapter 二 — Conflagration

Reorganization after the Meireki Fire

Meireki San-nen ・ 1657

On the eighteenth day of the first month of Meireki three (1657), the great fire that began at the Honmyō-ji in Hongō consumed sixty per centum of the city, including the donjon of the castle and the offices of the bureau itself. More than one hundred thousand souls perished. The records of the wards, kept on cedar shelves at Tokiwabashi, were lost entire.

In the rebuilding that followed, the bureau was reorganized under the joint direction of Hōjō Awa-no-kami Ujinaga, the surveyor of the new street-plan, and Ishitani Sadakiyo, the second machi-bugyō of the south office. The reforms of Manji two (1659) divided the city into the kita-machi and minami-machi jurisdictions, each with its own commissioner serving in monthly rotation — a practice termed tsukiban.

The bureau was rebuilt in masonry foundation at its present site at Gofukubashi-uchi, with broader fire-breaks (hirokōji) cleared at Sujikai, Hatchōbori, and Asakusa. The first dedicated hikeshi stations were chartered in this same period; their register is preserved on the page of Fire-Watch Towers.

“Let the wards be numbered, the conduits surveyed, and the watch-bells answer one to another — that no district shall, by night, lie unaccounted for.” — Machi-bure Jūrokkajō, Article I, Kan'ei 8
Chapter 三 — Lineage

Lineage of the Commissioners

Machi-bugyō ・ Eighteenth Century

What follows is the succession of the senior commissioners of the south office (minami-machi-bugyō) through the long period of administrative consolidation. Their stipends were fixed at three thousand koku, with an additional office-allowance of fifteen hundred ryō per annum for the maintenance of the bureau and its forty-two clerks.

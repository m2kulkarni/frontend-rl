Concrete & Oculus

The Pantheon Reconsidered
XIV·MAR — XXVIII·VI · MMXXV
Co-curated with the Soprintendenza Speciale di Roma

An Argument in Stone, Lime & Air

For seventeen hundred years the Pantheon's coffered hemisphere has stood without scaffolding, ribbing, or steel. Concrete & Oculus assembles, for the first time outside the Soprintendenza's working archive, twenty-eight original Hadrianic core samples, four 19th-century survey ledgers, and a 1:25 sectional model fabricated in the Museo's workshops, to ask the simplest of questions: how does a building made of mortar continue to refuse gravity?

The exhibition occupies the Hall of the Pediment and the adjoining Sala Aureliana on the museum's upper floor. Visitors are guided, in three rooms, from the quarry to the rotunda to the surveyor's notebook — and finally to the oculus itself, rendered at full diameter on the basilica's travertine floor.

Curators · F. Calzoni & M. Hadjipetrou · with C. Vergari, Soprintendenza Speciale di Roma
— I —

Hadrianic Concrete Formulae

The mortar that lifts the dome is not one mortar but five. Hadrian's engineers — almost certainly working from drawings prepared under Apollodorus a generation earlier — graded their opus caementicium by altitude. The lower drum, bearing the greatest compressive load, was packed with travertine and tufa rubble bound in a heavy lime-pozzolana paste drawn from the Pozzuoli beds south of the Bay of Naples.

Above the springing line, the aggregate lightens course by course. Brick and broken terracotta replace the travertine; nearer the crown, the masons turned to porous yellow tufo and finally to small lumps of pumice ferried, the inscriptions tell us, from the Aeolian Islands. The result is a graded shell whose density falls almost in lockstep with the structural demand placed upon it.

What the Soprintendenza's recent campaign has confirmed — and what this exhibition documents through cross-section and chemical assay — is the consistency of the volcanic ash itself. Specimens drawn in 2019 from the third register show the same characteristic phillipsite and Al-tobermorite signatures identified in earlier studies, the very minerals to which the mortar owes its long, slow, self-healing cure.

Two centuries of restoration mortars, layered against the original, are presented here side by side. The contrast — Hadrianic core, Renaissance patch, Settecento repair — is the exhibition's quietest and most eloquent display.

“Caementa quae in fornice ponuntur, quanto altius surgunt, tanto leviora esse oportet.” — Vitruvius, De Architectura, II.viii (cited on the room-I lintel)
— II —

The Geometry of the 43.3-Metre Dome

Hadrian's geometers gave the rotunda a single ratio: the diameter of the dome is exactly the height from the floor to the underside of the oculus. A perfect sphere can be inscribed within the volume — a fact the Renaissance rediscovered and which Serlio drew, with rather less precision, in 1540.

The five rings of coffers are not, however, a simple division of the hemisphere. Their proportions diminish toward the apex more rapidly than perspective alone would dictate, lightening both the apparent weight of the shell and its actual load.

— III —

Nineteenth-Century Engineering Surveys

The third gallery turns to the long century in which the Pantheon ceased to be merely a building and became a problem. From Pietro Bianchi's 1819 plumb-line survey onward, the rotunda was measured, prodded, and theorised about with an obsessive thoroughness — chiefly because its cracks, long known, were now visible to a public increasingly anxious about the antique.

Bianchi's notebooks, lent for the first time by the Accademia di San Luca, are displayed open at the page on which he records, with admirable understatement, that "the meridian fissure measured at the springing has not appreciably widened since the survey of MDCCLXXXIII."

Beside Bianchi we have placed the 1892 report of the engineer Luigi Cremona, whose photoelastic studies of the drum — performed with mirrors, plumb-lines, and the patience of an entire winter — first quantified what the masons had grasped intuitively: the dome's thrust is conducted not into the drum's mass but through the eight relieving arches concealed within it.

The exhibition closes with three folios from the survey conducted in 1934 by the Genio Civile, included here for the contrast they offer: drawings made by men who already had reinforced concrete in their hands, looking at a building that had no need of it.

“La cupola non grava: si sostiene da sé, come una cosa pensata.” — Pietro Bianchi, Relazione al Cardinal Consalvi, MDCCCXIX

From a private senatorial bequest to a civic institution of European standing — one hundred and thirty-six years in the service of Roman antiquity.

Origin & Ambition

A Senator's Bequest · A Republic's Inheritance

The Museo Aureliano traces its existence to the will of Senator Marco Aureliano Tessari, lodged with the prefecture of Rome on the seventeenth of March, eighteen hundred and eighty-nine. Tessari, a jurist of the second Depretis administration and an avid student of epigraphy, directed that his collection of inscriptions, votive altars, and Severan portrait heads — assembled over four decades from sites across Lazio and Campania — be vested in the City "for the instruction of citizens and the dignity of the Republic."

The bequest comprised four hundred and eleven objects, a library of three thousand volumes, and a working endowment of one hundred twenty thousand lire. Its sole condition was that the collection remain undivided and freely accessible on the calends of each month. That stipulation has been honoured without interruption for one hundred and thirty-six years.

For three decades the holdings were displayed in the Palazzo Tessari on the Via dei Genovesi, under the curatorship of the philologist Giulia Maranesi. The First World War strained the modest endowment, and by 1921 the trustees had begun to seek a permanent civic seat. Their attention turned, with the encouragement of the Soprintendenza, to the disused vault halls of the former Basilica di San Prisco on the Aventine — a sixteenth-century travertine structure raised over a Hadrianic insula and deconsecrated in 1873.

The conversion, supervised by the architect Pietro Calandra and completed in the autumn of 1923, retained the basilican plan, the apsidal niches, and the original travertine flagstones, while inserting a top-lit clerestory and a continuous epigraphic gallery along the south aisle. The Museo Aureliano opened to the public on the eleventh of November of that year.

Three Chapters

Bequest · Conversion · Reconstruction

The Bequest

A.D. MDCCCLXXXIX

Tessari's collection had grown from a single Augustan altar — purchased at auction in Tivoli in 1851 — into one of the most coherent epigraphic holdings in private hands south of the Alps. The senator catalogued every piece himself in a series of fourteen ledgers, now CAT-T-1/14 in the museum archive. His instruction that the collection remain "indivisible, public, and silent in the late afternoon" still governs gallery conduct after sixteen-thirty.

The Conversion

A.D. MCMXXIII

Pietro Calandra's intervention upon the Basilica di San Prisco was, by the standards of his contemporaries, austere. He removed only the seventeenth-century stucco and the Baroque altar; he preserved the apse, the travertine flooring, and the Hadrianic vault discovered beneath the nave during groundwork in 1922. The resulting plan — a continuous epigraphic gallery flanking a central sculpture hall — has been emulated at Verona, at Aquileia, and most recently at the Museo della Via Latina.

The Reconstruction

A.D. MCMLVIII

An incendiary strike on the south aisle, 4 March 1944, destroyed seventeen objects and damaged the clerestory. Renata Volpi — appointed director in 1949 — secured ten million lire from the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno and oversaw a thirteen-year restoration, completed on 11 November 1958, the thirty-fifth anniversary of the museum's opening. Her careful re-installation of the Severan portrait gallery remains substantially as visitors encounter it today.

Direction & Council

In Office Since MMXIX

The present director, Dott. Cesare Mancini, was appointed by the Consiglio Comunale on the fourteenth of June, two thousand and nineteen, succeeding Prof.ssa Adriana Lombardi (in office 2004–2019). A graduate of La Sapienza and the École française de Rome, Mancini directed the excavations at Ostia Antica between 2008 and 2017 and is the author of Iscrizioni dimenticate del Lazio meridionale (Quasar, 2015).

Under his direction the museum has restored the lower cryptoporticus, established a residency programme for early-career epigraphers, and completed the digitisation of the Tessari ledgers — a project funded in part by the Fondazione Roma and concluded in February of this year.

The curatorial council comprises eleven members appointed for renewable five-year terms: four serving curators of the museum, three external scholars elected by the Accademia dei Lincei, two municipal delegates, the President of the Soprintendenza Speciale di Roma ex officio, and one representative of the heirs of the Tessari estate.

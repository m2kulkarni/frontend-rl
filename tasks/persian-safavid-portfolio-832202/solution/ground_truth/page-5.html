A Biography in Cut Tile

Darius Farahani

tile-cutter, ornamental designer, founder of the atelier

Darius Farahani works in a tradition that does not hurry. Each square metre of his haft-rang and mosaic-faïence panels is the record of perhaps a thousand small decisions — about angle, about glaze, about the patience of geometry.

Early Years & Training

Darius Farahani was born in Tehran in 1978, the third child of a family of bookbinders whose shop sat at the edge of the Grand Bazaar. He has often said that his first instinct for pattern came not from the tiled walls of the city's mosques but from the gilded rule-borders his father pressed into the spines of books — small, exact, repeating things made by hand.

He left Tehran for Isfahan in 1998 and entered the Isfahan University of Art, taking the Bachelor of Fine Arts in Tilework in 2003. His undergraduate years were spent loosely within the circle of Ostad Mahmoud Farshchian, whose insistence on the slow study of vegetal scroll and palmette would shape Darius's later vocabulary. A 2002 thesis on the spandrel ornaments of the Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque — researched on foot, with a folding rule and a sketchbook — remains, in his own account, the foundation for everything that came after.

The Vakil Mosque, Shiraz

From 2004 to 2009, Darius was based in Shiraz as a junior — and later lead — restorer on the long conservation programme of the Vakil Mosque's Zand-period haft-rang panels. Five seasons on scaffolding, with brushes the width of a fingernail and a kiln two streets away, taught what no university can: that a tile is first an object and only afterwards an idea. The cracks of two and a half centuries do not negotiate.

He took from Shiraz a working method — quietly heretical inside Iranian conservation orthodoxy — of cutting replacement tesserae oversized and grinding them in place to the original tolerance, a habit borrowed from the wood-mosaicists of the city's marquetry workshops.

The Atelier, 2011 —

In the spring of 2011 he founded Atelier Naqsh-e Jahan — named, with affectionate hubris, for the great square of Isfahan — in a converted ice-house in the old quarter of Yazd. The atelier began with three workbenches and a single coal-fired kiln; it now employs four tile-cutters, two glaze-mixers, and a draughtsman, and accepts roughly eight commissions a year. The work divides, in his own description, into three streams: restoration (mosques, hammams, private houses on the Iranian conservation register), contemporary architectural ornament for new buildings willing to accept slow craft, and the small body of exhibited panels through which the atelier argues, publicly, for the seriousness of the discipline.

Cut tile is a slow argument with geometry. The kiln has its opinions, the clay has its own; one negotiates, again and again, until the pattern that was drawn at midnight in ink consents, by morning, to live in glaze.
— D. Farahani, Yazd, 2023

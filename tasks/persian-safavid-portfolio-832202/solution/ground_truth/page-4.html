Process · Method · Craft

Workshop & Method

The seven stages by which a cartoon becomes a wall — set down here as we practise them, in order, without shortcut.

A Note From the Master

What follows is the working method of this atelier, given in the order a panel is actually made. It is the same sequence used in the great Safavid workshops of the seventeenth century, adjusted only where modern kilns and modern lime have asked it of us. We make every piece by these seven stages; nothing skips.

I Tarh

The Cartoon

— ṭarḥ —

Every panel begins on linen-backed paper at 1:1 scale. Leila draws the geometry first in lead, building the underlying girih grid before any vine is permitted to enter the field. Only when the structure satisfies us does she ink the lines in cobalt and vermilion: cobalt for the contour each tile must follow, vermilion for the colour-zones that will later be assigned to the seven fired hues. A single spandrel cartoon for a small hammam takes between nine and fourteen days.

I Tarh
❦
II Pokhtan

Tile Firing

— pokhtan —

Reza fires our biscuit ware to 980° in the wood-fed downdraught kiln behind the courtyard, then glazes and refires the seven traditional colours separately to 940°: cobalt blue, turquoise, manganese aubergine, iron yellow, copper green, oxblood, and the white tin-opacified ground. Each colour is held in the kiln by a different schedule; turquoise alone wants eleven hours of cooling, or it crazes by the next morning. We accept roughly seven kiln-loads in ten as fit for cutting.

IIPokhtan
❦
IIIKhat-zadan

Scoring

— khaṭ-zadan —

The fired tiles are laid glaze-up on a felt block and the cartoon's shapes are transferred onto the glaze with a tungsten-tipped scribe. We use the cartoon itself as a template, pricked along its lines so that powdered charcoal will pass through and leave a faint dotted trace. A correct score is a single confident line; hesitation breaks the glaze and ruins the piece. Darius does the scoring on every panel that leaves the atelier.

IIIKhat
❦
IVAnbar

Nipping

— anbar-zadan —

The scored tile is broken to its outline with parrot-beak nippers, working from the perimeter inward, taking a millimetre at a time. A six-pointed star from the Ardestan commission requires roughly seventy nipping passes to bring within tolerance. The cut edge must bevel slightly inward, so that when the piece is later set face-down the plaster keys behind it. Yasmin trains on offcut squares for the better part of a year before nipping a finished colour.

IVAnbar
❦
VChidan

Laying Face-Down on the Cartoon

— chīdan —

The cartoon is laid flat on the casting bench glaze-side up, and each nipped piece is placed face-down upon it, glaze meeting paper. This is the moment the design is in fact assembled, but in reverse and in mirror. We work outward from the central seal so that any final tolerance error is pushed to the perimeter, where it can be dressed. A panel of one square metre holds, on average, between four and six hundred individual pieces.

VChidan
❦
VIGach-rizi

The Plaster Pour

— gach-rīzī —

A timber dam is set around the assembled bed and lime-gypsum plaster, mixed with goat-hair fibre at roughly nineteen grammes per litre, is poured to a depth of forty millimetres. The pour must be done in one continuous motion: a stop produces a cold joint that will fail on the wall within five winters. The slab is left undisturbed for three days. We have not yet used Portland cement and we do not intend to.

VIGach
❦
VIIBardashtan

Lifting

— bardāshtan —

On the fourth morning the cartoon is dampened and peeled away from the cured plaster, and the panel — now a single rigid tile-mosaic slab — is turned for the first time onto its face. The glaze meets daylight in its true orientation, often for hours we have only imagined it. Edges are dressed, missed pieces re-set in fresh lime, and the panel is signed on the reverse with the year and the master's mark. Only then is it crated for the wall it was made for.

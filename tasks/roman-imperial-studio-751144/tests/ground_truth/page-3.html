The Studio · On Method & Principle

Liber tertius — an essay on lineage, doctrine, and the four columns of our practice.

Vitruvius, writing for the Emperor Augustus near the close of the first century before our era, set down in his ten books De Architectura a doctrine that has not in two thousand years been improved upon: that building is the proper marriage of firmitas, utilitas, venustas—soundness, usefulness, and beauty. From this seed our atelier draws its name, and from this seed our every drawing must be capable of being traced.

The lineage we claim is not invented. It runs from the workshops of Augustan Rome through the Renaissance treatises of Sebastiano Serlio, whose seven books on architecture, published between 1537 and 1575, restored to the Cinquecento a systematic vocabulary of orders, doors, and stage settings. From Serlio it passes to Andrea Palladio, whose Quattro Libri of 1570 distilled antique example into a measured, drawable canon, and whose villas of the Veneto remain the finest demonstration that classical principle is not a costume but a discipline.

What we received from these masters we have not modified, only translated for our century. The atelier teaches and practises that an architect is, before anything else, a draughtsman; that he is, after that, a reader of stones; and that he is, finally, the servant of a public room. The orders are not ornaments to be applied; they are arguments to be proved. A column engaged in a wall must answer for itself why it stands there, what it carries, and at what scale it speaks.

· The Four Working Principles ·

Within the studio at Via dei Coronari we have, since 2011, organised our work around four principles. They are written above the drawing room door in incised travertine, and every commission—whether the small chapel at Frascati or the new tribunal hall at Lecce—is reviewed against them before it leaves our hands.

QUATTUOR
CANONES
  1. Axial Planning Every plan begins at a single line drawn from threshold to apse. Rooms gather upon that line in symmetrical pairs; circulation does not wander but processes. A building without an axis is, to us, a building without a sentence.
  2. The Round Arch We hold with the Romans, and not with the Goths, that the semicircular arch is the truest resolution of weight in masonry. From Pantheon to Aqua Claudia, it is the figure that proves a wall has thought about its load.
  3. The Coffered Surface Vaults, soffits, and ceilings shall be coffered—divided into the recessed panels that lighten the apparent mass of the structure and offer to plaster, gilding, or rosette the disciplined field upon which ornament may speak without shouting.
  4. Restraint of Colour The fabric of a building is travertine, marble, brick, and lime. To these we add, sparingly, the three accents of the Empire: Pompeian red for friezes, gold for the meander and the rosette, Tyrian purple only for the seat of authority. Nothing else.

These four are not preferences. They are the conditions under which a drawing earns its title. A young architect entering our studio is given, on his first day, a measured plan of the Maison Carrée at Nîmes and a sheet of Fabriano paper; he draws it in pencil before he is permitted to draw anything of his own. We have found, after a dozen years, that nothing else teaches the eye so quickly the difference between a building that knows itself and a building that does not.

We do not refuse the present age. We refuse only the proposition that the present age has rendered the antique illegible. The materials remain; the geometry remains; the public, when honestly addressed in the old grammar, remains capable of understanding it. That is the wager of the atelier, and the work shown in these pages is its evidence.

Marcus Aurelius Vegetti

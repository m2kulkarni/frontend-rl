The Five Orders
A Working Reference

"Ordo est membrorum operis commoda compositio." — Vitruvius, De Architectura, I.ii.
Compiled in the studio for the use of draughtsmen, masons, and clients.

Quinque Ordines

As distinguished by Vitruvius, Vignola (1562), and the practice of this studio.
I Tvscan

Tuscanicus

SIMPLEX · ITALICVS · GRAVIS

The Tuscan is the gravest and plainest of the orders, native to Italy and described by Vitruvius as the order of fortifications, granaries, and the rural temple. Its column rises seven diameters; its shaft is unfluted; its capital and base are reduced to plain mouldings. There is no frieze ornament, no triglyph, no enrichment.

In the studio it is reserved for utilitarian fabric — rusticated arcades, market loggie, military gateways, and the lower storey of substantial palazzi where the upper orders shall be richer.

Module1 diameter (D) at base
Column height7 D
Entablature1¾ D, plain architrave
ShaftUnfluted; ¼ D diminution
CapitalAbacus, ovolo, neck
On Use Tuscan: rural villas, fortified outworks, basements of palaces, and any fabric where strength must read before refinement. Never in sacred interiors above the ground course.
II Doric

Doricus

CIVILIS · MARTIALIS · VIRILIS

The Doric is the order of civic virtue and arms. Vitruvius assigns it to the temples of Mars, Hercules, and Minerva — divinities of strength — and the studio follows him. Its column is eight diameters, its shaft fluted with twenty channels, its frieze ordered by triglyph and metope.

It is the order of the basilica, the senate-house, the forum portico, the magistrate's loggia, and the war memorial. It bears, but does not flatter; it announces, but does not seduce.

Module½ diameter (½D)
Column height8 D
Entablature2 D; triglyph & metope
Flutes20, sharp-arrised
CapitalEchinus, abacus, annulets
On Use Doric: civic and martial buildings; senatorial halls, courthouses, regimental chapels, museum porticoes, and tombs of soldiers. Never in domestic interiors, never in bedrooms or in feminine apartments.
III Ionic

Ionicus

MATRONALIS · LITTERATVS · MITIS

The Ionic, born among the cities of Asia, mediates between the gravity of the Doric and the delicacy of the Corinthian. Its volute is its signature: the curl of a parchment, the coil of a lady's hair. It is the order of letters, of the matron, of the temples of Juno, Diana, and Bacchus. The shaft is nine diameters tall, fluted with twenty-four channels separated by fillets.

It belongs to libraries, academies, courts of equity, theatres, and the dwellings of the cultivated. It is also the proper order for the principal storey of a domus when set above a Doric ground.

Module½ diameter (½D)
Column height9 D
Entablature2¼ D; with dentil course
Flutes24, with fillets
CapitalVolute, ovolo, abacus
On Use Ionic: libraries, law-courts, theatres, music-rooms, the noble floor of palazzi, and the temples of learned and matronly divinities. Suited to interiors of refinement; admissible in domestic apartments of state.
IV Corinthian

Corinthius

VIRGINEVS · FLORIDVS · TRIVMPHALIS

The Corinthian is the maiden among the orders. Vitruvius records its invention by Callimachus, who saw acanthus leaves rising about a votive basket on a young girl's tomb. Its column is ten diameters; its capital, a bell wrapped in two tiers of acanthus crowned by volutes and a fleuron at the abacus.

It is the order of triumph and of the imperial sacred — the Pantheon, the Maison Carrée, the Temple of Castor and Pollux. The studio assigns it to all buildings of representation, and to the principal storey of every public façade where dignity must be joined to delight.

Module½ diameter (½D)
Column height10 D
Entablature2½ D; modillions, dentils
Capital2 tiers acanthus, volutes, fleuron
Flutes24, with fillets
On Use Corinthian: temples of Venus, Flora, Proserpine; triumphal arches, basilicas of state, opera-houses, the principal saloons of palaces, and any building dedicated to virgin or imperial divinity.

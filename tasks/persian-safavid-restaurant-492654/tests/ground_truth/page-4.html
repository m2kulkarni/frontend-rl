I. The Apprentice of Kashan

From a saffron tin in Naranjestan

Darius Mehrabani was nine years old the first time he ground saffron threads with sugar in his grandmother Goli's marble mortar. The kitchen sat at the back of a courtyard house in the Naranjestan quarter of Kashan, where the rosewater carts still came at dawn and the bread oven stayed warm from one Friday to the next.

His grandmother cooked from a recipe book that wasn't a book at all — a stack of loose pages copied in nastaliq from her mother's mother, with annotations in three coloured inks. The dishes were Safavid-era court formulas: stewed quince with split peas, lamb shank simmered in pomegranate molasses, the long-rice tah-chin layered with yogurt and barberries.

Darius left Kashan in 2007 for London, but he took the marble mortar with him. It now sits, still chipped at one rim, on the pass at Naqsh-e Jahan.

II. Clerkenwell & the Long Discipline

Twelve years at Moro

At Moro on Exmouth Market, under Sam & Sam Clark, Darius learned what his grandmother had never named: the discipline of restraint. He worked the wood-fired oven station from 2008 through 2012, then ran the pass for seven years more.

It was at Moro that he first cooked Persian food for strangers — a ten-course tasting in the spring of 2014 built entirely around saffron, sumac, and a single lamb. The menu we serve today on the chef's table descends in straight line from that night.

He left London on the 14th of March, 2018, with a folder of recipes and a verbal commitment from a tile-setter in Yazd named Reza Kamali.

The Vault Reza Built

Fourteen Months ◆ 41,200 Tiles ◆ One Dome
The Source

The dining room sits beneath a tiled vault drawn from the inner dome of the Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, completed in Isfahan in 1619 under the master architect Muhammad Reza ibn Ustad Hosein Banna Isfahani.

Where the original is twenty-three metres across, ours is seven. The geometry is faithful: a sixteen-pointed star pierced by lemon-shaped lozenges, opening from a central oculus of gilded ceramic.

The Maker

Reza Kamali, born 1962 in Yazd, came to us through a friend in Tehran. He spoke no English. He stayed in a flat in New Cross for fourteen months and returned to Yazd in May 2019 with three apprentices he had trained on site.

The Inner DomeAfter Sheikh Lotfollah, 1619
The Palette

Cobalt fired at 980°C, turquoise at 920°C, ivory plaster left to breathe between the tile-fields. The gold is ceramic, never leaf — it will outlast us. The whole interior is washed in candlelight from sundown.

The Tile-Count

41,200 hand-cut pieces. Of these, 1,612 are the gold-glazed star-tiles that mark the eight cardinal points of the vault — one for each year of the Safavid dynasty's high reign under Shah Abbas I.

The smallest tile in the room measures 9 millimetres on its longest side. It sits at the apex.

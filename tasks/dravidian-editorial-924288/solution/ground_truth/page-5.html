From Our Readers

The Margazhi Letters

Six letters received between October and November 2024, on the dating of the Airavatesvara ceiling, the contested vocabulary of "Dravida," and the engineering memory of the Tanjore drains.

To Our Correspondents

The Margazhi season has, as ever, been generous with its post. We received one hundred and fourteen letters in response to Issue XI (Aippasi 2024); printed below are six which struck the editors as essential — for their corrections, their dissents, and in one case for the long working memory of a hand that has spent forty years among the Kaveri's flood-gates.

Letters have been edited only for length. Postmarks and dates of writing are preserved. Replies, where given, are by the Editor and run in peacock blue.

  1. Letter I · On the Airavatesvara Ceiling Coimbatore · 14 October 2024

    The Mandapa Ceiling Is Twelfth-Century, Not Sixteenth

    From Dr. K. Padmavathy, retired Reader in Indian Art History, Bharathiar University

    Sir — In Mr. Vaidyanathan's otherwise admirable essay on the Rajagambhira mandapa at Darasuram ("The Chariot That Does Not Move," Issue XI, pp. 42–61), there is a dating I cannot allow to pass uncorrected. The painted lotus-medallion ceiling above the eastern aisle is given there as "a sixteenth-century Nayaka overlay." This is, I am afraid, an old error inherited from Sivaramamurti's 1952 monograph, and one that the 2009 ASI conservation report at Tiruchirappalli circle has already revised.

    The pigment analysis carried out by the National Research Laboratory for the Conservation of Cultural Property in February 2008 found three superimposed layers, the lowest of which contains lapis lazuli ground in a casein medium and is stratigraphically continuous with the consecration plaster. Rajaraja II's reign is not in question. The ceiling is contemporary with the temple, c. 1166 CE, and the so-called "Nayaka brightness" is in fact a second campaign of c. 1340, not 1560.

    I write only because the misdating has now been repeated so often in the popular literature that it has begun to appear in undergraduate examination scripts as fact. Your journal has the standing to correct it.

    Yours faithfully,
    K. Padmavathy
    R.S. Puram, Coimbatore 641 002

    Dr. Padmavathy is right and we are grateful. Mr. Vaidyanathan, on being shown the letter, accepts the correction without reservation, and observes only that he had been reading the 1952 figure into the 2009 plates without consulting the laboratory appendices — a sin he proposes to atone for in our Panguni number, with a fuller note on the casein medium and what its survival in the Kaveri delta tells us about temperatures inside a closed mandapa across eight centuries.

    — Ed.

  2. Letter II · On Vocabulary Pondicherry · 22 October 2024

    "Dravida" Is a Building Term, "Tamil" Is Not

    From Prof. R. Selvakumar, École française d'Extrême-Orient (associate)

    Sir — Your Issue XI editorial proposed, almost in passing, that the time had come to substitute "Tamil temple architecture" for the Sanskrit-derived "Dravida" in our running heads, on the grounds that the latter is "a colonial taxonomic residue." I beg leave to differ, and to differ at some length.

    "Dravida" in the Manasara, the Mayamata, and the Kashyapa Shilpa is not an ethnic or linguistic label. It is a structural one. It denotes a vimana whose superstructure is a stepped pyramid of articulated talas, each tala carrying its parapet of kuta, sala and panjara aedicules, and culminating in an octagonal or circular sikhara. By that definition the temples at Pattadakal in northern Karnataka are Dravida; the early Pandyan rock-cut shrines at Kalugumalai are Dravida; and so, for that matter, are several twelfth-century Hoysala foundations whose patrons would have been baffled to find themselves enrolled under a "Tamil" rubric.

    To collapse a structural term into an ethnic one is to surrender a piece of working vocabulary that the silpins themselves used. We do not rename the Doric order "Peloponnesian" because the Dorians were a people; we keep the term because it describes a column. I would urge the Review to keep "Dravida" for the same reason, and to use "Tamil" only where the political or linguistic geography is itself the subject.

    Yours, in the interests of precision,
    R. Selvakumar
    Rue Suffren, Pondicherry 605 001

    Prof. Selvakumar's distinction is the right one, and the editorial overstated its case. We will continue to use "Dravida" as a structural term, reserving "Tamil" for matters of patronage, language and place. The two are not interchangeable, and our running heads will be amended from Issue XIII accordingly.

    — Ed.

  3. Letter III · On the Tanjore Drains Bangalore · 29 October 2024

    Forty Monsoons Inside the Brihadisvara: A Drainage Memoir

    From Sri V. Ramaswami Iyer, Executive Engineer (retd.), Tamil Nadu PWD, Cauvery Circle

    Sir — I served the Public Works Department from 1966 to 2004, and from 1981 onward my charge included the Brihadisvara at Thanjavur during the north-east monsoon. Your gazetteer note of last issue describes the prakara drains as "a graceful Chola conceit"; with respect, they are nothing of the kind. They are a working hydraulic system, and one that I had occasion to clean, measure and on three occasions defend from well-meaning but disastrous repair.

    The temple sits on a podium some 3.5 metres above the surrounding plain, and the prakara is not flat: it falls about 90 mm from the south-west corner toward the north-east, where the principal cloacal sluice discharges into what was once a feeder channel of the Vadavar. The slope is not visible to the eye and was not, I believe, recorded in any nineteenth-century drawing. I measured it myself in October 1987 with a dumpy level borrowed from the Mannargudi sub-division because we could not understand why the south-west corner remained dry in a cyclone while the east mandapa flooded within twenty minutes.

    The granite paving slabs of the prakara are bedded on a 200 mm course of fine river sand over a puddled clay layer. Rainwater enters the joints — they were never meant to be sealed — passes laterally through the sand under hydraulic gradient, and exits through twenty-three dressed stone outlets at the base of the outer wall. In 1992 a contractor, on instructions I shall not name, pointed the joints with cement mortar across the entire south aisle. Within one monsoon the paving had heaved by 40 mm and two Nandi pavilions had cracked. We spent the better part of 1994 raking out the mortar by hand.

    I write because your gazetteer entry, charming as it is, will be read by people who make decisions about repair. Please tell them: do not point the joints. The Cholas did not forget the mortar. They left it out on purpose.

    With regards,
    V. Ramaswami Iyer
    Sadashivanagar, Bangalore 560 080

    This is the kind of letter an editor opens twice a decade. Sri Ramaswami Iyer has agreed, after some persuasion, to expand his account into a full essay for our Vaikasi number, with the level-survey diagrams from his 1987 notebooks. We shall print it under the title he has chosen himself: "The Cholas Did Not Forget the Mortar." Readers contemplating any sort of conservation pointing on a Chola prakara are asked, until then, to do nothing.

    — Ed.

  4. Letter IV · From the Diaspora Singapore · 4 November 2024

    The Sri Mariamman on South Bridge Road Is Not a Copy

    From Mrs. Lakshmi Pillai, Tanglin

    Sir — I have subscribed to the Review since its first number, in part because I had begun, in middle age, to feel a certain restlessness about the temple at the foot of South Bridge Road in which I was married in 1978. Mr. Krishnamurthy's Issue XI feature on diasporic gopuram-building has now answered a question I had not quite known how to ask.

    His footnote describing the 1925 Sri Mariamman gopuram as "a faithful but reduced copy of the Madurai type" is, I think, slightly unfair. The sthapati Naidu was working not from a copybook but from memory and from a single sheet of measured drawings he had brought from Cuddalore in 1903. The compression of the talas — five instead of the seven one would expect on a gopuram of that footprint — was not an error of reduction. It was a response to the colonial municipal height limit of 22.86 metres (75 feet) imposed on non-Christian religious buildings in the Chinatown ward.

    I learnt this from Naidu's grandson, who showed me the original ordinance in 1996. The Madurai type was not copied; it was negotiated with the municipality. That seems to me a different and more interesting story.

    With kind regards,
    Lakshmi Pillai
    Tanglin, Singapore 247 909

    Mrs. Pillai's correction is welcome and her point a serious one: the gopuram outside Tamil Nadu is almost always a negotiated object, and we have not done enough with the archives that survive in the city halls of Penang, Colombo, Durban and Port Louis. We hope to commission a longer survey on this in the course of 2025.

    — Ed.

  5. Letter V · A Brief Dissent Toronto · 11 November 2024

    Sacred Geometry, or Just Geometry?

    From Mr. A. Rajagopal, retired structural engineer

    Sir — Your column writer Mr. Subramanian, in his piece on the vastu-purusha-mandala (Issue XI, p. 78), describes the 8×8 padavinyasa as "encoding cosmological truths in metric form." I am sixty-eight, I designed bridges for thirty-one years for the Ontario Ministry of Transportation, and I would like to enter a small protest.

    The 8×8 grid is a perfectly good engineering convenience. It allows a master mason to set out a square plan, locate his load-bearing piers at the intersections of every second line, and divide his elevations by simple fractions without recourse to a ruler. That it also acquired, over the centuries, a cosmological gloss is a matter of cultural history. It is not a discovery about the universe.

    I do not begrudge the silpins their cosmology. I do begrudge the contemporary writer who treats every right angle in a Tamil temple as a coded message from the gods. The right angle is there because the wall would otherwise fall down.

    Sincerely,
    A. Rajagopal
    Don Mills, Toronto M3B 2T5

    Mr. Rajagopal's dissent is one we periodically need. The Review's position has always been that the cosmological reading and the engineering reading are not in competition: a grid that holds up a vimana and a grid that locates the deity's navel are, in this tradition, the same grid. But Mr. Subramanian has been reminded, gently, that the second reading must not be allowed to swallow the first.

    — Ed.

  6. Letter VI · A Question Edinburgh · 19 November 2024

    Where Do You Stand a Camera at Gangaikondacholapuram?

    From Ms. Catriona MacLeod, Lecturer in History of Art, University of Edinburgh

    Sir — I will be in the Kaveri delta for the first three weeks of February 2025 with a small group of postgraduate students, and we have permission to photograph at Gangaikondacholapuram for two mornings. May I ask the readership a simple, practical question: where, exactly, does one stand to obtain the elevation that Percy Brown reproduces opposite p. 116 of the 1959 edition?

    I have measured the photograph against the published plan and I cannot make the geometry work from any point inside the present compound. Either the boundary wall has been moved since 1942, or Brown was photographing from a ladder. I should be grateful for advice.

    With thanks in advance,
    Catriona MacLeod
    Old College, Edinburgh EH8 9YL

    Ms. MacLeod is observant. The compound wall has been moved — twice, in 1956 and again in 1978. The 1942 photograph was taken from a point now occupied by a forestry office. We have written separately to Ms. MacLeod with the coordinates of the nearest accessible vantage and the time of morning at which the eastern face of the vimana is unshadowed. Other readers in the delta in February who wish to make the same image are welcome to write to the editorial office for the same note.

    — Ed.

How to Write to Us

Letters on any matter raised in our pages are welcomed, particularly those that correct, contradict or extend. We give priority to writers with a working relationship to the buildings under discussion — silpins, conservators, engineers, archaeologists, priests.

Letters should not exceed eight hundred words. Postal correspondence is preferred; we still answer it.

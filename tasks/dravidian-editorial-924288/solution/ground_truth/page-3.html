Yali at the Threshold

A reading of the lion-elephant guardian capitals
of the Pudu Mandapam, Madurai

By R. Vaidehi Krishnamurthy

Stand at the eastern entrance of the Pudu Mandapam on a December morning, before the cloth merchants have rolled out their bolts, and the colonnade declares itself: one hundred and twenty-four piers in four files, each crowned by a creature that is half lion and half elephant, rearing on a crouching makara, its forepaw raised against an enemy who has long since stopped arriving.

The yali — also vyala, also sardula — is the most overworked and least studied figure in the south Indian repertoire. We catalogue gods. We measure plinths. We argue about shikhara proportions. But the guardian capitals at the threshold, the ones every visitor passes under and few ever look up at, have not been redrawn in a serious way since the Madras Survey of 1932, when a draughtsman named A. Rangaswami Iyengar spent eleven months in the mandapam with a plumb line, a folding rule, and a roll of tracing linen.

This installment of the Notebook reproduces eight of Iyengar's plates, with permission of the State Archives at Egmore, alongside our own photogrammetric overlays made in February of this year. What follows is not a survey but a reading — a slow walk down the western aisle, pier 47 through pier 62, in which the iconography is allowed to speak before the metrology arrives.

I. The Posture of the Threshold

Tirumalai Nayak's masons, working between 1626 and 1635 under the master Muthiah Sthapati, gave the Pudu Mandapam yalis a posture that is unusual even by the standards of the late Vijayanagara workshops at Vellore and Tadpatri. The forepaw is raised — common enough — but the head is turned outward, away from the worshipper, toward the street. The yali is not protecting the sanctum from us; it is protecting us from whatever is out there.

The block from which each capital is cut measures, on average, 2 cubits 4 angulas wide by 3 cubits 11 angulas tall (roughly 96 × 178 cm). The stone is the dense grey-green charnockite quarried at Tirupparankunram, eight kilometres south, and brought in by bullock cart along a route still legible in the field boundaries. A single block, the masons told the 1932 surveyors, was worth four months of an apprentice's labour.

II. What the 1932 Plates Catalogue

Iyengar's eleven months produced thirty-four sheets, twenty-eight of them now in the State Archives at Egmore (acc. 14/MA/1933, formerly Madras Govt. Press). Of these, the present article reproduces Plates IV, IX, X, XV, XXII, XXIV, XXIX, and XXXI. The remaining sheets — chiefly cross-sections through the lower torus — are scheduled for our next issue. The variation is more disciplined than it looks. Seventy-two of the one hundred and twenty-four piers carry a yali rampant on a crouching makara — the canonical posture given in the Mayamata, ch. 33, v. 47–52, and again in the Manasara, ch. 50. The departures, when they come, are deliberate: the parrot at pier 60 marks the lateral entry from the bazaar, the twin-headed yali at pier 59 sits at the geometric centre of the colonnade, and the figure in repose at pier 62 closes the western run before the visitor turns toward the Meenakshi shrine proper.

III. The Six-Day Block

What does it take to rough out one of these capitals? In November we put the question to Murugesan Sthapati, the seventh-generation master of the Sthapati workshop at Swamimalai, on the south bank of the Kaveri opposite Kumbakonam. He was, at the time, finishing a granite Nataraja for a temple near Coimbatore and had two apprentices on a yali block destined for a private mandapam in Karaikudi. He answered, as he tends to, by reaching for the chalk and the stone.

A Conversation at Swamimalai

Murugesan Sthapati · Workshop No. 4, Bharatha Sthapathi Street · 27 November 2024

Q. Six days, you said, to rough out a yali block. Where do those six days go?

Day one is for the centre line. Nothing else. The boy draws the brahmasutra down the face of the block in red ochre, and I check it with the plumb. If it is wrong by more than the width of a thumbnail, we wash it off and he draws it again. We do not begin cutting on day one.

Q. And day two?

Day two is the four corners — the kona. We strike the bounding box of the head, the shoulder, the raised paw, the hindquarter. These are four rectangles, and they must close on the centre line. The apprentice uses only the point chisel, no bushhammer yet. By evening you can see the yali standing inside the stone, but only in your mind.

Q. The forepaw is the hardest, no?

No. The hardest is the curl of the trunk. The forepaw is undercut, yes, and an apprentice will break it on his first attempt — every apprentice breaks one paw, that is part of becoming a sthapati. But the trunk has to come forward from the body without ever leaving the stone. If you cut it free, the block is finished. Day three and day four are for the trunk and the mane. We do them together, because they share the same depth.

Q. What does the apprentice do on day five?

He waits. He sharpens chisels. He watches. On day five I cut the eye and the open mouth — the boy is not allowed to touch the face of a yali for the first three years. The face is where the figure receives its breath.

Q. And day six?

Day six is the makara base, the lotus medallion at the hip, and the small ornament. Decoration. By the evening of day six, the block is rough — what we call rupa-pratima, the form-image. Finishing is another twenty days, sometimes thirty. But the figure is already alive on day six. If it is not alive on day six, we begin again with another stone.

Q. You said earlier that the Pudu Mandapam yalis taught you something specific.

Yes. The turn of the head. My grandfather copied a yali from pier 51 when he was twenty — he made a small one, in soapstone, for his teacher. He told me: the Madurai masters knew that a guardian who looks at you is not a guardian. A guardian looks past you, at what is behind you. That is the lesson of the Pudu Mandapam. We try to teach it on day five, when the boy is watching and not cutting.

IV. A Note on Survival

The Pudu Mandapam is, at the time of writing, in the seventeenth year of a contested conservation. Cloth merchants pay ₹14,800 per annum for a stall against a pier; the Archaeological Survey would prefer they pay nothing and leave. The piers themselves bear the marks of both arrangements: hooks driven into the makara base of pier 54, soot from a small stove at pier 58, and, at pier 62 — the yali in repose — a fresh coat of vermilion on the lower torus, applied for Margazhi by a tenant whose family has held the stall since 1947.

This is, on the whole, how the figures have lasted. The colonnade has been a market for at least three centuries; the yalis have been continuously looked at, touched, garlanded, and occasionally cursed, by people who had no theory of conservation and no need of one. The 1932 plates record a building already in vigorous secular use. The 2024 overlays record the same. Whatever else is to be said about the Pudu Mandapam — and a great deal is being said in the High Court at Madras — the yalis are still at their posts.

The Pillar Notebook will return in Issue XIII (Panguni 2025) with the second half of the Pudu Mandapam survey: the lower torus, the lotus medallions, and a measured comparison with the yali capitals at the Ramaswamy temple, Kumbakonam.

A Reader's Glossary

Five terms for the standing column

padma n.
The lotus, both flower and form. As a moulding, the swelling cushion-course directly beneath an abacus; as an icon, the seat of the god. At the Pudu Mandapam it appears as a medallion at the yali's hip, eight petals, full-blown.
kumbha n.
The pot-shaped element at the base of a pillar; literally "vessel." In the Dravida order the kumbha is the swelling above the plinth and below the palagai. It is also, mythologically, what the gods carry the amrita in.
prastara n.
The horizontal entablature carried by the colonnade — the band of mouldings between the capitals and the parapet. In Tamil practice, the prastara is composed of the kapota (eave), the vyalavari (yali frieze), and the hara (small pavilion frieze), in that ascending order.
shikhara n.
The capping stone of a tower; in Dravida usage, the domed or pyramidal finial that crowns the vimana over the sanctum. Not, in this tradition, the entire tower (a Nagara usage that has bled south through guidebooks).
kalasha n.
The pot-finial set above the shikhara; literally a brass or copper water-pot consecrated at the temple's completion and renewed at every kumbhabhishekam. A miniature kalasha appears as ornament on the abacus of piers 63–66.

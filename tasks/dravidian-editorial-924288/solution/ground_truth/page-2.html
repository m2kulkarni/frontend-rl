The Headlining Essay

Feature № 01 · Cosmology & Plan

Concentric Worlds: Reading the Seven Prakaras of Srirangam

At Tiruchirappalli's island temple, the world arrives in seven walls. The outermost gate opens onto a vegetable bazaar; the innermost shelters a reclining god in continuous sleep. Between them lies a graded cosmography in granite — a thesis, this essay argues, that the temple plan is not metaphor for the cosmos but the cosmos itself, rendered legible at walking pace.

Lakshmi Viswanathan moves out from the sanctum in deliberate stages, reading the Chitirai Veedhi by lamplight, the Uttara Veedhi at noon, and the great Rajagopuram at dusk, when the kitchens behind the fourth wall begin their preparations for the night offering. She brings to bear inscriptions from the reigns of Kulottunga I and the second Vijayanagara dispensation, the field surveys of S. R. Balasubrahmanyam (1971), and three weeks of conversations with the Sri Vaishnava archakas of the Ranganathaswamy temple between September and October 2024.

The argument turns, finally, on the soft transition between the third and fourth enclosures — the edge at which the marketplace yields to the precinct, and money is no longer counted aloud. It is here, Viswanathan proposes, that the temple performs its quietest and most radical work.

By Lakshmi Viswanathan, Reader in Architectural History, Madras Institute of Development Studies.

Feature № 02 · Civic Architecture

The Mandapa as Civic Hall

In the abandoned bazaar at Hampi, the thousand-pillared Kalyana Mandapa was never only a hall for the marriage of gods. R. Krishnaswami Aiyangar — drawing on Vijayanagara revenue rolls of 1565 — recovers the mandapa as a place of rents collected, weights tested, and grievances heard, between the ceremonies that gave it its present name.

"The pillar is a column of stone; the colonnade is a constitution."

Feature № 03 · Conservation

After the Paint Dries: Madurai, 1959–2024

Sixty-five years of polychromy, repainting, and dispute at the Meenakshi Sundareswarar temple, from the controversial 1959 kumbhabhishekam through the 2018 ASI restoration cycle. Anita Ratnam interviews the Pandian craft families, the Archaeological Survey, and the temple's own sthapati to ask what, in a living temple, conservation is for.

"We do not preserve a wall. We preserve the right of a wall to be repainted in the year that follows."

Editor's Note · Margazhi 2024

On the discipline of walking around things

The features in this issue began as an editorial argument about scale. We had, at first, intended a single long essay on Srirangam — and found, when we sent the writers into the field in August, that the temple refused to be the single thing we wanted it to be. What returned was a set of essays each committed to one register of attention: the cosmographic, the civic, the chromatic. We have placed Lakshmi Viswanathan's Srirangam piece at the head not because it is longest but because it teaches the eye how to read the rest.

A note on practice: every contributor visited their site at least twice, in two different liturgical seasons, and every measurement reported has been checked against published Archaeological Survey of India plans where available. Where it is not, we have said so.

— V. R. Padmanabhan, Features Editor  ·  Mylapore, 14 December 2024

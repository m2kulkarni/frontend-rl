A Field Guide to the Tamil Temple Country

Eight sites along the Kaveri delta and beyond — visited, paced, photographed, and measured by the editors between Aippasi 2022 and Margazhi 2024. Founding dates after the ASI; vimana heights from the 2017 re-survey.

▲  Compiled at the Editorial Office, Mylapore — Cross-referenced with ASI Volumes XXIII–XLI — Eight Entries · 1,011 Photographs Filed
I. Site · Imperial Chola Apex of the Survey

ThanjavurBrihadisvara · Periya Kovil

Thanjavur is the gravitational centre of any Tamil itinerary: Rajaraja Chola I's foundation of 1010 CE remains, by some margin, the tallest pre-modern temple vimana on the subcontinent. The 16-tier sikhara is dressed in a single granite cap-stone said to weigh 81 tonnes, hauled along a six-kilometre earthen ramp from Sarapallam. We walked the prakara at four in the morning; the Nandi pavilion, added two centuries later by the Nayaks, casts a moon-shadow exactly equal to its own height. The mural chamber on the upper ambulatory — open by written application only — preserves Chola fresco beneath a Nayak overlay, both surveyed by Sivaramamurti in 1957.

— Field notes by R. Anantharaman, Senior Editor

II. Site · Later Chola

DarasuramAiravatesvara

Smaller than its Thanjavur and Gangaikonda predecessors, Darasuram is the Chola laboratory — the place where the imperial idiom is allowed, finally, to play. Rajaraja II commissioned the temple around 1166 CE, and the mandapa front is carved as a wheeled stone chariot drawn by horses, hooves splaying outward from the plinth. The miniature panels along the basal mouldings narrate the sixty-three Nayanmars in fingertip-sized vignettes; one needs a torch and a willing watchman. The site is scrupulously maintained as a UNESCO listing under the Great Living Chola Temples inscription; expect a light, courteous footfall even on Pradosham evenings.

— Photography by Lakshmi V. Iyer

III. Site · Chola / Vijayanagara

KumbakonamSarangapani · Adi Kumbeswara

Kumbakonam is less a single monument than a town arranged around tanks. The Mahamaham theertham, observed once in twelve years, is held to draw nine sacred rivers into a single rectangular pool ringed by sixteen pavilions; we measured each in February 2024, finding the corner mantapas almost identically 6.4 metres on a side. The Sarangapani temple's eastern gopuram rises in a ratha-form chariot of eleven tiers, its stucco repainted on a six-yearly cycle by hereditary craftsmen of the Pandurangapuram colony. Brass lamps, betel-leaf vendors and the noise of the Kaveri bypass keep the precinct emphatically alive.

— Survey by S. Krishnamurthy

IV. Site · Chola / Pandya

ChidambaramNataraja Sabha

Chidambaram has no vimana in the conventional sense: the sanctum is the Chit Sabha, a roofed wooden hall whose copper-tiled superstructure is a rectangular dvi-tala rather than a soaring tower. What Chidambaram has, in compensation, are four carved gateway gopurams of nine and eleven storeys each, the eastern dating to Kulottunga III and the others completed under Kopperunjinga. The 108 karanas of the Natya Shastra are carved on the inner jambs of the eastern gopuram — a freestanding choreographic manual in stone. The Dikshitar priesthood, distinguished by the single tuft, retain hereditary custodianship under a 2014 Supreme Court ruling.

— Field notes by R. Anantharaman

V. Site · Madurai Nayak

MaduraiMeenakshi Sundareshwarar

Madurai is the great Nayak city: a temple-as-grid, where the four prakaras of the Meenakshi precinct still organise the streetscape outside their own walls. The fourteen gopurams range from 45 to 52 metres; the south gopuram, completed under Tirumalai Nayak in 1623, retains its original stucco programme — a pantheon of 1,511 figures repainted every twelve years by a single family of stapathis. We were given access to the thousand-pillar mandapa on a Tuesday morning, when the temple admits scholars before the public hours. The acoustic columns, struck with a knuckle, sound seven distinct pitches — all heptatonic, none tempered.

— Photography by Lakshmi V. Iyer

VI. Site · Chola / Vijayanagara

SrirangamRanganathaswamy

Srirangam is the largest functioning Hindu temple in the world by area: 156 acres held within seven concentric prakaras on an island in the Kaveri. The outermost wall, four kilometres in circuit, encloses streets, houses and small bazaars; the inner enclosures tighten progressively until the gold-leaf vimana of the reclining Vishnu, the Pranavakara Vimana, is reached. The Rajagopuram on the southern axis was completed only in 1987, by the Ahobila Mutt and the Tamil Nadu PWD — at 73 metres, it is the tallest gopuram in Asia. The festival enclosures fill, during Vaikunta Ekadasi, with perhaps 400,000 pilgrims in a single dawn.

— Survey by S. Krishnamurthy

VII. Site · Vijayanagara

HampiVirupaksha · Vitthala

Hampi sits at the technical edge of our Tamil remit, but no editorial conscience can omit it: the Vijayanagara emperors carried the southern idiom northward into Karnataka and back, and Hampi's masons trained generations of Tamil stapathis. The Vitthala mandapa's musical pillars — fifty-six monolithic granite columns each yielding a distinct tone — were partly silenced by colonial-era curiosity (visitors were encouraged to strike them with sticks); the ASI fenced the bay in 1986. The stone chariot of Vitthala, half-size of the Darasuram model, remains for many the iconic image of the Deccan plateau.

— Field notes by R. Anantharaman

VIII. Site · Late Vijayanagara

LepakshiVeerabhadra

Lepakshi is the great mural pavilion of the late Vijayanagara world. The Veerabhadra temple, founded in 1530 by the Aravidu brothers Virupanna and Viranna under Achyuta Deva Raya, is built around a granite outcrop on the Penukonda road; the Kalyana Mandapa, deliberately left unfinished, offers an X-ray section of how a sixteenth-century pillared hall was assembled. The ceiling fresco of the Natya Sabha — fourteen metres long — is the largest single painted surface of the period in the south, executed in mineral pigment over lime ground. We were granted study access through the kind offices of the ASI Bengaluru circle in early 2024.

— Photography by Lakshmi V. Iyer

¶ A Note from the Editors

On What Counts as a Vimana

Where two heights are plausible — the structural sanctum tower and the more visible gateway gopuram — we have given the figure most often cited in ASI dimension tables, and named the element. The Srirangam and Madurai figures are gopurams; Thanjavur and Darasuram are sanctum vimanas in the strict sense.

Heights are quoted to the metre, occasionally to the decimetre. Discrepancies of half a metre across sources are routine and often turn on whether the kalasha finial is included.

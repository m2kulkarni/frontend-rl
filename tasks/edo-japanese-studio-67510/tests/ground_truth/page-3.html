Journal · 帳面 · Vol. XII

On Joinery —
Notes from
the Bench.

Three entries set down in the order they were thought, between the cutting of one dovetail and the rough fitting of the next. The discipline observed here admits no nails, no metal fasteners, no modern adhesive beyond rice paste and nikawa hide-glue warmed over the bench-stove.

Kuramae · 蔵前 · Reiwa 6 · entries by the bench-master Ogata
Ninth month, third day · 九月三日

On the seasoning of paulownia.

Kiri arrives from the Aizu yards in the ninth month, sawn into rough boards a shaku and four sun wide, and we set it standing in the back lean-to where the river air comes through the slats. It must not be hurried. A board pulled inside before the equinox will cup within the first winter; one left out past the rains of the sixth month following will check at the ends and lose its singing weight. The merchant Hayashi-san of Sumida ward sends six boards each autumn at thirty-eight thousand yen the bundle, and of those six perhaps four become drawer-fronts for tansu and two are quietly returned to firewood.

The test is by ear and by palm. A seasoned kiri board, struck once with the knuckle, answers in a dry low note — not the hollow tap of green wood, not the brittle click of timber dried too fierce in a kiln. The grain, run under the thumb, should feel like fine paper laid over a still pond. When such a board is found, it is marked in sumi on the end-grain with the year-character and set aside until the commission calls for it.

The old apprentices were taught: kiri is the wood that breathes for the kimono. Build the chest tight and the silk dies inside. Build it true and the chest itself does the breathing — opening a hair in the rains, closing again in the dry of winter, keeping the inside at one quiet humour the year through.

— set down at the eighth hour, by lamplight
Eleventh month, eleventh day · 十一月十一日

On the geometry of the shippō-tsugi joint.

The shippō-tsugi — the seven-treasures joint — is not difficult to cut, only difficult to draw. Once the lines are set down faithfully on the end-grain, the saw and the chisel will follow. I use a marking-gauge of boxwood, the blade ground to a flat lance, and lay out the four interlocking circles which the joint takes its name from. The tangent points must fall at exactly one-third and two-thirds of the board's thickness; a hair off and the male and female faces will refuse one another, or worse, will close with a gap one cannot see but the wood will know.

This joint goes into the carcase corner of a tea-utensil box (chabako) where no fastener may show and where the lid, sliding, must not bind on the warmth of a summer afternoon. I cut three this morning for the commission of Mori-sensei of Yanaka, due at the new year. The first I rejected — the inner circle ran shallow by half a bu. The second held. The third I cut to keep the second honest.

One does not glue this joint. Rice-paste at the shoulders, nothing in the pins. The wood, swelling against itself across forty winters, makes the bond. Nails would shame it.

— bench-master Ogata, evening
Second month, nineteenth day · 二月十九日

On sharpening the kanna in winter water.

The water in the trough behind the bench freezes at the rim by the second month, and one must break the skin of ice with the back of the hand before laying the stone. This is right and proper. A kanna blade sharpened in summer water comes off the stone too easily; the steel has not been made to argue for its edge. In the cold of February, the stone bites slow, the slurry runs thick, and the angle one finds is the angle one has earned.

I keep three stones: an Amakusa for the rough, an Aoto from Kameoka for the middle, and a natural Nakayama awasedo bought at thirty years' apprentice's wages, on which the final edge is laid. The Nakayama is the colour of rained-on tile and asks for ten minutes at least. The blade — a Chiyozuru Sadahide forged in 1962 — must come off it able to take a shaving of three hundredths of a millimetre off Yoshino cypress without tearing the spring grain.

One tests the edge against the back of the thumbnail, never against the pad. If the steel bites, work begins. If it slides, the stones are gone back to. The kanna does not forgive a hurried morning, and the winter does not forgive a kanna unsharpened. I have learned this twice; once is not enough.

— before the morning shutters were opened

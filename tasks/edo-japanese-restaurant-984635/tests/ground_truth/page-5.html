How a Booking is Made

Reservations are taken by telephone, between the hours of ten in the morning and four in the afternoon, by the proprietress or her daughter. We do keep a written register, in the manner of the older houses, and we should like to know your name, the number in your party, and any allergies or fish you cannot take, at the time the booking is set down.

For the full kaiseki — seven courses, drawn from that morning's market and from the recipe books of the Asakura family — we ask for three days' notice, so that the fish and the first vegetables may be ordered properly. For parties of six guests or more, or for the engagement of an entire room for the evening, please give us seven days; this allows the kitchen and the staff to prepare without strain.

Walk-in guests are welcomed at the counter when seats remain, but on most evenings between mid-October and the close of cherry-blossom season the house is taken by reservation alone. Children above the age of ten are welcome in the zashiki rooms; we do not seat infants, with apologies.

The Four Zashiki Rooms

Tsuru-no-ma — the Crane Room

First FloorEight TatamiTokonoma with Bunsei scroll

The largest of the four, opening southward onto the inner garden and the stone lantern given to the house by a Kanda lumber merchant in the second year of Kaei. A pair of cranes worked in gold thread, by a Kyōto craftsman of the Meiji thirties, hangs above the tokonoma. Suitable for formal kaiseki, family observances, and small wedding meals.

Reserved as a single party only — we do not seat two groups in the same room, in keeping with house custom.

Matsu-no-ma — the Pine Room

First FloorSix Tatami

A quieter, west-facing room, set behind a sliding fusuma painted with young pines in sumi wash. Favoured by regular guests for the evening meal in winter, when the brazier is brought in and the river wind is held off by the closed shōji.

Second FloorSix Tatami

Reached by the narrow staircase at the back of the entrance hall. Plum branches in old gold leaf decorate the ranma transom; the window opens to a view of the neighbouring storehouse roof and, on a clear night, the moon as it passes Kanda Myōjin.

The smallest room, in the riverside annex built upon the Asakura family's old kura foundations. Two latticed windows give onto the Kanda River; in the second week of June, the proprietress will, by old habit, set out a small lantern and a rush blind for those who book this room. A Word on the Manner of Things

Kiritsubo keeps to the older usages where they remain practical. We do not take bookings by electronic mail; the telephone is preferred, and a written reply card may be sent by post on request.

Should you wish a particular dish from the seasonal kondate — the spring sweetfish, say, or the winter river-carp simmered in saké lees — please mention this when you telephone, and we will see what the morning market allows.

On Dress  ·  Yosoi Relaxed but considered — no slippers, please. Yukata are welcome in summer; we ask gentlemen to remove hats at the genkan, and that mobile telephones be silenced at the threshold of the zashiki.

桐 壺 Kiritsubo

Ryōtei of the Kanda Riverbank — Est. Bunsei 7 · 1824

Since Bunsei 7 (1824), at the bend below Shōhei Bridge. — from the kitchen book of Kiritsubo Yoshibei, fourth proprietor
— 一 —

家The House

Kiritsubo opened its sliding cedar doors in the seventh year of Bunsei, when the Kanda merchant Sasakura Tōbei set down his ledgers and took up the kitchen knife. Six generations later, the same recipe books — bound in indigo cloth, marked with the umebachi seal — still guide the morning's preparations. The zashiki rooms look out across the embankment toward Shōhei Bridge, much as they did when the Tokugawa rule was still ordering the city.

— 二 —

川The River

The Kanda flows past our kitchens at a measured pace, dark in summer with eel and ayu, bright in autumn with returning trout. Our fishmonger Kichizō calls before dawn from the Yanagibashi landing; what enters the kitchen at five o'clock is on the table by seven. We hold to the older practice — one fish, one method, one season — and refuse the convenience of the icebox for any creature taken from the river itself.

— 三 —

季The Season

Kaiseki at Kiritsubo follows the twenty-four sekki of the old calendar. We are entering Kanro now — the cold dew — when matsutake arrive from the Tamba hills and the river chestnut sweetens. The autumn course begins with a clear suimono of hamo and chrysanthemum, advances through grilled ayu salted three days, and closes with steamed rice cooked over matsutake and a single roasted ginkgo nut. Nine courses; ninety minutes; one room at a time.

Autumn — Aki no Kondate Matsutake
by lamplight, in a private zashiki.

From the eighth of October until the second snowfall, the small zashiki on the river side — six tatami, paper lanterns, a single hanging scroll changed each week — is given over to the matsutake course. We seat one party only between the hours of six and nine.

Aki no Kaiseki — nine courses, ¥28,000 per guest. Sake pairings from Kanda's Toshimaya brewery, ¥6,500.

家伝The House & Its Lineage

Eight generations
at the Kanda riverbank.

桐壺 ／ 神田川岸 料亭

From a single teahouse opened in Bunsei 7 to the ryōtei standing today, the Kawamura household has cooked at the same bend of the Kanda for two hundred years. What follows is a brief account of the house, its timbers, and its line.

Chronicle

From Bunsei to Reiwa — a chronicle of the house.

桐壺八代

Kiritsubo was opened in the seventh year of Bunsei (1824) by Kawamura Tōbei, the second son of a Kanda salt-merchant family who had grown weary of the warehouse and taken instruction in tea from a retired priest of the Kan'ei-ji. The first house was a simple riverside chamise of three mats and a kettle, set on pilings at the foot of Shōhei Bridge. Tōbei served grilled ayu from the upstream traps, salted plum, and a sencha drawn from spring water carted in from Ochanomizu.

The house survived the great Ansei fires of 1855 by the narrow margin of a westerly wind, though the kitchen wing was lost and the second proprietor, Kawamura Kihei, rebuilt it in the winter of that year using cedar floated down from the Tama. A ledger from Kihei's hand, preserved in the inner storehouse, records the cost of the rebuilding at 六十二両二分 — sixty-two ryō and two bu — and notes the names of the carpenters from Kanda Sakuma-chō who executed the work.

Conversion from teahouse to full ryōtei came under the fourth proprietor, Kawamura Sōshichi, in Meiji 12 (1879). Sōshichi had spent four years in Kyōto under a cook of the Tankan-ryū kaiseki tradition, and on returning to Kanda he extended the house with a second zashiki, an inner garden, and a proper preparation kitchen. The recipe books from his hand — bound in indigo-dyed cloth and now numbering eleven volumes — remain the working archive of the kitchen, supplemented by the additions of each subsequent generation.

The Kantō earthquake of Taishō 12 cracked the main beam but did not bring the house down; the Shōwa air raids of 1945 spared the block by a chance of wind. Renovations in 1962, 1989, and 2014 have replaced tatami, re-thatched the inner roof, and reinforced the kitchen — but the structural cedar of Kihei's rebuilding still carries the load of the upper floor.

Three Generations

Marks left on the timbers, on the recipe books, on the garden.

壱 ／ I

Kawamura Tōbei

Founder. Bunsei 7 (1824) — Tenpō 14 (1843). Opened the original three-mat teahouse on pilings at Shōhei Bridge. Recipes for grilled ayu, salted plum, and the house dashi base are preserved in his hand on rice paper now mounted in the inner zashiki.

四 ／ IV

Kawamura Sōshichi

Fourth proprietor. Meiji 12 (1879) conversion to full ryōtei after four years in Kyōto under the Tankan-ryū kaiseki kitchen. Built the second zashiki, the inner garden, and the eleven indigo-bound recipe volumes still in working use.

八 ／ VIII

Kawamura Sumie

Current proprietor since 2011. Eighth of the line. Trained at the Tsukiji fish market and at Kikunoi in Kyōto. Restored the river-fish menu after the 2008 reopening of the Kanda upstream traps and reads the recipe books aloud each Tuesday morning.

The Machiya Preserved

A merchant townhouse, kept to its old measure.

The structural frame is the same cedar raised by Kihei in 1855: two-bay front, three-bay deep, a low nakaniwa court between the front and rear zashiki, and a tiled kitchen wing along the western side. The cedar lattice (kōshi) at the entrance was remilled in 1989 from the original timber and re-set on the same iron pins.

Tatami in both zashiki are laid in Bingo igusa rush, replaced every seven years by the same family of mat-makers in Hiroshima Prefecture who served the house under the fifth proprietor. The inner garden carries a single old plum tree — the umebachi of the house mon — said to have been planted by Sōshichi's wife on the day the second zashiki was opened.

Guests will find no chairs and no electric lighting in the inner zashiki; lamps are andon, and seating is on flat cushions at a low chabudai. We ask that visitors remove their footwear at the genkan stone, where slippers are provided.

The Eighth Generation

A note from Sumie, who keeps the books now.

I came back to Kanda in the spring of 2008, after eleven years away — four at Tsukiji on the riverfish counter, seven at Kikunoi in Kyōto under the late Murata-san. My grandfather, the seventh proprietor, had been ill that winter, and the house needed a hand at the kettle again.

What I have tried to do, since taking the books in 2011, is to neither modernise the kitchen out of recognition nor freeze it as a museum piece. The recipe volumes are read aloud each Tuesday morning before the market run; what we serve at dinner is what the season and the river have given us, set against what Sōshichi, or Kihei, or my grandfather wrote down. If a dish has no entry in any of the eleven books, I will not yet serve it.

The house remains open by reservation only, six evenings a week, for no more than twenty-two guests at a sitting. We are grateful for your visit.

川村 澄江 — Kawamura Sumie, viii.

De Anno Vinario

The Roman wine-year was no idle calendar. In Februarius, the vineyards of Latium were pruned by the same hand that had set them; in the long heat of Augustus and September, the grape was gathered to the chant of vintners; and at the festival of the Vinalia, in Aprilis, the new must was offered to Jupiter before any cup met a Roman lip. To drink wine, in the Empire, was to remember a god.

Of all the wines so consecrated, three stood above the rest. Falernian, raised on the slopes of Mount Massicus in Campania, was the wine of senators and emperors; the vintage of the consulate of Lucius Opimius, in 121 B.C., was already legend in Pliny's day, drunk a hundred years old and amber as honey. Caecuban, from the marshes near Fundi, was the wine Horace called generosum — noble — and reserved for occasions of triumph. Massic, paler and more austere, was the soldier's reward and the philosopher's friend. These three our cellar honours; these three we pour, in the proportion the ancients set down, mixed with cool spring water in a krater of Corinthian bronze.

What follows is our Index Vinorum, drawn from the cellar beneath the Aventine. The ancient names we offer in their vinted memory; the modern names — Italian and Greek — we offer as their living descendants, raised on the same soils, by the same suns, beneath the same lares.

ACHAIA · GRAECA
— De Mulso Domus —

Of the house mulsum a word in closing. Honey from our own hives at Tibur is warmed gently with a young white from the Alban Hills, perfumed with bay, black pepper, and a single thread of saffron.

It is poured, in the manner of the ancients, into a small earthenware cup at the moment the gustatio is set down — the eggs, the olives, the pickled mullet — and is offered, as the law of hospitality requires,

gratis · without price · to every guest at table

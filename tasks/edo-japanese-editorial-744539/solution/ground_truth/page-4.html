Interview — Volume IV, no. 2

The Nishijin Loom 西陣機 ・ 京都織匠覚書

Hosokawa Kiku, fourth-generation weaver of the Nishijin district, on patience, provincial dye, and the five canonical patterns that pass under her shuttle. A morning conversation, conducted on Imadegawa-dōri, in a workshop that has not moved since 1887.

Interview   by Tachibana Sōsuke  ·  Photographs withheld at the weaver’s request; replaced herein by descriptive plates.

三 ・ 問 ・ 三
問 · Q.I

Your loom is a Jacquard from 1893 — older than this neighbourhood’s electricity. Why keep it?

Because it remembers. The mechanism was assembled here in Nishijin in the twenty-sixth year of Meiji, after my great-grandfather returned from Lyon with three crates of punched cards and a head full of new ideas. Newer looms can do what this one does — faster, with fewer broken threads — but they do not produce the same fabric. There is a hesitation in the shed of an old loom, a half-second of slack as the harness settles, and that hesitation is what makes the silk hold light the way it does.

Four generations have set it up: my great-grandfather Hosokawa Tsunejirō, my grandfather Saburō, my father Akira, and now myself. We have replaced the heddles, the reed, the breast-beam, almost everything that wears. The frame is still oak from Tanba. I would no more discard it than a calligrapher would discard her father’s brush.

問 · Q.II

Walk me through setting up seigaiha on the Jacquard.

Seigaiha — the blue-ocean wave — is the simplest of the five and therefore the most exposed. Any clumsiness shows. The pattern repeats on a unit of two-hundred warp ends and one-hundred-forty wefts; we punch four-hundred and eighty cards for one full vertical course. I prepare them on a Friday and I prefer to be alone in the workshop, because miscounting a single hole shifts every wave thereafter by half a hand.

The warp is silk from Tango, dressed with a thin rice-paste sizing. For a traditional kimono fabric we run twenty-eight-hundred warp ends across thirty-eight centimetres — fine, but not the finest. The shuttle carries indigo-dyed weft on the figured rows and a slightly darker indigo on the ground. The trick of seigaiha is that it must look like water at rest: you should not see the weaver’s hand at all.

問 · Q.III

And asanoha — the hemp-leaf — is famously a child’s pattern. Does that change how you weave it?

It changes the colour, mostly. For an infant’s under-kimono I will weave asanoha in safflower red on a cream ground; the red is from Yamagata, three days’ soak, and it is paid for by the gram, not by the bolt. For an adult’s obi I take the same pattern down to indigo on indigo, and the eye reads it as almost geometrical, almost severe. The point of asanoha was always protection — its six-fold symmetry was thought to ward off illness — and a baby in safflower asanoha is, in the old reading, hedged by six small prayers per square inch.

The setup is unforgiving. The repeat is small, only forty-eight wefts, and a single broken warp end will bisect a leaflet so cleanly that you cannot mend it; you must cut back and rerun the course. I lost an afternoon to it last Tuesday.

問 · Q.IV

Shippō, sayagata, kikkō — the three remaining canonicals. Which is the hardest?

Sayagata, without question. The interlocked key-fret is mathematically simple but optically merciless: any drift in the beat of the weft, any slackness in the warp tension, will produce a visible curve in what is supposed to be a straight line. I do not weave sayagata in summer. The humidity in Kyoto from the seventh through the ninth month makes the silk too lively; the figure goes soft. We commission sayagata bolts in late autumn or in the deep cold of the second month.

Shippō — the seven-treasure circles — is the kindest pattern. Forgiving of error, generous to the eye. Kikkō, the tortoise-shell, sits between the two: hexagonal, demanding of the harness, but the symmetry hides a multitude of small sins.

問 · Q.V

A bolt of figured silk from your workshop sells, I am told, for between three-hundred-and-sixty-thousand and eight-hundred-thousand yen. How do you decide?

By the dye, mostly. A bolt in Awa indigo with safflower accents and a small amount of gardenia yellow — three plant dyes, three soakings — runs to seven-hundred-twenty-thousand. The same pattern in chemical dye, which we still produce for theatrical clients and for export, is offered at three-hundred-and-eighty. The weaving labour is identical; the difference is the time and the provenance of the colour.

A bolt is twelve-and-a-half metres. At our pace — between forty and sixty centimetres a day, depending on the figure — that is roughly a month at the loom. We complete twenty-two bolts a year. Last year we completed nineteen, because of a punched-card error in March that cost us a whole bolt of sayagata.

The Workshop, 7:40 a.m.

A long, low room facing north onto Imadegawa-dōri. Two looms, one in use, one shrouded under unbleached cotton. Sidebar · 染料覚

The Three Provincial Dyes

  • Ai · Indigo Awa Province Fermented in the Yoshino river basin since the Muromachi period. Our house buys from the Furushō workshop in Tokushima, eight kilograms of sukumo per quarter. Six successive baths bring silk to its full midnight; three baths, the colour of a clear evening sky.
  • Beni · Safflower Yamagata, Mogami River Picked at dawn in the sixth month, when the dew is still on the petal. The yellow is washed out with cold water; what remains is the red. Twelve-thousand yen per ten grams in the spring market of 2024; enough, with care, for a quarter-bolt of accent.
  • Kuchinashi · Gardenia Kii Peninsula The dried fruit, soaked overnight, gives a clear and durable yellow that does not bleed in the rinse. We use it for the figure in shippō and, sparingly, for the sun-warmed cream of an infant’s asanoha.

“The loom hesitates — that half-second is what makes the silk hold light.”

— Hosokawa Kiku, Imadegawa-dōri, Kyōto

The Five Canonical Wagara

Hosokawa-san returns to the same five figures throughout the conversation. For the reader’s convenience, a brief schedule follows; a fuller treatment is given in our companion Pattern Library.

一
Seigaiha
青海波
Concentric arcs evoking still water. The simplest unit, the most exposed.
二
Asanoha
麻の葉
Six-fold hemp-leaf. Worn by infants for protection; by adults for restraint.
三
Shippō
七宝
Interlocking circles, the seven Buddhist treasures. The most forgiving figure.
四
Sayagata
紗綾形
Linked key-fret. Geometric, demanding; not woven in summer humidity.
五
Kikkō
亀甲
Tortoise-shell hexagon. A symbol of longevity, kind to the harness.
Editor’s Note

The Hosokawa workshop occupies the second and third buildings on the south side of Imadegawa-dōri, west of the Horikawa intersection. Visits are by prior arrangement only; the loom in use is not a demonstration piece. Hosokawa Kiku-san accepts apprentices irregularly, and never more than one at a time. A small showroom on the ground floor is open to the public on the second and fourth Saturday of each month, from ten until three.

Conducted in the workshop on the morning of the seventh day of the third month, 2024. Translated from the Kyōto dialect by the interviewer. The umebachi seal that closes each answer is the household mark of the Hosokawa line.

A Travelogue · 木曽街道

On the Kisokaidō 木曽路にて — 旅日記

BY OKADA SUMIKO  ·  Seven post-stations between Magome and Narai  ·  Set against Hiroshige's Sixty-Nine Stations of 1835–1838

From Magome to Narai I walked seven of the sixty-nine stations of the Kisokaidō, carrying nothing but a notebook bound in indigo paper, two brushes, an ink-stick from Nara, and a pair of straw waraji which I replaced twice. The road climbs and falls between cedar and cypress; the Kiso river is never out of earshot. I went not to retrace Hiroshige — that is impossible — but to ask, walking, what the prints leave out.

What follows is the notebook in fair copy: each entry dated by the lunar calendar of Bunsei reckoning, kept honest as I could to the hour, the weather, and the bowls of soba. I have changed nothing of the prices, though the innkeeper at Tsumago laughed at me for setting them down.

The road, drawn in sumi — seven of sixty-nine.

Magome ↗ Tsumago ↗ Nojiri ↗ Suhara ↗ Agematsu ↗ Fukushima ↗ Yabuhara ↗ Narai. Eight place-names, seven days of walking; some twenty-three ri in all (about ninety kilometres in modern measure), reckoned along the old post-road, not the river-cut shortcuts the porters now prefer.

The sketch at right was drawn at a tea-stand above Torii-tōge, the pass between Yabuhara and Narai, on the Ninth Month, twentieth day, at the Hour of the Horse.

道 中 日 記
第四十三駅 · Station the Forty-third

Magome 馬籠宿

Ninth Month, 13th day. Clear after rain. Departed Nakatsugawa at the Hour of the Snake; arrived Magome by the Hour of the Sheep. Lodged at the Tajimaya, fee one hundred eighty mon with two meals.

Magome stands tilted on the slope; the stone road climbs so sharply that the ridge of one inn becomes the threshold of the next. After rain the cobbles glisten like the back of a black carp, and the cedar gutters run audibly. Hiroshige drew the village at dusk with two travelers and a packhorse, and what he understood — that I did not, until I had walked it — is that the eye in Magome is always tilted. There are no horizontals.

At the Tajimaya the supper was clear miso, mountain trout grilled with sansho, pickled gourd, and rice that had been husked in the village. A pilgrim from Ise sat opposite and told me the cost of his straw cape (twenty-four mon at Ōkute), and the price he was offered for it (eight mon, refused). He laughed, and the lamp wavered, and the maid brought a second pot of barley tea without being asked.

第四十二駅

Tsumago 妻籠宿

Ninth Month, 14th day. Clear after rain; departed Tsumago at the Hour of the Dragon. Cool wind from the north.

Tsumago is the quieter sister of Magome — laid along the river, its lattices darker, the smoke thicker because the kitchens here use a softer wood. I rose at the Hour of the Tiger and could hear the Kiso river plainly through the paper screen. At dawn the mist sat in the valley like the milk-skin of warmed soy, and the swallows of the eaves cried as if the season had not yet turned.

The post-station office, kept since Keichō, still keeps its register. The clerk let me read the entries for the Eighth Month — sixty-three travelers in one day on the festival of the Suwa shrine; one packhorse with broken hoof; a Dutch interpreter proceeding to Edo with three boxes of glass.

第四十駅

Nojiri 野尻宿

Ninth Month, 15th day. Overcast, with rain at the Hour of the Monkey. Crossed the Yogawa bridge twice, having forgotten the brushes.

Nojiri is called nanamagari — "the seven turns" — for the road bends back upon itself so often that the village is twice as long as the distance between its endpoints. Walking it, I twice passed the same well, twice greeted the same woman pounding millet in a wooden mortar. The third time she handed me a pickled chestnut without speaking.

Hiroshige's print of Nojiri is among the loveliest in the series — a wooden bridge in spring rain, the riders veiled. Mine was autumn, the rain colder, and the bridge timber softer with damp. The trout-seller was already gone for the day.

Lunch
Soba, hot, with grated yam — 18 mon
Tea (twice)
At the Yogawa stand — 4 mon each
Rain
From the Hour of the Monkey, lasting three hours
The river does not change its tone for the traveler; only the traveler changes his ear. By Suhara I had begun to hear it as music.

SUHARA · 須原宿

Ninth Month, 16th day, evening. The Kiso here runs broader and slower; the cedar gives way to a paler pine. Lodged at the Iseya, fee one hundred forty mon. Wrote two pages by candle, then slept badly because of the moon.

第三十八駅

Agematsu 上松宿

Ninth Month, 17th day. Bright; a high cold wind from Mt. Ontake. Walked to the Nezame-no-toko in the afternoon.

The Nezame-no-toko is a stretch of pale rock through which the Kiso has cut a corridor as smooth as a lacquer tray. I sat there an hour, sketching, and a boatman came up to tell me — without prompting — that the deepest pool is twelve ken across, that no one drowns there because the kami of the place "takes only those it likes," and that the price of crossing in his boat at flood season is forty-eight mon, but never in autumn.

Agematsu's cypress is the famous one. Even outside the inn the air smells of fresh-cut wood; the carpenters of Nagoya buy here. I bought one cypress chopstick, finely planed, for two mon.

第三十七駅

Fukushima 福島関所

Ninth Month, 18th day. Cold and clear. Held at the barrier from the Hour of the Horse to the Hour of the Sheep, while papers were inspected.

Of the four great barriers of the realm, Fukushima is the strictest after Hakone. The clerk asked my province, my purpose, my temple of registry, and the contents of the long box (brushes, a stone, two volumes of the Tōkaidō meisho zue for comparison). He read the title aloud and, satisfied that I was harmless, stamped the pass in vermilion.

The town is grander than Tsumago — two-storey lattices, deeper eaves — and the soba is worse: twenty mon for a thin broth, the noodles overlong. I record this not in complaint but as evidence that even Hiroshige's road has its disappointments.

Barrier
Sekisho — papers stamped, no fee
Soba (poor)
20 mon
Hours detained
One toki — about two hours
第三十五駅 / 第三十四駅 · The Pass

Yabuhara → Narai 藪原 · 鳥居峠 · 奈良井

Ninth Month, 20th day. Clear, then mist. Crossed the Torii-tōge — 1,197 metres — between the Hours of the Hare and the Horse. Six hours, with one rest.

The pass is the spine of the road. Yabuhara at its foot is famous for combs of minebari wood, the fine ones polished with horsetail and bee-wax; I bought one for my mother for sixty-four mon, a price the shopkeeper called yasui and I pretended to believe. The climb begins gently and then is not gentle. Halfway up there is a stone torii — for which the pass is named — and a small shrine to the mountain kami, with a wooden ladle that has been there, the priest says, "since before my grandfather's grandfather."

On the descent the mist closed in, and the road became silver. I could hear the Kiso river below, although the river had by then become a different river — the Narai, flowing the other way, toward the Sea of Japan. It is at the pass that the country turns over. Narai itself is a kilometre of dark lattices, and its inns smell of the lacquer-makers' workshops. I lodged at the Echigoya, ate soba (sixteen mon, excellent), and slept ten hours without moving.

Coda · 跋 An afterword on Hiroshige, walked rather than looked at.

What the prints leave out, and what they keep.

Hiroshige's Sixty-Nine Stations of the Kisokaidō was begun in 1835 with Keisai Eisen and completed by Hiroshige alone in 1838 — a project whose calm has often misled. Walking the road one understands that the calm is a choice. Hiroshige left out the smell of woodsmoke at four in the morning. He left out the ache in the calf above the Torii-tōge, and the way a porter's voice carries up a valley at twice its true volume. He left out the price of soba.

But what he kept — the tilt of Magome, the mist of Tsumago, the bend of Nojiri, the pale rock at Agematsu, the stiff officials of Fukushima, the crowning torii of the pass — these are not ornaments. They are the memorable shape of the road itself, what one carries home in the body when the notebook is closed. To walk the Kisokaidō with the prints in hand is to discover, gratefully, how exactly Hiroshige looked, and how little he flattered.

I have come back to Kyōto with my brushes worn down, sixty-four mon poorer for a comb, and the conviction that the post-stations of the Kiso road are not a series at all, but one continuous sentence in which the river is the verb.

— Okada Sumiko

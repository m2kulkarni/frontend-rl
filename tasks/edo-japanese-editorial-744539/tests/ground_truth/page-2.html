Feature Essay · Volume XII · Autumn, Reiwa 6

Thirty-Six Views, Reconsidered

Two centuries on, Katsushika Hokusai's Fugaku Sanjūrokkei remains less a series of landscapes than a sustained argument about looking — about the geometry of attention, the colour of distance, and the particular silence a mountain holds when surrounded by water.

By Tachibana Sōen, Contributing Editor
Filed from Nakagyō-ku, Kyōto · 9th of the Long Moon

FUGAKU · 三十六景 · 1830 — 1832
Part I · The Argument of Foam

It is tempting, after so many reproductions on tea-tins and umbrella sleeves, to believe that one has already seen the Great Wave. One has not. Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura — the Wave Off the Coast of Kanagawa — was published by Nishimuraya Yohachi sometime between the second and fourth years of Tenpō, in an edition that almost certainly numbered between five and eight thousand impressions. To stand before a first printing today, as I did this past spring at the Hosomi Museum, is to understand abruptly that the print is not an image of a wave at all. It is an image of a sky that happens to be intruded upon by water.

The compositional argument is laid out with an architect's coldness. Hokusai gives roughly two thirds of his block to negative space — the pale lower atmosphere, the grey-white above — and into that emptiness he places, off-axis and miniaturised, the cone of Fuji. The mountain, the ostensible subject of the entire series, is rendered no larger than a thumbnail. A cresting wave, by contrast, occupies the whole upper-left quadrant. The visual hierarchy is inverted: the immortal peak is dwarfed by a transient column of foam that, in life, would collapse within seconds.

This inversion is not playfulness. It is, I would argue, the entire point of the late series. Hokusai had begun the Fugaku in his seventieth year, and would issue the supplementary ten plates only in 1834. The sequence is the work of an artist who had outlived his own first reputation and was returning to landscape with the patience of someone who knows he will not have time to do it twice.

On the matter of bero-ai

Much of the print's strange modernity rests on a single pigment. Bero-ai — Berlin blue, Prussian blue, ferric ferrocyanide — had been imported through Nagasaki in small quantities since the 1820s, but only became affordable to commercial publishers around 1829, when a Chinese-manufactured equivalent reached Edo at a fraction of the Dutch price. Hokusai and his publisher were among the very first to use it at scale. The deep, almost violent blue of the wave's hollow, and the cooler wash of the receding sky, are both bero-ai, applied in gradations of hand-printed bokashi that no earlier indigo could have sustained without bleeding.

The pigment matters because it is what allows the print to argue. An indigo wave would have spread, softened, become atmospheric. Bero-ai holds an edge. The foam-claws against the dark trough are crisp enough to read as drawing rather than as weather. Hokusai is not depicting a wave; he is diagramming one, and bero-ai is the ink of diagrams.

Note on Pigment

Prussian blue first appears in Japanese commercial woodblock printing c. 1829, in fan-prints by Keisai Eisen. By 1831, Hokusai's publisher Nishimuraya was advertising the Fugaku at 16 mon per sheet — roughly a bowl of soba — making the series, despite its imported pigment, a popular rather than aristocratic object.

Edition & State

First-state impressions are distinguished by the unbroken outline block, a cleaner key around the boatmen's heads, and the publisher's mark Eijudō 永寿堂 in the lower margin. Of the surviving impressions worldwide, fewer than one hundred and twenty are considered first-state.

Part II · Silence as Composition

The off-axis Fuji

Western landscape, by long convention, places its subject near the centre and balances it with secondary masses. Hokusai's Fuji, in plate after plate of the Fugaku, refuses this. In the Red Fuji (Gaifū Kaisei) the mountain pushes hard into the right edge. In Kōshū Kajikazawa a fisherman on a rock outweighs the mountain entirely. In Sundai, Edo, the peak is glimpsed through a gap in the rooftops as a casual incident of geography.

The argument here is theological as much as compositional. Fuji is sacred precisely because it does not require centring. The mountain organises the world by being in it; the artist's task is to find the angle from which that organising fact becomes visible without being announced. To place Fuji on axis would be to flatter the viewer's attention. Hokusai prefers to ambush it.

In Kanagawa-oki this strategy reaches its limit. The wave is the visual event; the mountain is what the wave is failing to obscure. Fuji is small because eternity is small in any given moment of weather. And the negative sky between them — that long, almost embarrassing band of nothing — is the medium through which the argument is conducted.

Negative sky, balanced foam

Consider what occupies the right two-thirds of the upper register: a thinly graded wash, no horizon line, no birds, no cloud-script of the kind a Kanō-school painter would have felt obliged to provide. It is empty, and the emptiness is doing structural work. Cover it with a sheet of paper and the print collapses; the wave becomes hectic, the boats becomes legible only as ornament. The composition lives in the tension between the foam's busyness and the sky's refusal.

This is, I think, what makes the Fugaku feel so durable. They are prints that know the value of what they leave out. A century later, one finds the same instinct in the screen-paintings of Higashiyama Kaii, and in certain photographs by Domon Ken, and in the opening shots of Ozu Yasujirō. It is, at the risk of sounding provincial, a very Japanese argument: that depiction is mostly the management of absence.

Hokusai signed the print, in his seventy-second year, with the sobriquet Hokusai aratame Iitsu hitsu — "from the brush of Iitsu, formerly Hokusai." He would change his name twice more before he died at eighty-nine, claiming, famously, that everything he had produced before the age of seventy was not worth counting. The Fugaku are the work of someone still becoming. That, too, is part of the argument.

Further Viewing

First-state impressions are held in the British Museum (1906,1220,0.534); the Metropolitan Museum, New York (JP1847); the Tokyo National Museum; and the Sumida Hokusai Museum, which holds two. The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, holds a state with weakened key-block — useful for comparing wear in the foam contour.

On the boatmen

Three oshiokuri-bune, the fast cargo boats that ran fresh bonito and live fish from Bōsō to Edo's Nihonbashi market. Eight oarsmen per boat. They are not figures of terror so much as of work: the wave is their ordinary weather.

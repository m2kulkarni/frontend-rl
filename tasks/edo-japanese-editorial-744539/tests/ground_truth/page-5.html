Pattern Library 五つの和柄

Five canonical textile patterns — wagara — recur through the printed and woven matter of late-Edo Japan with such persistence that they functioned, in effect, as a shared visual vocabulary. Each carries a name, a date of first record, and a settled emblematic meaning. What follows is a brief catalogue: origins, symbolism, and the trades and stations of life in which the pattern was customarily used.

The Five Canonical
Wagara, in Order of
First Recorded Use.

The names below are drawn principally from the Hinagata Kikō of Kanbun 6 (1666) — the earliest surviving kimono pattern book — and from Hishikawa Moronobu's twelve illustrated pattern books of Jōkyō 1 (1684). Where dates appear, they refer to the first appearance of the pattern in dyed or woven textiles, not to the underlying geometric form, several of which are far older and travel from Tang China through Heian-period court robes before settling into the merchant wardrobes of Edo.

— I —

Seigaiha

青海波
Symbol:
calm seas, fortune unending
First record:
Heian gagaku robe, c. 794
Usage:
fishermen, court music, summer kosode

The name — "blue-sea waves" — descends from a piece of Tang-derived court music whose dancers wore the pattern. As a textile motif it spread south from the Heian court to the fishing villages of Settsu and Sagami, where it was understood as a quiet petition for safe return.

By the Genroku era it had been thoroughly absorbed into civilian dress and is the wagara most often seen in Hokusai's marginalia.

— II —

Asanoha

麻の葉
Symbol:
vigorous growth, a child's health
First record:
Buddhist statuary, Kamakura era
Usage:
infant swaddling, undyed bleached linen

A six-pointed star inscribed in a hexagon, taken from the leaf of the hemp plant — itself the fastest-growing fibre known to Edo households. Custom held that the first cloth wrapped about a newborn should bear this pattern so that the child might grow as straight and as quickly as the hemp itself.

Rendered, by the late eighteenth century, in cinnabar against indigo for women, indigo against bleached white for boys.

— III —

Shippō

七宝
Symbol:
harmony, the seven Buddhist treasures
First record:
Hinagata Kikō, 1666
Usage:
brocade obi, temple-banner silks

Four overlapping circles produce a continuous lattice of four-petaled stars and pointed ovals. The seven treasures of which the name speaks are gold, silver, lapis, agate, coral, crystal, and pearl — listed in the Lotus Sūtra and recited at New Year by every Kyōto weaver of consequence.

Rendered most carefully by the workshops of Nishijin in gold-leaf and indigo silk; Karaori robes of the Nō stage almost invariably carry it.

— IV —

Sayagata

紗綾形
Symbol:
longevity, an unbroken way
First record:
Ming damask imports, c. 1570
Usage:
samurai under-robes, mounting-paper

Interlinked manji-fret. The form arrived from Ming-dynasty saya damask in the late sixteenth century and was at once adopted by warrior households for the under-robes worn beneath formal kamishimo. Its appeal was the unbroken line: a path that turns without ever ending.

Hand-mounters of woodblock prints used a finer version of the same pattern as backing-paper, where it survives still on the verso of every well-kept Hokusai sheet.

— V —

Kikkō

亀甲
Symbol:
endurance, ten thousand years
First record:
Shōsō-in textiles, c. 756
Usage:
Nō costume, ceremonial obi

The hexagon of the tortoise's shell. The proverb — tsuru wa sen-nen, kame wa man-nen, the crane a thousand years, the tortoise ten thousand — fixes the pattern's meaning to the longest-lived creature the language knows.

Found among the eighth-century textiles preserved at Tōdai-ji's Shōsō-in repository, it is the oldest of the five wagara still in continuous use, and the most reserved: kikkō belongs to ceremony, not the street.

參考書目
  1. Hishikawa Moronobu (菱川師宣). On-hiinakata [御雛形] — twelve illustrated pattern books. Edo: Yamagataya Ichirōbei, Jōkyō 1 (1684). The foundational corpus for civilian wagara; held in fragments at the Tōkyō National Museum and complete at the Spencer Collection, NYPL.
  2. Anonymous compiler. Hinagata Kikō [雛形菊慕] — pattern book of women's kosode. Kyōto: undocumented publisher, Kanbun 6 (1666). Earliest known printed kimono-pattern manual; the source for the canonical names of Shippō and Sayagata as set down here.
  3. Hayashi Yoshikazu, ed. Edo Sarasa: Some-iro no Hyakka [江戸更紗 染色の百花]. Kyōto: Nigensha, reprint 1972. A standard reference for dye-recipes and pattern dating across the Bunsei and Tenpō eras.
  4. Nishijin Textile Industrial Association, ed. Nishijin Ori no Rekishi to Bunken [西陣織の歴史と文献]. Kyōto: 1968 ; consulted at the Nishijin Textile Centre archive, Imadegawa-Horikawa.
  5. Tanaka Atsuko. "On the Provenance of the Asanoha Hexagram in Kamakura Sculpture," Bulletin of the Kyōto National Museum 14 (1992), pp. 47–73.

Foundation

Established in 1142 as a daughter-house of the Abbey of Saint-Évroult-en-Ouche, the priory of Saint-Évroult-de-la-Ferté grew under the patronage of the Counts of Alençon to become one of the most accomplished monastic ensembles of upper Normandy.

A Chronicle

History of the Priory

MCXLII  —  MCMLVIII

Survival

Suppressed in 1791 and stripped of its library by Napoleonic decree, the buildings passed in 1873 to the département de l'Orne. The cloister and chapter house were restored in 1958 under the architect Yves Froidevaux.

Chronologia Prioratus

Eight Centuries of the Priory

The Master Mason

Geoffroy de Lisieux

fl. 1186 – c. 1212

Named in three surviving fabric rolls of the priory, Geoffroy de Lisieux is credited with the elevation of the chapter house and the southern walk of the cloister. His banker-marks — a small lozenge bisected by a single stroke — appear on more than two hundred ashlars now visible in the surviving walks.

Trained, it is thought, in the workshop of the cathedral of Lisieux, he carried into the Pays d'Ouche an early Gothic vocabulary of pointed arches on slender colonnettes, banded capitals carved with crockets and stiff-leaf, and the use of fine Caen stone for ornamental work above a core of local Vimoutiers limestone.

His name disappears from the priory accounts after 1212. A funerary slab now mounted in the eastern walk, reading Hic iacet Gaufridus magister operis, is traditionally identified as his.

Anno Domini MCXLII

Foundation under Saint-Évroult

The priory was founded on the feast of Saint Bartholomew in 1142 by Hugues IV, lord of La Ferté, who gave to the abbey of Saint-Évroult-en-Ouche eighteen acres of pasture, the mill at Bocquencé, and the right to take stone from his quarry above the Charentonne. Twelve choir-monks were sent from the mother house under prior Anselme du Sap. Construction of the first conventual church, a modest cruciform building in the Romanesque manner, was completed before 1167.

Of this earliest fabric only the lower courses of the eastern apse survive, incorporated into the later chevet. The capitals of the crypt — four of which are preserved in the lapidary gallery — bear stiff palmette ornament close to that of Saint-Pierre-sur-Dives.

MCXCVIII — MCCXV

The Great Rebuilding

The decisive transformation of the priory belongs to the episcopate of Robert de Glanville, bishop of Lisieux, whose patronage between 1198 and 1215 financed the rebuilding of the cloister, the chapter house, and the upper stages of the church. Under the master mason Geoffroy de Lisieux a new vocabulary entered the Pays d'Ouche: pointed arcades on coupled colonnettes, ribbed quadripartite vaults, and refined stiff-leaf capitals carved in Caen stone.

It is the cloister of these years — eighty-four bays in four walks — that survives, in part, to the present day. The chapter house, vaulted in nine bays on a central pier, is the only complete monastic chapter house of its date in upper Normandy.

MCCC — MDC

Late Medieval Endowment

The priory weathered the depredations of the Hundred Years' War with the loss of its western range, burned in 1418 by an English foraging party, and the temporary departure of the community to Bernay. The fifteenth century brought a slow recovery under priors drawn from the Argouges and de Mathan families, marked by the insertion of large flamboyant tracery into the cloister windows and the installation, around 1486, of a polychrome retable of the Dormition now in the museum's central gallery.

By the end of the sixteenth century the community had fallen to seven monks. A reform under the Congregation of Saint-Maur in 1644 restored discipline and library, and Maurist scholarship copied many of the priory's earliest charters before they were lost.

Anno MDCCXCI

Suppression and Dispersal

By decree of the Constituent Assembly of 13 February 1790, and effective at La Ferté in March 1791, the priory was suppressed and its goods declared biens nationaux. The five remaining monks departed; the conventual buildings were sold at auction in October of that year to a local cloth merchant, Nicolas Lebailly, for 11,400 livres. The church and the western range were demolished between 1793 and 1796 for building stone.

The library — some 1,840 volumes, including a glossed Bible of c. 1190 and the autograph cartulary copied by Dom Vincent Le Tellier — was crated and removed to Alençon in 1804 by order of the Préfet, then dispersed during the Napoleonic re-organisation of the départemental collections. Forty-three manuscripts have since been identified in the libraries of Alençon, Caen, Rouen, and the Bibliothèque nationale.

MDCCCLXXIII

Acquisition by the Département

The surviving cloister and chapter house, by then serving as a cider press and cattle-byre, were acquired in 1873 by the département de l'Orne for the sum of 18,000 francs, on the recommendation of the antiquarian Léon de la Sicotière. They were classified monument historique in 1875. A first campaign of consolidation, supervised by the diocesan architect Eugène Ruprich-Robert, removed agricultural accretions and stabilised the vaults of the chapter house.

The collection of architectural fragments — capitals, voussoirs, tomb slabs, and the great tympanum of the Last Judgement recovered from a barn at Glos — was assembled in these years and forms the nucleus of the present museum.

MCMLVIII

The Froidevaux Restoration

The decisive modern intervention was undertaken between 1955 and 1958 under Yves Froidevaux, Architecte en chef des Monuments historiques. Froidevaux removed the nineteenth-century lean-to which had enclosed the southern walk, recut the missing tracery of seven cloister bays from surviving fragments, and reinstated the original paving of the chapter house. His campaign also opened the lapidary depot in the former refectory undercroft, where some four hundred carved fragments are now arranged.

The priory reopened to the public on 14 June 1958. Subsequent campaigns in 1987 and 2011 have addressed the polychromy of the chapter house keystones and the conservation of the surviving stained glass.

The Patron

Bishop Robert de Glanville

1198 – 1215

A younger son of the Anglo-Norman Glanville family, Robert was elected to the see of Lisieux in 1198 after a decade as cantor of Bayeux. His seventeen-year episcopate coincided with the most ambitious building campaign in the priory's history, financed in large part from his personal mensa and from a tithe granted by Philippe Auguste in 1204 on the salt-trade of the Touques.

Glanville's surviving register, preserved at Lisieux until 1944 and now known only through the eighteenth-century transcription of Dom Pommeraye, records seven separate visits to the priory between 1201 and 1213, and the dedication of the new chapter house at Whitsun 1208.

His tomb, originally before the high altar of the priory church, was destroyed in 1793; the gisant in our nave is an early-fourteenth-century replacement, possibly commissioned by his great-nephew.

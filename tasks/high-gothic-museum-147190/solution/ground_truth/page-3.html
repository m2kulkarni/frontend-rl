On Cobalt & Madder, Lead & Light

To stand at the foot of the western lancets of Chartres on a clear March morning is to be confronted, before any iconography, with a fact of pigment. The cobalt of the early thirteenth century — a colour whose chemistry was still imperfectly grasped by the masters who poured it — does not behave as later glass behaves. It absorbs the low northern sun and returns it as a weight, a substance one might almost touch. Beside it, the madder reds of the Tree of Jesse fragments, smelted with tin and a small admixture of copper, glow with the warmth of a banked fire. Between these two poles — the deep-water blue of the heavens and the arterial red of the genealogy of Christ — the entire programme of the early Chartrain workshop is staged.

The forty-two fragments assembled here, drawn from the reserves of the Centre international du vitrail and from a handful of departmental loans, span the four decades that followed the conflagration of 1194. They are not, for the most part, the famous panels: those remain in situ. They are the offcuts, the trial pieces, the sections salvaged during the restorations of 1872 and again during the campaigns of 1976–1981. Their importance is precisely that they are workshop matter. One sees, on the back of catalogue number XVII, the soft chalk-mark of a glazier's compass; on number XXIX, the unmistakeable scorch of a kiln set too hot.

"The lead-came is not a constraint upon the image — it is the image's first draughtsman, the black ink that precedes all colour."

The lead-came geometry of the Chartres lancets has been studied since Émile Mâle, but it bears restating that the came is not a constraint upon the image — it is the image's first draughtsman, the black ink that precedes all colour. The glazier of the Beauce drew with lead before he ever drew with brush. In the medallions of the Jesse, one observes how the came is permitted to swell at the joints, to thicken at the patriarchs' shoulders, to thin where a prophet's hand reaches toward the dove of the Spirit. The came's metallurgy — H-section, hand-drawn, its tin content varying from twelve to eighteen percent — gives it a flexibility our own age has lost.

The iconographic programme of the Tree of Jesse, as Chartres received it from the lost windows of Saint-Denis (c. 1144), is an essay in vertical reading. From the recumbent Jesse of the lower register, sleeping after the fashion of the Old Testament fathers, the trunk ascends through David, Solomon, and the twelve kings of Judah, each enthroned within a foliate quadrilobe, until at the summit Mary holds the Christ Child against the gold-leaf ground of glory. To either side, the prophets — Isaiah, Jeremiah, Daniel, Ezekiel and the rest — turn inward, their scrolls inscribed with the verses that the genealogy fulfils. It is an exegesis in glass.

What the visitor will notice, walking from fragment to fragment in the order of the catalogue, is that the cobalt is never a single cobalt. The blue of the background fields is one shade; the blue of Mary's mantle, another, slightly violet; the blue of the borders, a third, more saturated, almost black at the came. This is not the accident of weathering. The Chartrain glazier maintained a small but disciplined palette of three blues, three reds, two yellows (one true, one silver-stained), and a single green achieved by superimposition. Within so narrow a range, every variation of shade is intentional, and every intentional variation is, in the medieval sense, a meaning.

It remains for me only to thank the Centre international du vitrail, and in particular Madame Geneviève Aubry, for their extraordinary generosity in lending these works; and to commend to the visitor a slow, repeated passage through the rooms. The light at Saint-Évroult is not the light of Chartres, but it is a northern light, and at the right hour — between half-past three and four o'clock on a winter afternoon — these fragments remember what they were made for.

II · Checklist of Works

Forty-two Glass Fragments

A representative selection from the catalogue. The full checklist of all forty-two pieces, with technical annotations, is available in the printed brochure (€4) and at the welcome desk of the Salle du Chapitre.

Jesse Recumbent

Cat. nº I  ·  CIV-1194-A

Pot-metal cobalt and madder, with silver-stained yellow halo. From the lower register of the western Jesse lancet, c. 1196. The patriarch's beard is rendered in a single grisaille line of remarkable economy.

Centre international du vitrail, Chartres.

King David Enthroned

Cat. nº IV  ·  CIV-1198-D

Quadrilobe medallion, cobalt ground. Harp held in left hand; right hand raised in benediction. The lead-came work at the throne's lions is exceptionally fine. Loan from Beauce diocesan reserves.

Évêché de Chartres, dépôt 1976.

Prophet Isaiah

Cat. nº IX  ·  CIV-1201-F

Half-length figure, scroll inscribed EGREDIETUR VIRGA. Madder mantle over cobalt tunic. Restored 1872 by the workshop of Lobin of Tours; the restoration leads remain visible.

Centre international du vitrail, Chartres.

Solomon Crowned

Cat. nº XII  ·  CIV-1203-B

A small but jewelled fragment: the king's golden crown is set with three small roundels of pot-metal green, achieved by superimposition of yellow on cobalt. Earliest known instance of the technique at Chartres.

Musée des Beaux-Arts de Chartres.

Virgin & Child

Cat. nº XVII  ·  CIV-1207-C

Crown of the Tree, severely damaged 1568 and reset c. 1620. The reverse bears a chalked compass-mark of the original glazier — the exhibition's most intimate object. Cobalt mandorla, gold-leaf ground.

Centre international du vitrail, Chartres.

Border Quatrefoil

Cat. nº XXIX  ·  CIV-1217-K

Foliate border-piece in deep cobalt with madder counter-changes. The kiln-scorch on the left edge is the result of an over-fired second firing, c. 1218. Donated by the Chapter of Chartres in 1981.

Cathédrale Notre-Dame de Chartres.

III · Gallery Wall-texts

Reading the Lancets

Six interpretive panels accompany the visitor through the two galleries, with longer treatments in the printed catalogue (€18, available at the welcome desk).

Salle du Chapitre · Panel 1

The Fire of 1194

On the night of 10 June 1194, lightning struck the western tower of the Cathedral of Notre-Dame de Chartres, and the fire that followed consumed the timber roof, the clerestory, and a great quantity of the twelfth-century glazing of Bishop Fulbert's choir.

Within a week the legate Melior of Pisa had pledged Roman support; within a month the rebuilding had begun. The new lancets, conceived for an enlarged programme, were begun by 1196 and the western Jesse was in place by c. 1200.

Salle du Chapitre · Panel 2

Cobalt & the Eastern Trade

The cobalt blue of the Chartres lancets was made not at Chartres but in the small glass-houses of the Forêt d'Othe, south-east of Troyes, from ore brought along the trade-route that ran from Saxony down the Rhine and into the Île-de-France.

That ore — cobaltite mixed with arsenic — was roasted in the open air, the arsenic driven off as a poisonous smoke, and the cobalt oxide combined with potash and sand. The colour, once fired into the glass, is chemically permanent: eight centuries have not faded it.

Cellier-Nord · Panel 3

The Geometry of Lead

The H-section lead came of the early thirteenth century was hand-drawn through a die of pearwood, then hammered cold to harden the lateral wings. Its tin content — between twelve and eighteen percent by weight — was carefully maintained, since too much tin made the came brittle, and too little made it slump in the heat of summer.

The glazier worked on a wooden table, the cartoon drawn full-size in charcoal, and assembled the panel from the centre outward. The solder was tin-lead, applied with a heated iron and tallow flux.

Cellier-Nord · Panel 4

The Tree of Jesse

The iconography of the Tree of Jesse derives from Isaiah 11:1 — egredietur virga de radice Iesse, et flos de radice eius ascendet — and entered Western art through the Carolingian commentary of Hrabanus Maurus and the lost windows of Suger at Saint-Denis (c. 1144).

At Chartres the programme is set out vertically across nine registers: Jesse, David, Solomon, four kings of Judah, the Virgin, and the Christ Child. The seven flanking prophets are arranged not chronologically but by the relevance of their prophecies to the genealogy.

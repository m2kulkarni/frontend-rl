Domus · The House

The House

A modern Roman trattoria above the orange grove of the Aventine — built inside an 1881 olive-oil cellar by a sister and a brother, opened on the feast of Sant'Anselmo, MMXIV.

Anno Domini MMXIV

A Sister, A Brother, A Cellar

Lucia Ferri had been cooking in the kitchens of Trastevere for eleven years when she found the room. It was March of 2013, raining sideways, and the iron grate at Via di Sant'Anselmo 14 had been chained shut since 1974. The landlord, an octogenarian named Renzo Castellani, remembered the place as a fondaco di olio — an olive-oil cellar — built by his great-grandfather in 1881 to serve the convents of the Aventine. The barrel vaults still smelled faintly of pressed Sabina olives.

Her brother Tommaso Ferri, then a sommelier at a hotel near the Spanish Steps, walked the seventy-three steps down with her the following Sunday. They stood under the eight terracotta groin vaults for a long time and said almost nothing. By the end of that summer they had signed the lease, and on the 21st of April 2014 — the traditional birthday of Rome — Trattoria Aventina opened to thirty-six covers and a single tasting menu of six plates.

A decade on, the project remains essentially what they sketched on a napkin at Bar San Calisto: a Roman kitchen rooted in the four quinti of cucina romana, a cellar with the patience to hold its own bottles, and a room that asks nothing of the guest beyond an appetite and an evening.

I · Locus

The Room

Eight vaults of hand-laid Roman brick rise from a floor of cocciopesto the colour of dried fig. The cellar's original iron rings — once used to hoist amphorae of oil — still march along the spring-line of each arch; we have asked them only to hold candles.

Forty-eight covers occupy the long room, seated at oak tables milled from a single Umbrian trunk. The walls are bare lime plaster. There is no music after eight o'clock. The acoustics, which Tommaso insists were tuned by Roman masons who never read a textbook, return conversation at a courteous volume.

II · Focus

The Kitchen

At the heart of the cellar stands a wood-fired oven of grey Vesuvian stone, built in Sorrento by the Acunzo family in the winter of 2013 and trucked north on the Autostrada A1 across three days of February rain. It reaches 480°C by ten in the morning and holds it until midnight on a quiet diet of beech and Lazio oak.

Lucia's brigade is six strong. The pasta is rolled by hand each morning on a marble slab quarried at Carrara in 1962; the rigatoni for the amatriciana are cut to forty-two millimetres, no more. A copper pan from her grandmother's kitchen in Amatrice hangs above the pass — used, not displayed.

III · Forum

The Market

Every morning at six-twenty, a cook from the brigade walks the eleven minutes down to Mercato Testaccio. The list is short and it changes: puntarelle from Sezze in January, vignarola peas from Velletri in April, figs from the Castelli Romani in late August.

We buy whole lambs from Aldo at stall 47, ricotta di pecora from the Brunelli sisters at stall 12, and bread from Roscioli's bakery on Via dei Chiavari. What does not arrive on foot arrives on the back of a Piaggio Ape, which has, in ten years, not once been late.

"We did not invent anything. We swept a cellar, lit an oven, and asked the city to come down the steps."
— Lucia Ferri, Gambero Rosso, March MMXXII

Annales · A Decade in the Cellar

The Chronicle

MMXIV
Doors open on the feast of Sant'Anselmo, 21 April. Thirty-six covers, six plates.
MMXVI
First mention in Gambero Rosso. The wood-fired oven is re-lined in Sorrento stone.
MMXVIII
The cellar expands beneath the orange grove; 412 references catalogued.
MMXX
Closed eleven weeks. Reopens with a daily produce-box delivered across Rome by bicycle.
MMXXII
Bib Gourmand, Michelin Italia. Lucia is named Chef of the Year by L'Espresso.
MMXXIII
The amari programme launches: forty-eight bottles, all pre-MCMLXXX.
MMXXIV
Tenth anniversary. A new tasting menu, Decennium, €95 per guest.

Departmentum I · Ex Libris

Ex LibrisThe Monthly Classics-Press Review

by Anne-Marie Foucher, Maître de conférences, Sorbonne
September · October · November MMXXIV

Four books cross the desk this quarter, and three of them are worth your shelf. Princeton's reissue of Mary Beard's SPQR in a tenth-anniversary edition (cloth, $34.95) adds a new fifty-page postscript on the historiography of the provinces — the most useful thing she has written about Rome in a decade. Read it before the rest.

From Cambridge, Llewelyn Morgan's Ovid: A Very Short Introduction (paper, £8.99) is exactly what the format demands and no more: lean, literary, and unembarrassed by enthusiasm. He is particularly fine on the Tristia, which he reads, against the current, as the poet's best work. I am not certain he is wrong.

Yale's translation of Maurizio Bettini's Il dio elegante, now The Elegant God (cloth, $30), recovers Mercury as something stranger than the Olympian footnote we inherited from the handbooks — a god of the threshold, the verge, the awkward in-between. The English of Lucia Pasquini reads beautifully.

❦   ❦   ❦

The fourth book — Penguin's revised Aeneid in Shadi Bartsch's verse — has had its applause elsewhere. I will say only that I miss Fitzgerald, and that the new line is brisker, plainer, and (one suspects) destined for syllabi. A copy reached me with the Penguin jacket band already creased; even the publishers, it seems, expect their Vergils to be carried about.

Forthcoming, and warned of: Oxford's Cambridge Companion to Tacitus, ed. A. J. Woodman, January MMXXV; and a long-rumored Suetonius from Tom Holland, slated for the spring.

Departmentum III · Inscriptiones

InscriptionesOne stone, read closely

unpacked this issue by the editors
CIL VI 1199 · Via Appia, Mile III

A small travertine slab, broken on the right, recovered in 1873 from the third milestone of the Via Appia and now in the lapidarium of the Museo Nazionale Romano (inv. 115284). The text, in carefully cut capitals of the late second century, reads:

D · M · S
FELICVLAE LIB · DVL[CISS]
QVAE VIXIT ANN · XXIII
MENS · IIII · DIES · XI
FECIT · T · AELIVS · HER[MES]
CONIVGI · B · M · F
CIL VI 1199 (= ILS 8459)

“To the spirits of the departed. For Felicula, his sweetest freedwoman, who lived 23 years, 4 months, 11 days. Titus Aelius Hermes made this for his wife, well-deserving.”

The stone is small — 38 cm by 27 — and quite ordinary. That is its interest. Felicula was freed (liberta), then married her former master (coniugi); the formula bene merenti fecit is the commonest closure in Roman epitaphs and survives, slightly Christianized, into the fourth century. What the stone documents is the steep, ordinary trajectory of a Roman household: Hermes, himself almost certainly a Greek-named freedman of Hadrian's family (the nomen Aelius is the give-away), reaches manumission, buys and frees Felicula, and within a decade is burying her on the Appia.

The lacuna in line 5 has been read three ways. HER[MES] (Henzen, 1873) remains the standard; HER[ENNIANVS] (Mommsen, per litteras) is unlikely on spacing; HER[ACLIDA], proposed by Solin in 2009, is now the consensus where the family was of Bithynian origin. We retain Henzen.

Departmentum II · De Re Coquinaria

De Re CoquinariaRoman cookery, reconstructed

by Tullio Renzi, chef-proprietor, Trattoria del Pantheon, Roma
From Apicius, Lib. IV · Patina de piris

Apicius gives us a pear flan in seven lines and assumes you know the rest. I have been cooking it for fifteen years at the trattoria — autumn only, when the Volpina pears from the Veneto come in — and have come to think of it as the most honest dish in the corpus. There is no fraud in it. Pears, eggs, wine, pepper, a little honey: the kitchen of a senator who is not trying to impress anyone.

Patina de piris · for VI

  • 800 g ripe pears (Volpina or Bosc), peeled and cored
  • 6 large eggs
  • 120 ml passum (a sweet raisin wine; or vin santo)
  • 2 tbsp clear honey
  • ½ tsp finely ground long pepper
  • a pinch of cumin, ground
  • 1 tbsp olive oil, plus more for the dish
  • sea salt, scant

Poach the pears whole in lightly salted water for nine minutes; drain, cool, and pass through a food mill. (Apicius says teres, “grind”; a mortar will do, but the mill is faster and more honest about it.) Beat the eggs with the passum, honey, pepper, cumin, and salt; fold in the pear pulp. Oil an earthenware patina generously, pour in the mixture, and bake at 160°C for 35–40 minutes, until just set and faintly bronzed at the edges. Dust with more long pepper. Serve warm, never hot, with a glass of the same passum.

A note on the pepper. Apicius means piper longum, which the Romans imported by the ton from the Malabar coast and which is not what comes out of your mill at home. It is hotter, sweeter, and more floral than black pepper, and the dish does not work without it. Penzeys carries it; so does any decent Indian grocer. Buy whole and grind on the day.

Departmentum IV · Epistulae Lectorum

LettersFrom the Readers

replies to the Spring number's piece on Vitruvian proportion
Received April–July MMXXIV

Professor Halloran's essay (“The Module and the Man,” Issue XXXIX, pp. 14–29) provoked a postbag larger than any we have had since the Pompeii number. We print, with light editing, four of the more useful replies. The author's response will appear in Issue XLI.

From Prof. Indra Mehta · Yale, New Haven

Halloran is right that the De architectura is not a metaphysical document, and right to scold the Renaissance for treating it as one. But he overcorrects. Vitruvius does, in III.1, treat the body as a commensus — a measured agreement — and the word matters. To strip it of every Pythagorean echo is to flatten the prose into something Vitruvius himself would not recognize.

— with collegial regards.

From Mr. Giles Carrington · Bath, England

I read the piece on the train from Paddington and was struck by the footnote on Inigo Jones. May I add: the Banqueting House at Whitehall (1622) uses a column-to-intercolumniation ratio of almost exactly 1 : 2.25, which is closer to Vitruvius III.3 than anything Palladio actually built. I have measured it, in the rain, with a perfectly ordinary tape. The English Vitruvians were better readers than we credit.

From Sig.ra Carla De Luca · Roma

Una piccola correzione. Halloran writes that the Pantheon's interior diameter is 43.3 m. It is 43.44 m, measured by Lugli in 1938 and confirmed by the Soprintendenza in 2006. The fourteen centimetres are not nothing, when one is arguing about ratios.

— cordialmente.

From Mr. Daniel O'Hare · Brooklyn, NY

I am not a classicist — I am a carpenter — and I want to say that the article was the first thing about Vitruvius I have read that treated him as a working man with a working problem. Thank you. I keep the issue on the bench.

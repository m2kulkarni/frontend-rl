— Six Long Reads from Issue XLI —

Our principal essays for the spring quarter: hydraulic concrete read as theology; the painted walls of a vanished villa; Trajan's Column rendered as serial newsreel; and three further dispatches from the long afterlife of Rome.

Issue XLI · Six Essays · 22,400 Words
Edited by Helena Vaughan-Pryor

№ I Engineering · Religion · Material Culture

Concrete Gods Roman Hydraulic Lime and the Engineering of Belief

How pozzolanic mortar — quarried at Puteoli and slaked in seawater — let Roman engineers bind the marine basin at Caesarea, the dome at Baiae, and the ambitions of two emperors. A working theology of the mix.

№ II Painting · Archaeology · Campania

The Painted Walls of Oplontis A Villa Reconstructed Pigment by Pigment

Forty years of conservation at Villa Poppaea have yielded a Second-Style colour scheme no photograph has ever truly captured. A walk through cinnabar and Egyptian blue, room by room.

№ III Sculpture · Propaganda · Dacian Wars

Trajan's Column as Newsreel Reading 155 Scenes Frame by Frame

Long before cinema, the helical frieze on the Via dei Fori Imperiali edited a war into footage: cuts, establishing shots, and the Dacian campaigns staged for a Roman public that could only see the bottom thirty feet.

№ IV Law · Slavery · Social History

The Manumission Tablets of Herculaneum Freedom, Notarized and Filed in Triplicate

A small wooden archive recovered from the House of the Bicentenary records the legal moment a slave became a citizen. What the language of release tells us about a system that almost never released anyone.

Vindolanda, in Letters A Garrison's Birthday Invitations and Sock Requisitions

The wooden tablets of Northumberland describe a damp frontier in the most ordinary idiom: socks, sandals, beer, the prefect's wife asking Severa to come for her birthday. Empire as inventory list.

Mussolini's Forum, Unfinished The EUR District and the Long Shadow of Latinity

A travertine satellite city was meant to host a 1942 World's Fair that never happened. What survives is a strange, instructive monument to how regimes weaponize antiquity — and how antiquity outlives them.

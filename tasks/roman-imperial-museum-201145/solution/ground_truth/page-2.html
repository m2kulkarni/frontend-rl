Permanent Collection

Quattuor Salae · Four Halls of Imperial Rome

The permanent collection of the Antiquarium Traianum gathers more than three thousand objects of stone, bronze, and tessellated pavement, drawn from the imperial precincts of Rome and the cities of Italia. Across four contiguous halls the visitor traverses the Empire from the constitutional settlement of Augustus in 27 B.C. through the Severan reconstructions of the early third century — a span of nearly two and a half centuries of governance, ritual, and craft.

The Four Halls

Salae Quattuor Antiquarii

Sala degli Imperatori

Hall the First · Sala I

The Hall of Emperors

Eighty-four marble portraits trace the iconographic evolution of the imperial likeness from the chiselled, idealised features of Augustus — drawn from the Forum of Augustus type — through the verism of the Flavians, the philosopher-bearded Antonines, and the harsh, drilled curls of Caracalla. Heads from Tivoli, Velletri, and Ostia stand alongside three full-length cuirassed statues recovered from the Basilica Ulpia in 1937. Wall labels in Latin and the modern languages give the cursus honorum and known portrait conventions for each subject.

Featured · Pièce Maîtresse Cuirassed Trajan, after A.D. 108

Carrara marble, height 2.21 m. Excavated from the southern hemicycle of the Forum of Trajan in 1932; the breastplate bears in low relief a personification of conquered Dacia flanked by trophies.

Sala delle Iscrizioni

Hall the Second · Sala II

The Hall of Inscriptions

More than seven hundred Latin inscriptions are mounted along the long walls of the lapidarium, organised by genre: dedicatory, honorific, funerary, sacral, and military. Visitors will find the diplomata militaria of Antoninus Pius, three columbarium plaques from the slope of the Esquiline, and a group of fragmentary fasti consulares. Each stone is accompanied by a transcription expanding the abbreviated Latin and an English summary; rubbings of inaccessible faces are mounted alongside.

Featured · Pièce Maîtresse Funerary Altar of Q. Sulpicius Maximus, A.D. 94

White marble altar with Greek and Latin inscription commemorating an eleven-year-old boy who improvised hexameter verse at the Capitoline Games of Domitian. From the Porta Salaria, restored in 1996.

Sala dei Mosaici

Hall the Third · Sala III

The Hall of Mosaic Pavements

Twenty-two floor pavements, lifted whole or in numbered sections, are remounted at gallery level for direct viewing. The pavements derive principally from the insulae and thermae of Ostia Antica, with a smaller selection from the Vesuvian cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum. Geometric black-and-white compositions in the Ostian manner are juxtaposed against the polychrome figural emblemata of the Bay of Naples, allowing the visitor to register at once the regional grammar of the craft.

Featured · Pièce Maîtresse Pavement of the Seven Sages, c. A.D. 130

Polychrome opus tessellatum, 4.6 × 3.8 m, from the Caseggiato del Serapide at Ostia. Each philosopher is named in Greek capitals and accompanied by a witticism in Latin set within a tabula ansata.

Sala dell'Architettura

Hall the Fourth · Sala IV

The Hall of Architecture

A purpose-built gallery of three bays presents the constituent vocabulary of imperial public architecture: Corinthian and composite capitals from the Forum of Trajan, modillion cornices from the Templum Pacis, and coffered marble fragments from the Basilica of Maxentius. The eastern bay is dominated by full plaster casts of the Cancelleria reliefs, taken from the originals in the Vatican Museums; the western bay houses a 1:50 wooden reconstruction of the Markets of Trajan as proposed by Boni in 1907.

Featured · Pièce Maîtresse Cancelleria Reliefs, casts (originals A.D. 93–95)

Two large Domitianic state reliefs depicting the profectio and adventus of the emperor, with the figure of Domitian later recut into Nerva. Casts taken in 1971 with the permission of the Musei Vaticani.

A Note from the Curator

"Sic transit gloria mundi — yet the stone remembers what the chronicle forgets."

— Dott.ssa Livia Marcellina Verani, Curator of the Permanent Collection

The arrangement of the four halls follows neither strict chronology nor strict typology, but rather the visitor's path through the imperial idea itself: first the face of the prince, then the public word in stone, then the floor on which Roman life was lived, and finally the ordered framework of column and cornice that gave to Rome its visible discipline. New acquisitions are recorded each Kalends in the Bulletin of the Antiquarium, available without charge from the porter's lodge.

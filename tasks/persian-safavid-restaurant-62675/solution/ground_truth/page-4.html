Lineage — The Kitchen's Long Memory —

From the royal pavilions of Shah Abbas to a tiled hall in our own quarter — four hundred years of slow rice, charcoal smoke, and the dried-lime broth that never left the household pot.

◈ ◈

From the Royal Kitchens of Isfahan an essay on inheritance, in three measures

Our kitchen begins, as every Isfahani kitchen begins, on the long avenue of the Chahar Bagh, in the year 1598, when Shah Abbas I moved his capital south and ordered the building of a city whose squares would be measured by maydan and whose tables would be measured by saffron. The royal kitchens of the Safavid court were not one room but a guild of rooms — the matbakh-e khassa for the shah's own plate, the matbakh-e amma for the eight hundred who ate in his name each evening, and the cold pantry by the river where pomegranates were pressed and the dried limes of Hormuz were broken with the back of a copper spoon.

Three techniques walked out of those kitchens, through the servants' doors, and into the houses of the city, where they have stayed. The first is tahdig — rice rendered slowly against an oiled pot until its underside is the colour of a Tabriz brick — a discipline of patience taught not by recipe but by ear, listening for the moment the steam thins and the grain begins to sing. The second is the charcoal-pit kebab: lamb threaded on a flat skewer the width of a man's thumb, salted only, turned exactly four times above coals of vine-wood. The third, and the most quietly Persian of all, is the broth darkened with limoo amani — limes dried in the sun on the rooftops of Bandar Abbas — which gives our khoresh-e gheymeh and our ash-e reshteh the sour, almost smoky depth that no fresh citrus can imitate.

"A kitchen is a city's longest memory.
Forty columns stand on twenty — the rest is reflection."

What survived the four centuries between the court and our doorstep was carried not by chefs but by grandmothers — by the women of the Bazaar-e Bozorg in Isfahan and the lane households of Shiraz, who borrowed the royal techniques, simplified them for the home pot, and quietly improved them. The recipes we cook tonight are theirs, not the shah's: the saffron rice with sour cherries that the women of the Jolfa quarter have made for Nowruz since the Armenian merchants arrived in 1606; the slow-cooked fesenjan of pomegranate and walnut that came down from the Caspian villages; the herb-stew ghormeh sabzi which has no fixed recipe and yet no two cooks of Isfahan would mistake one another's.

We honour that lineage by refusing shortcuts. Our rice is rinsed seven times and parboiled in salted water before its second cooking. Our saffron is bloomed, never sprinkled. Our charcoal is vine-wood from the Shiraz vineyards, delivered on Tuesdays. And our dried limes, twelve kilograms of them in the dry-store at any moment, come from a single family in the south who have been splitting and drying them in the same way since the Qajar period.

The Hands at the Pot two cooks, one inheritance

Roya Tabrizi

Chef de Cuisine

Born in Tabriz in 1978 to a household of carpet-weavers and cooks, Roya trained for nine years at Naqsh-e Jahan, the Isfahani restaurant on the southern edge of the great square, where she rose from rice cook to head of the hot kitchen under the late chef Mahmoud Eslami.

In 2014 she moved to London for three seasons under Hélène Darroze at The Connaught, returning with an obstinate conviction that classical Persian cookery did not need to be reinterpreted — only respected. She has run our kitchen since the house opened.

Behnam Sarraf

Pastry & Sweets

Behnam is from Yazd, the city of confectioners, and learned the discipline of sugar from his uncle's qand-rizi workshop on Imam Khomeini Street, where rosewater syrup was cooked twice daily in copper basins from 1962 onward.

He brings to our table the small, exact sweets of the Safavid kitchen — sohan-e asali of saffron and pistachio, baklava-ye Yazdi with cardamom, and a rosewater faloodeh served only between May and September, when the snowmelt at the kitchen door is at its sweetest.

The Three Principles of the Pantry how we source — and why we refuse to compromise

— I —

Saffron

Pressed within ninety days of harvest, never older. Our threads come from a co-operative of fourteen growers outside Torbat-e Heydarieh, in Khorasan; we receive twelve grams each Friday, sealed in dark glass.

— II —

Rice

Domsiah from a single paddy in the Gilan lowlands, hand-pounded at the Karimi family mill near Lahijan. One harvest a year, eighty sacks reserved for our kitchen, no blending.

— III —

Lamb

From the Bakhtiari highlands at 2,400 metres, where the flocks are moved twice a year between summer and winter pasture. Whole carcasses, hung six days, butchered in our cold room each Tuesday morning.

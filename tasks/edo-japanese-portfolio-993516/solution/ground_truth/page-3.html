The Process

板・筆・馬連 Block, Brush,
and Baren.

Seven stages, four hands, one breath. From the first hanshita drawing brushed in Nara sumi to the final suri impression pressed by horse-tail baren — a single edition of forty prints can occupy the bench for ninety-six working days. What follows is the route the paper takes through this machiya in Nishijin.

壹Stage I

Hanshita

下絵 — the underdrawing
8–14 days · sumi on minogami

The hanshita is the master drawing — a single brushed sheet that determines every key line and every colour separation that will follow. Yashiro draws it twice: once on rough paper for composition, then again, more slowly, on a thin minogami sheet from Iwano Heizaburō's mill in Echizen. The second drawing is the one that will be sacrificed, pasted face-down to the cherrywood and abraded away to leave only its sumi traces.

Pigment for this stage is always Kobaien shōen-boku, a pinewood-soot ink stick ground fresh on a Tankei suzuri stone. Nothing else holds a clean line under the chisel.

貳Stage II

Hanshita-bari

版下貼り — pasting the drawing
a single afternoon · nori paste, patience

The drawing is brushed, face-down, onto a planed block of yamazakura — wild mountain cherry harvested from the southern slopes of Yoshino, kiln-dried for a minimum of four winters. The paste is shōfu-nori, wheat starch cooked thin enough to release the paper but strong enough to hold it overnight.

Once the paste sets, Yashiro rubs the back of the sheet with a fingertip soaked in camellia oil. The minogami fibres lift away in tiny rolls. What remains, ghost-clear on the wood, is the line the chisel will follow.

參Stage III

Hori — keyblock

彫り（主版） — carving the line
22–40 days · 9 chisels, 1 hangi-tō

The keyblock — omohan — carries the drawing's black line and nothing else. Yashiro carves it with a hangi-tō knife held like a writing brush, supported by the second knuckle of the index finger. Around the line, the wood is cleared with five widths of aisuki flat chisel and three U-gouges (marunomi) for the broad fields.

Two registration marks are cut at this stage: a right-angled kagi in the lower right corner of the block, and a straight hikitsuke bar along the bottom edge. Every subsequent block in the edition will be cut to receive these same two marks. They are the difference between a print and a smudge.

肆Stage IV

Kyōgō-zuri

校合摺り — proof impressions
3–6 days · 12 to 18 proofs pulled

From the finished keyblock, twelve to eighteen proofs are pulled in sumi onto thin gampi. These are not for sale; they are working drawings. Yashiro brushes each proof with a single colour intention — one for the indigo plate, one for the cinnabar, one for the soft pale yellow of kihada bark — marking out the area each separation block will need to carry.

Each marked proof is then pasted to its own block of yamazakura. A print of forty colours requires forty kyōgō and at least twenty-two blocks, since most blocks are carved on both faces.

伍Stage V

Hori — colour blocks

色版彫り — carving the separations
35–70 days · the longest bench

This is where the months go. Each colour block is cleared so that only the area that will print remains in relief — sometimes a leaf, sometimes a single eyelash. The two registration marks are cut last, with a square and a steel rule, never by eye.

For graduated washes — bokashi — a shallow channel is gouged at the colour edge to hold a reservoir of water. The pigment is brushed into it; the gradient happens on the block, not on the paper.

陸Stage VI

Kami-shime

紙締め — sizing & conditioning the paper
2 days · dōsa (gelatine + alum)

The edition paper is hōsho from Iwano's mill — 100% kōzo, 64 g/m², deckle-edged. Before printing, each sheet is brushed with dōsa, a thin solution of nikawa gelatine and alum, to set the surface so pigment sits where it is laid rather than wicking through the fibre.

The sized paper is then damped in stacks between two sheets of soaked newsprint and pressed under a flat board for a full night. By morning, every sheet in the stack has reached the same humidity. A drier sheet will refuse the colour; a wetter one will cockle.

柒Stage VII

Suri

摺り — the impression
9–18 days · baren, brush, breath

The damp sheet is laid into the kentō registration and pressed. Pigment is brushed onto the block with a circular maru-bake of deer hair, mixed wet with a touch of nori paste so that it holds where it is placed. The baren — a coiled cord of horse-tail wrapped in a fresh bamboo sheath — is pulled across the back of the paper in long arcs from the centre out.

One block, one colour, one pull. Then the sheet is lifted, hung to rest, returned to its damp stack, and the next block is inked. A print of forty colours is touched by Yashiro's hand at least eighty times before it is signed.

"The block remembers the hand. The paper remembers the block."

— Tomé Yashiro, studio notebook, autumn 2019
Pigment & Provenance

Three colours, three colourmen.

The studio buys from three houses only. None of them have websites; two are reached by letter. Their materials are what makes a Hangiten print look the way it does, and they are the reason a five-colour edition cannot be hurried.

Sumi

墨 — pinewood-soot ink, ground fresh

Stick ink from Kobaien of Nara, founded Tenshō 5 (1577). Yashiro keeps three grades on the bench: an aoboku for crisp keylines, a warmer chaboku for shadow, and a sōen pinewood stick reserved for proofing.

Kobaien · Nara · est. 1577

Ai — indigo

藍 — fermented sukumo, Awa province

Sukumo paste from Watanabe Yoshio, ai-shi of Tokushima — one of four remaining masters certified by the prefecture. The studio buys 1.8 kg each November, fermented in a cedar vat for one hundred days. The blue deepens with each impression; a single edition will draw down half a kilogram.

Watanabe Aizome · Tokushima

Shu — cinnabar

朱 — mercuric sulphide, levigated by hand

True cinnabar from the last colourman in Osaka's Doshōmachi quarter, Nakamura Enogu-ten, working since Bunkyū 3 (1863). Twelve grams per year, in a glass vial sealed with red wax. Synthetic cadmium is refused; the difference shows in raking light, twenty years on.

Nakamura Enogu-ten · Ōsaka · est. 1863
The bench

Tools the studio still uses daily.

Most are older than the printer. The hangi-tō was inherited from the sixth generation. The baren is rewrapped in fresh bamboo every nineteen impressions — a count the eighth generation marks in pencil on the studio wall.

Hangi-tō版木刀 · the carving knife
Single-bevel, sharpened on Aoto and Awasedo stones. Six in rotation; the oldest dates to 1908.
Aisuki合透き · flat clearing chisels
Five widths from 3 mm to 24 mm, by Tasai of Sanjō, forged 1971.
Maru-bake丸刷毛 · pigment brushes
Deer-belly hair, set in cherry handles by Hosoido of Tokyo. Re-haired every fourth edition.
Hon-baren本馬連 · horse-tail baren
Coiled hon-zuru core wrapped in takenokawa bamboo sheath, replaced ~every 19 pulls.
Yamazakura山桜 · wild mountain cherry blocks
Yoshino-grown, kiln-dried four winters minimum. Standard plate 36 × 24 × 2.4 cm.

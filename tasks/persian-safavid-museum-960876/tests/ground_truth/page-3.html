Cobalt & Cream
Tilework from the Maydān-i Naqsh-i Jahān

14 Mehr✦22 Esfand 1403
6 October 2024 — 12 March 2025
Curatorial Essay

From Seven Colours to a Single Mosaic

An exhibition tracing the chromatic and architectural shift in Isfahan's tile workshops at the turn of the seventeenth century.
By Dr. Farzaneh Mostofi, Chief Curator of Architecture & Decorative Arts

When Shāh ʿAbbās I redrew the southern face of his capital between 1598 and 1629, he did not merely commission a square — he commissioned a palette. The Maydān-i Naqsh-i Jahān, that long sheet of brick and water at the heart of Isfahan, became the proving ground for a generation of tile-cutters who would refine, abandon, and finally reinvent the surfaces of Persian sacred architecture.

This exhibition gathers ninety-four objects — full revetment panels, fragments of dado, working sketches on gridded paper, and the rare surviving glaze-trial sherds from the royal kiln at Junbadhān — to ask a single question. Why, within roughly four decades, did the workshops of Isfahan move away from the swift, painterly economy of haft-rang ("seven-colour") overglaze tiles, only to return to the slower, costlier, and structurally more demanding craft of muʿarraq mosaic faience?

The conventional answer — that mosaic was simply prestigious, and the Shāh could afford prestige — flattens what the surviving evidence shows to have been a sustained, technically argumentative period. The cream-ground spandrels of the Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque (begun 1011 AH / 1602 CE) sit in dialogue, not opposition, with the haft-rang revetments of the Imam Mosque half a generation later. The two techniques shared masters; they shared cartoons; in at least three documented cases on view here, they shared a single wall.

"The cobalt is never alone. It listens for a thread of cream beneath, and answers it."
— Ustād Ḥusayn Banna, attributed, c. 1614

The visitor will encounter, in the first gallery, the chromatic argument itself: ten panels arranged on a single ivory wall, ordered by date rather than provenance, so that the eye may track the slow draining of mustard-yellow and the steady ascendance of turquoise and that particular oxidised cobalt which Isfahani potters called lājvardī-yi shāhī, "the royal lapis." The second gallery turns from colour to hand, and to a single hand in particular: that of Ustād Ḥusayn Banna (fl. 1597–1629), whose signature — three palmettes locked into a six-pointed star — appears on twenty-two of the works gathered here, and whose workshop training, we now believe, accounts for the unusual coherence of the south-east spandrel programme of the Imam Mosque.

The closing gallery is quieter, and deliberately so. It contains the fragments — what was broken in the 1722 siege, what was lifted by nineteenth-century travellers, what was returned in the late twentieth century, and what still waits, on the floor of a Tehran storeroom, for the next conservator. Cobalt and cream, it turns out, are also the colours of patience.

Three Readings

Walls, Spandrels, and a Workshop Hand

The exhibition is divided into three gallery sequences, each anchored by a single monument and a single argument about technique.
Gallery I

The Sheikh Lotfollah Dome

Twelve revetment fragments and a full quarter-cartoon, on loan from the Bonyād-i Mostazafān archive, allow the dome's interior to be read as a single contracting spiral. The cream ground is original; the cobalt has been retouched twice.

12 objects · 1011–1028 AH

Gallery II

Spandrels of the Imam Mosque

Four full spandrel reconstructions, set at original height and angle, frame the gallery's central axis. The visitor is asked to compare the haft-rang of the eastern īwān with the muʿarraq of the qibla, separated by some eleven years and a single chief tile-master.

23 objects · 1020–1040 AH

Gallery III

The Hand of Ustād Ḥusayn Banna

Twenty-two signed pieces, four sketchbooks (three on first public exhibition), and the kiln-list of 1024 AH document a single workshop's production cycle. The closing wall offers a side-by-side of master and apprentice palmette, distinguishable by half a millimetre of stem.

38 objects · 1006–1038 AH

A Reading Room · Vol. IV

Architecture & Ornament

Four illustrated essays drawn from the Museum's bench of curators & visiting scholars, on the geometry, vegetal grammar, and epigraphic skin of the Safavid building.

Isfahan · 1501–1736Edited by N. FarahmandSpring 1402 / 2024
— Essay the First —

The Two-Centred Arch and the Iwan

by Dr. Mahdi Tabrīzī, Curator of Architecture
Diagram I The points of striking, set inward of the jambs by one-third the clear span, give the arch its slow rise and broken apex.

The Safavid arch is not a half-circle; nor is it the squat pointed arch of the early Saljūq mosques. It is a qaws-i dū-markazī — a two-centred arch — drawn from two compass points set inside the jambs of the opening, each striking an arc that meets its companion at a sharp apex. To the eye it reads as composure rather than as thrust. The weight comes down softly; the silhouette inscribes itself against the sky.

The form had ancestors in the Ilkhānid and Tīmūrid bays, but it was at the Maydān-i Naqsh-i Jahān, between 1602 and 1638, that the geometry was disciplined into a building grammar. Shāh ʿAbbās I and his master-builder ʿAli Akbar Iṣfahānī worked the arch into three registers: the colossal pīshṭāq portal, the iwan that recesses behind it, and the smaller arcaded niches that relieve the flanking walls.

The iwan is the deeper figure. A barrel-vaulted hall open on one face, it is at once threshold and room, oriented to the courtyard rather than to the street. In the Masjid-i Shāh the four iwans are not symmetrical in function — the qibla iwan swells into a domed sanctuary — but they are symmetrical in proportion, each rising to the same shoulder line, each crowned by the same stalactite muqarnas hood.

What modern photographs flatten, the visitor's body recovers: the iwan is a sectioned tunnel of cool air, its rear wall withdrawn into shadow, its tilework reading not as flat ornament but as weather — as a sky pulled down over the head. The two-centred arch is the instrument by which this is made.

"The pīshṭāq does not announce the building; it postpones it. One enters not a mosque but the idea of a mosque, three times over."

It is worth saying that the arch was also a constructive economy. Its lower haunches, being nearly vertical, allowed the masons to corbel inward with brick laid on edge before any centering was struck; the upper arcs, springing from a higher impost, could be turned over a light wooden form. The geometry served both the mind and the wage.

In situ Masjid-i Shāh, Isfahan: clear span of the qibla iwan, 22.4 m. Apex height above pavement, 33 m. Centring points struck 7.5 m within the jambs.
— Essay the Second —

Girih: Five Tiles & Their Permutations

by Prof. Shīrīn Behzādī, Department of Geometry & Pattern
The Five Each tile carries a fixed arrangement of internal strapwork at 36°/72° angles; matched edge-to-edge, the strapwork joins to give the apparent star-and-polygon field.

Girih, from the Persian for knot, is the name given by craftsmen to the family of geometric patterns generated by a small kit of polygonal tiles, each one inscribed with internal strap-lines at precise angles. The patterns appear, to the casual eye, to be drawn as a single continuous web of stars and zig-zag belts; in fact they are tiled.

The kit, as recovered by Necipoğlu's reading of the Topkapı Scroll (MS H. 1956) and corroborated by the patterns at the Friday Mosque of Isfahan, comprises five shapes: the regular decagon, the regular pentagon, the elongated hexagon, the rhombus, and the bow-tie. Their interior angles are all multiples of 36°. The moment one accepts this, what had looked like inspired complication reveals itself as combinatorial discipline.

The decoration on a Safavid spandrel — the half-arch field above the haunches — typically reads as a ten-pointed star surrounded by a rotating cluster of five-pointed stars and irregular hexagonal cells. The underlying tiling is invisible. The masons did not paint the tile-edges; they painted only the strapwork that crosses them. Erase the strapwork and you would see the tile-grid; erase the tile-grid (as the eye does) and you see only the knot.

This concealment is, I think, the argument of the pattern. It is not a demonstration of geometry; it is a veil drawn over geometry. The viewer is invited to suspect a hidden order without being given its key. The instinct is theological as well as aesthetic: the visible world as a cipher of the invisible.

"Five tiles, infinite walls. The Topkapı Scroll teaches the kit; the workshop teaches forgetfulness of the kit."

One technical note. The Safavid girih sometimes admits a sixth, "sub-girih" tile at smaller scale, fitted within the larger cells to produce a self-similar second pattern — a feature noticed by Lu and Steinhardt (2007) at the Darb-i Imām in Isfahan, and read by them as a quasi-crystalline tiling avant la lettre. Whether the masons knew this in our terms, or in theirs, is a question that lies outside what the brick will tell us.

Diagram II Strapwork (oxblood) crossing the decagonal tile boundary. The eye reads continuity; the workshop laid two units.
— Essay the Third —

Arabesque as Argument: Vine, Lotus, Palmette

by Dr. Yāsamīn Pāyandeh, Curator of Tilework
Three Stems

The arabesque is often described, even by sympathetic commentators, as decoration. It is not. It is an argument made in plant-form about the nature of growth — and, by extension, about the relation of the made world to its making.

Three vegetal motives carry the argument in the Safavid scheme. The spiralling vine, or islīmī, is the structural ground: a continuous stem that turns back on itself, throws subsidiary stems into the field, and never closes as a finite figure. The lotus, imported by way of Yuan and Ming porcelains and naturalised in Tabrīz under Shāh Ismāʿīl, supplies the bloom — frontal, flat, eight- or twelve-petalled, often in cobalt against ivory ground. The palmette, the half-fan of leaves splayed against an axis, supplies the cadence: it is the figure to which the stem returns when it needs to rest.

The argument these three together advance is that growth has no termini, only stations. Look at the spandrel of a Safavid tile-revetment: the vine enters the field at its lower corner, climbs, throws a palmette at the spring of the arch, climbs again, throws a lotus at the apex, descends. Where it meets the boundary of the field it does not stop; it folds back, turns, and re-enters the same space at a new angle.

"The vine is not painted to fill the panel. The panel is laid out to receive the vine."

This is the deep difference between a Safavid arabesque and, for instance, a Renaissance grotesque, with which it is sometimes superficially compared. The grotesque is heraldic and centred; it has a top and a bottom, a place where the eye is meant to rest. The arabesque has neither. The eye is taught to follow, not to settle. One critic has called this an infinity in the second person — addressed, not abstract.

Colour reinforces the lesson. The Safavid palette restricts the vine to a single hue (most often a dark cobalt or a turquoise), so that the eye cannot use colour to single out a hierarchy of stems. Every part of the plant is the plant.

A note on date The mature islīmī of Isfahan is anticipated in the cuerda seca tiles of the shrine of Shaykh Ṣafī al-Dīn at Ardabīl (c. 1540). By the construction of the Lutfullāh Mosque (1603–1619) the vocabulary is settled.
— Essay the Fourth —

The Calligraphic Band as Architecture

by Dr. Reza Ḥājj-Sayyid, Reader in Epigraphy
Diagram IV

The Safavid building is read as much as it is seen. A continuous calligraphic band — sometimes a single line of thuluth in white on cobalt, sometimes a doubled register with a square kūfic running below — encircles the drum of the dome, the spring of the iwan, and the threshold of the portal. These bands are not labels. They are structural members in the building's argument.

Consider the great inscription of the Masjid-i Shāh, signed by ʿAli-Riżā ʿAbbāsī in 1025 AH / 1616 CE, that runs round the interior of the qibla iwan. Its content is a Qurʾānic citation: the Throne Verse (II:255) and the opening of Sūrat al-Fatḥ. Its placement, however, is architectural. It marks the precise horizon at which the wall begins to curve into vault. The eye, which has been climbing the tiled dado, the spandrel, and the arch, meets the band as it would meet a cornice in a classical building: as the line at which weight is gathered and turned.

The band thus performs a double office. To the literate it delivers a text; to the illiterate it delivers a string-course. Neither office is decorative. The text consecrates the building ("and his Throne extends over the heavens and the earth" — placed at the line where the heavens of the dome begin); the string-course knits the storeys.

"What the architrave is to a Greek temple, the thuluth band is to the Isfahan iwan: not an addition to the tectonics, but their utterance."

It is also worth remarking what the band is not. It is not a frieze in the antique sense — it does not represent action — and it is not a tabula in the medieval Latin sense, set apart from its surround by a frame. It is continuous with the field. The same cobalt that fills the ground of the band fills the ground of the spandrel adjacent; the same gold rule-work that keylines the band keylines the arch.

One reads, finally, by walking. The Safavid inscription is too long to be taken in from a single station; the visitor must move, and the architecture moves the visitor — round the iwan, into the sanctuary, beneath the dome — at a tempo set by the rhythm of the script.

Hands ʿAli-Riżā ʿAbbāsī (d. c. 1628) is the chief calligrapher of the period; his rival, Mīr ʿImād al-Ḥasanī, kept primarily to manuscript work. The architectural hand is broader, more interlocked, designed to read at thirty paces.

A Select Bibliography

For the visitor who would carry the reading further
  • Necipoğlu, Gülru. The Topkapı Scroll — Geometry and Ornament in Islamic Architecture. Getty Center for the History of Art and the Humanities, Santa Monica, 1995. The indispensable study of MS H. 1956 and of the polygonal-tile method that underlies girih.
  • Blair, Sheila S., and Jonathan M. Bloom. The Art and Architecture of Islam, 1250–1800. Pelican History of Art, Yale University Press, New Haven & London, 1994. Chapters 7 and 8 remain the standard English-language survey of the Safavid building programme at Isfahan.
  • Pope, Arthur Upham, and Phyllis Ackerman, eds. A Survey of Persian Art from Prehistoric Times to the Present. 6 vols. Oxford University Press, London & New York, 1938–39 (suppl. vols. 1960–67). Volumes II and IV gather the foundational documentation of Safavid architecture and tilework; the photographic record alone repays a week's reading.
  • Lu, Peter J., and Paul J. Steinhardt. "Decagonal and Quasi-Crystalline Tilings in Medieval Islamic Architecture." Science 315 (23 Feb. 2007): 1106–1110.
  • Hillenbrand, Robert. Islamic Architecture: Form, Function and Meaning. Edinburgh University Press, 1994.
  • Babaie, Sussan. Isfahan and Its Palaces — Statecraft, Shiʿism and the Architecture of Conviviality in Early Modern Iran. Edinburgh University Press, 2008.
  • Soucek, Priscilla P. "The Arts of Calligraphy," in The Arts of the Book in Central Asia, ed. B. Gray, UNESCO/Shambhala, 1979.

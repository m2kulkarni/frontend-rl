— About the museum —

A House of Patient Looking

دیوانخانه ـ خانهٔ نگاه

Founded in 1968 around the bequest of the merchant-collector Mīrzā Hādī Tabrīzī, the Dīvānkhāneh has, for more than half a century, kept watch over the workshops, manuscripts, and courtly furnishings of Safavid Isfahan — a city that, between 1501 and 1736, was reputed in Persian to be nesf-e jahān, half the world.

EST. ۱۳۴۷ / 1968
i. history

From a Bequest to a Pavilion

1968 Founded by deed of bequest in the Tabrīzī family house on Khīābān-i Chahār-Bāgh, with twelve carpets, ninety-two ceramics, and a single illuminated Shāhnāma.

The museum opened its doors in the autumn of 1968, when the heirs of the Tabrīzī merchant family transferred to public trust the collection assembled over four generations of the silk-and-tile trade between Isfahan, Tabrīz, and the caravanserais of the Safavid royal road. The first galleries occupied two andarūnī rooms of the family's town house; admission was free, on Fridays only, by written request.

By the late 1990s the holdings had grown to some 4,300 objects, and the house could no longer hold them. In 2004, after a seven-year campaign of restoration directed by the architect Ferīdūn Sāmī, the museum took possession of the Bāgh-i Dīvānkhāneh, a late-seventeenth-century garden pavilion attributed to the workshop of Shaykh Bahāʾī's successors. Its long ayvān, twin tile-revetted arcades, and reflecting tank now form the spine of our permanent display.

Today the Dīvānkhāneh is a research museum first and a public gallery second. Our mandate, set by the founding deed and reaffirmed in the 2011 charter, is the study, conservation, and patient exhibition of the visual culture of Safavid Iran — with particular attention to the workshops of Isfahan under Shāh ʿAbbās I (r. 1588–1629) and his successors.

2004 Reopened in the restored Bāgh-i Dīvānkhāneh garden pavilion, after a seven-year structural campaign directed by the architect Ferīdūn Sāmī.
ii. curatorial departments

The Four Workshops

I
Tilework &
Architecture

Cuerda seca panels, haft-rang revetments, and the architectural fragments recovered from Maydān-i Naqsh-i Jahān between 1972 and 2009.

Keeper: Dr Bahrām Najafī
II
Manuscripts &
Painting

Illuminated Shāhnāma and Khamsa volumes, single-page album leaves, and the calligraphies of Mīr ʿImād and his successors.

Keeper: Dr Lālé Ḥosaynī
III
Metalwork
& Arms

Inlaid brass ewers, astrolabes of the Lahore-Isfahan school, and the dynastic arms of the qūrchī cavalry, including damascened sabre-blades.

Keeper: Dr Karīm Mostowfī
IV
Textiles
& Carpets

Polonaise silk-and-metal-thread carpets, kemḥā velvets of the Yazd looms, and a study collection of dyestuffs and undyed yarns.

Keeper: Dr Pūrān Eslāmī

iv. on conservation

“We hold that the Safavid object is not a relic but a slow voice; that conservation is a duty of patience, not a craft of erasure. What the workshops of Isfahan made for the eye of the Shāh, we keep for the eye of the careful visitor — repaired only where the object asks to be repaired, and never beyond the reach of a future hand that may know more than ours.”

— Article IV, Charter of 2011
v. scholarly access

The Reserves & Their Doors

For Scholars and Conservators

The Dīvānkhāneh keeps approximately 4,800 of its 5,200 catalogued objects in study reserves on the lower floor of the north arcade. Access is granted by written application to the Archive Office, ordinarily within fifteen working days, for credentialed researchers, museum colleagues, and registered postgraduate candidates.

Applications should describe the object or class of object requested, the period of consultation, and any conservation questions to be addressed. The Archive Office will respond with a reading-room schedule and, where appropriate, the conditions under which photography or sampling may be undertaken.

Postal correspondence and short-notice visits are not the same channel: please write first, even from within Isfahan.

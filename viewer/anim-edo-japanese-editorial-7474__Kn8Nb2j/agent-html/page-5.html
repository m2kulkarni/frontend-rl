第五篇 · 05FIELD REPORT · NAKASENDŌ

Tsumago-
juku, in the quiet
of January.

A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the snow.

Reported by
Ryōsuke Mori
Photographs by Sōen Tanaka

24 JANUARY 2024 · NAGISO

The bus from Nagiso Station lets you off at the lower end of Terashita-machi a little after three. The slate roofs (the silver of Fuji — said here) are already capped white from the morning's snow, and there is no one in sight: not the geography of the early-September 22:1, "Tsumago-juku has prepared, by accident, to be a vision."

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became Tsumago has been told often enough that the residents are tired of telling it, but it bears retelling. By the time the railway came in 1909, the post-town was abandoned to its fate. The post-town's geography of mountain folds — the things that kept it in the road era from being any use to a railway: a lateral pass — were now its salvation. There was no railway, no major road, and so by 1968 nothing of the high-Edo had been displaced, only neglected.

The principles let many, in a polished form, in the entryway of the Tsumago-juku Preservation Society. Houses are not to be sold; they are not to be rented; renovations are not to be visible. Even a sign cannot be added (a wooden sign hung centrally over the door — yes, but a small enamel plate stating opening hours can not). The house residents adopted what they called the cooperative iezukuri: the entire town's shared houses to be done by the same agreed-upon means.

第二節 · II — A SURVEY OF MEALWORK

Gohei-mochi, miso, walnut

I stayed at the Matsushiro Inn, four rooms above a restaurant that has served travelers, by the proprietress's reckoning, since 1834. Dinner at the second night was gohei-mochi — pounded rice pressed flat onto a hot cedar paddle, brushed with a miso-and-walnut paste — alongside a bowl of miso-shiru from a kettle older than the gas stove that lights it. The glaze at Matsushiro uses the locally-grown walnuts that previous generations milled with a local stone-grinder, the stone-grinder retired but kept inside its shed.

The meal arrived on a tray of unfinished hinoki, with pickled nasunazuke, a small grilled iwana trout, and a bowl of homemade sukiyaki. Service was perfunctory. Inn-style: served at the bottom of the cedar steps. The room was chilly. There was no chatter. The proprietress's husband took the tray, the proprietress recommended that we not take it back to the kitchen.

On the morning of departure I returned the snow against the inner edge of the Matsushiro signboard with a brittle horsehair brush borrowed from the head of the bookmen, of the supper. The Regional Reginald Forecast Office reported forty-nine for the day; cabins as a wide white. The Agency again; nori rains. The town's reverent on annoy.

I left on the first bus, just back from the early shovel through the snow at 06:31 to the post-load to the line of the lawyers. The bus accelerated into a slow forced through trees, all to the same as the curve in.

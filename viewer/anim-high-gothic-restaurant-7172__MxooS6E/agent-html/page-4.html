OF THE STONES

The building at 14 Rue des Lombards was raised in 1247 as the western annex to the chapter-house of Saint-Merri, where the canons assembled to read the rule and to take their evening meal in silence. The ribbed plaster vault, restored in 1864 by the architect Pol Bourgéry, survives nearly entire above the present dining room.

After the suppression of 1791 the stones passed through the hands of a glaze-maker, a printer's foundry, and — for forty-three years — the Maison Cosson et Fils, dealers in candle-wax and sealing-wax in the manner of the Marais. The trade gave the building its smell of beeswax, which has never quite left.

In the spring of 1981 the rooms stood empty, the front shutters closed for nine years. Marcélile Charpentier, lately departed from Le Vase d'Argent after the rotis season under Claude Terrail, took the lease in September of that year.

OF THE KITCHEN

Marcélile opened the doors of the Maison de la Verrière on the eve of Saint-Martin, 1982, with a kitchen of four cooks and a card of nine dishes — among them the entremets en velours and the tarte de la haute Joue which remain on the menu to this day.

The cookery is drawn, line by line, from three sources: the kitchen rolls of the Archbishop of Reims for the years 1389 to 1421; the Viandier of Guillaume Tirel, called Taillevent; and the orchard accounts of the Cistercian house at Mortemer in the Vexin, which named seventy-one varieties of pear.

Nothing arrives on the plate that could not, in principle, have been served at a Lenten chapter in the year 1400 — saving only the New World tomato, which the Maison declines.

THE DINING ROOM

One enters from the street through a low door of oak, set with iron straps copied from the gate of the church of Saint-Julien-le-Pauvre. Within, the room is long, narrow, and tall — proportions inherited from its monastic use — and the walls are washed with a dry cobalt distemper made for the house by the studio of Atelier in Chartres.

The tables are oak refectory boards from the dissolved priory of Saint-Mihiel near Soissons, six in number, each seating seven. They were acquired at the dispersal of 1968 and bear the priest's notch-mark on the underside.

On a winter evening, with the eighty-four candles lit and the kitchen door swinging, one might be saving supper to the year of the Black Prince. — LE FIGARO, RÉOUVRANT, 1982

No electric lamp burns in the dining room. Light comes from eighty-four candles, set in iron sconces on the walls and in oak candle-trees on each table, lit by Henri Beauvilliers at the o'clock and replaced before service every third night.

Hokusai's
Wave, Read
Slowly

By Kenji Akiyama · Photographs courtesy of Kanagawa Prefectural Museum of Modern Art
Published: 18 SEPTEMBER 2024 · 12 MIN READ

For two centuries the West has read Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura as a portrait of menace — an instant before catastrophe, a wave poised to devour three slender boats. This is, I want to argue, an almost wilful misreading. The wave is not falling. It is repeating.

執筆 · WRITTENKenji Akiyama
資料 · ARCHIVEKanagawa MMA
編集 · EDITM. Endō
公開 · PUBLISHED18 SEP 2024

To read Nami-ura as a still-frame catastrophe is to mistake the woodblock for a photograph — to read Hokusai as a frozen instant. He is not a frozen instant. The form of the seventh wave on the seventh second of an October morning, painted in 1831, in a series of thirty-six views of Mt Fuji.

Consider the boats. There are three — oshiokuribune, the fast cargo skiffs that ran fish from the Izu peninsula to Edo. They are bowed not by panic but by acceleration: a rower's stroke has just been completed. The crews lean forward, their oars already lifted out of trough — into chamber. Hokusai is not drawing fear. He is drawing form.

The Wave is not falling. It is repeating. And what the woodblock asks of us is not horror but slow attention.

The Prussian blue — the colour Hokusai paid for, in 1830, with a year's gross income — entered the empire from Berlin via Nagasaki and was a luxury imported pigment. The faintest cyan in the tip of the foam claw is the same Prussian blue, diluted at twenty-to-one in rice paste, run through one of the lightest impressions Hokusai ever permitted his block-cutters to make. It is the most expensive pigment in the print, used for nothing more weighty than the suggestion of foam.

Look at the negative space. The mountain does not disappear into the wave; it is held in the wave. The same arc that bears the foam claws bears Mt Fuji. The wave is not threatening the mountain — the wave is the mountain, in another medium.

What I want to propose, then — and what the front cover of every Hokusai compendium published in the West for the past forty years denies us — is that the wave is not falling at all. It has not fallen. It will not fall. It is, instead, the repeating shape of the world that holds Mt Fuji.

TWO CLOSING ARGUMENTS, COMPRESSED
"But surely the wave is about to fall."
"Hokusai feared the sea — see his late letters."

No. But the wave's eternal poise is not denial of the sea's ferocity. The print does not ask us to forget that the sea kills. It asks only that we hold the moment between intention and gravity long enough to recognise it as a moment — that, in fact, is what the print is for. — K.A.

The Fifty-Three
Stations,
Two Hundred
Years On

By Mariko Sato  ·  ENGLISH · 日本語  ·  LEAD ESSAY  ·  14 MIN READ
執筆 · WRITTEN BYMariko Sato
写真 · PHOTOGRAPHSS. Tanizaki
編集 · EDITORSH. Ueda · K. Mori
公開 · PUBLISHED10 SEP 2024

A t 5:42 on the morning of the third of May, with the bronze plaque of Nihonbashi still cold under our palm, I shouldered a thirteen-kilo pack and set out for Kyoto on foot — a journey Hiroshige rendered in fifty-five woodblock prints in 1833. Within a decade the Tokugawa shogunate were expected to finish in three days. I gave myself twenty-three.

The Tōkaidō, the eastern sea-road, was always more idea than infrastructure. By the time Utagawa Hiroshige published his Gojūsan-tsugi series with the Hōeidō publishing house, the route had been formally surveyed for two centuries. Fifty-three official post-stations dotted the imperial capital, each with its honjin for daimyo retinues, its rice merchants, its bathhouses, its quarrels. What Hiroshige did was less to document the road than to translate it — the road as weather, as encounter, as the slight hesitation of a porter at a river crossing.

The First Hour Out of Edo

Hiroshige's prints move in time and feel the weather of a morning still wet from rain. Shinagawa, the first plate after Nihonbashi, shows a daimyō procession bottle-necked at low tide, the porters' yawns more vivid than the lacquer of the palanquins. The print is signed in the lower-right corner with the date and Hiroshige's seal, and the carving block was demolished in 1923 in the great Kantō earthquake.

I carried two books, Smith and Foster's 1986 translation of the series, with its lavish foldouts of the Hōeidō edition, and a 1974 walking guide by the Shizuoka Prefectural Tourist Bureau, which I had bought for ¥400 at a Jinbōchō second-hand shop the week before leaving. The guide was thirty years older than I was. I assumed I would still find rest-houses in Yui.

Hiroshige's genius was to draw not the road but the breathing of being on it — the moment a porter shifts his weight and the river briefly stops being only a river.

The Lake, the Pass, the Print

The fiftieth print of the Hōeidō series, Tsuchiyama at Haru no ame, shows a string of porters bent under green oilcloths in a spring downpour, the trail underfoot the colour of bruised plum. We arrived in Tsuchiyama on the seventeenth day with the cloud-cover stalled in the pass exactly as Hiroshige had drawn it.

Where the Inn Still Stands

Of the fifty-three stations Hiroshige drew, eleven preserve buildings of nineteenth-century stock; only one — Seki — preserves an entire honjin block intact. The remaining seven hold the tatemae of a station: a whitewashed road, a swept gable, a lantern lit at four. Forty-two have been overrun by the highway, by motorways, by the patient erosive logic of the Tōkaidō Shinkansen, which carries a passenger from Tokyo to Kyoto faster than Hiroshige could draw a single bridge.

I had walked four hundred and ninety kilometers to arrive in a town where I could find more information about a man whose face is unrecorded than about anyone living. I did not shrug especially well.

Arrival, Without Ceremony

On the twenty-third day, at 4:11 in the afternoon, with the sky over Sanjō Ōhashi the colour of dilute ink and the brass of the bridge-rail warm against the touch of a hand, I set down the pack and ate a rice ball that had been sold to me by a woman in Yamashina who refused payment. The road had ended, and like every long road, it ended by becoming a place.

— Mariko Sato spent twenty-three days walking the Edo–Kyoto route in May 2024, supported in part by the Hori Wood-block Foundation. Photographs by Shun Tanizaki, Hōeidō plates courtesy of the Tokyo National Museum.

Five Stations Where the Honjin Still Stands

Seki — 関宿
Forty-seventh post-town; near complete street of honjin and tea-vendors, restored by villagers in 1969.
Ōiso — Kambara
Two adjoining honjin, the oldest by far, walls finished in unrendered earth.
Goyu — Akasaka
Twin lodges separated by 1.7 km of pine; both still owned by descendant families.
Tsumago — Magome
Strictly speaking Nakasendō but printed by Hiroshige's pupils as part of a parallel set.
Mitsuke
Reduced but extant: the honjin's outer gate dates to 1804.

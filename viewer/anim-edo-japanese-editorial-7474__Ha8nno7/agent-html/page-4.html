Issue XIV · III

Hokusai's
Wave, Read
Slowly

By Kenji Akiyama
Contributing Editor · Edo print history
12 minute read · 28 October 2024
PRINT
Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura
Under the Wave off Kanagawa
SERIES
Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji
富嶽三十六景 · 1831
FIRST PRINTING
~ 8,000 impressions
PIGMENTS
Berlin / Prussian blue
Sumi · iron-gall

For two centuries the West has read Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura as a portrait of menace — an instant before catastrophe. I want to argue, on almost wilful misreading. The wave is not falling. It is repeating.

To stand four to a hair from Japanese — I had felt it on the spine cover of the Kashio Bunkan library at the Met — is to consider the print first as a sequence of decisions, second as a glance. We examine the 1831 keyblock — Prussian blue, foam claws, the sliver of Fuji — and trace how Katsushika's geometry of pressure made a single sheet outlast its empire.

This is not a metaphor I am exporting. The insights for the registration in registers are pulled from a 1916 reading by an actual ukiyo-e scholar, Tsuneji Sasaki, who interpreted Hokusai as a printmaker before he was a painter — fascinated less by what the wave ought to dramatize than by what the printer had to register on the woodblock to do it.

To stand four to a hair from Japanese — I had felt it on the spine of the Kashio Bunkan library at the Met — is to read the print first as a sequence of decisions, second as a glance. The Berlin trading of indigo, I argue, is a paradox of the printmaker's century rather than the dramatic gesture. 中国 indebtedness for the pigment, fragile, comes through the Dutch trading post at Dejima — not invented in 1791 by Heinrich Diesbach in Berlin, in a year that Edo would not be able to use it for another forty years. Hokusai's eight-printing of Nami-ura is the first work in which the pigment had been let out, late in his life, into the open air.

The Berlin trading of indigo, I argue, is a paradox of the printmaker's century rather than the dramatic gesture. So one is allowed to imagine that the wave, hung in the centre of the colour, ought to be carrying not a threat, but a kind of triumph: that the pigment had crossed the world in a barrel and been laid down by twenty different printers and was now, in this sheet, finally home.

The wave is not a wave. It is a mass-being inflected — water performing its geometry.

Read the way the printer registered the bands of foam: it stops the eye in a motion, but the white parts the wave have. The bands are not running; they are a marker of where pressure was placed and where it was not. They are the printer's evidence of touch.

Consider the foam. There are forty-seven 'claws' — and the eye reads them as motion, but they begin painted with no centroid and registered with no offset. They are signed twice; first in pencil, then in pressure.

FROM THE SERIES
弐 · II
PRINT №2 · 凱風快晴

The Cyan Cure

Beginning in 1831 Hokusai's bibliography is reset by the printer Eijudō, in a year when nothing else but indigo arrived in the Edo dockyards. Reading the cyan, you can read what month it landed, by what boat.

参 · III
PRINT №3 · 凱風

The Three Shifts

Eight printers running double-shift through 1832 to keep up with demand. The keyblock would be re-cut twice; the proofing block thirty-eight times. By the close of 1833, the demand had outrun the original block.

参 · IV
PRINT №4 · 雪中

Mt. Fuji, in Scale

The placement of Fuji at one-twelfth of canvas is a printerly judgement, not a sketcher's. From the printer's bench, the print teaches the painter where the print needed something solid to register against.

"But surely the wave is about to fall."
"Hokusai feared the sea — see his late letters."

A note: my readers from the field of art history will find this an indulgent extrapolation. So in their context, will the printer's children of Edo. The print, in any case, is at least 80% pigment and 20% argument. I have left the argument visible, the pigment unmolested.

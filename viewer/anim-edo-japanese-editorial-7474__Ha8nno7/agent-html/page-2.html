The Fifty-Three
Stations,
Two Hundred
Years On

By Mariko Sato
Published 27 October 2024
Five Stations Where the People Still Stayed

A walk from Nihonbashi to Sanjō Ōhashi finds five inns that never stopped serving travelers — and a sixth that never reopened.

FROM THE ESSAY
"The road was thinner than the prints suggest — five paces wide, scuffed at the edges by a thousand sandalled feet."
SECTIONS

A chill in the morning of the first day. Mist on the bronze plaque of Nihonbashi still cold under my palm at six o'clock. I begin where Hiroshige began — at the foot of the bridge that, by the woodblock count, was zero of fifty-three. The number means nothing now. The tide along the Sumida has moved on. The fish market that funded the bridge's reconstruction has decamped to Toyosu. What survives is the slope of the road and the obstinate tilt of the wood lintels above the noodle shop on the right bank, where the proprietor still hands you tea before he asks where you are going.

I leave at six-twelve. My pack is light. The first photograph I take, by accident, is the same composition Hiroshige drew at his second printing. Something in the way one stands on this stone, holding a bowl, has not changed in two centuries.

The First Hour Out of Edo

What follows is a question for anyone who has walked the road for more than its scenery: how much of the picture is the picture, and how much is the road insisting on being seen the way the print taught us? Hiroshige's genius was to draw what he saw and to teach us to see what he had drawn. Two hundred years on, you cannot distinguish your impression from his.

I suspect this is not a failure of the eye. It is a small, accurate fidelity. The traveler who walks past Shinagawa at first light, lake to her left, sees what every traveler since 1832 has been told to see.

The Lake, the Pass, the Print

Hiroshige's genius was to draw what he saw and to teach us to see what he had drawn. Two hundred years on, you cannot distinguish your impression from his. The bend at Kanagawa-juku where Tōkaidō meets the bay is now a pedestrian crossing under an elevated rail line, and yet for a moment, looking westward toward where the post-house stood, I feel the print rather than see it. The road insists on its old gestures even when the buildings refuse them.

Where the Inns Still Stand

Of the fifty-three honjin and 248 waki-honjin recorded in the Tenpō register, fewer than a dozen remain in any form. Three are museums; two are inns the public can still book; the rest survive as foundation stones, name-plaques, the occasional cedar beam reused in a postwar reconstruction. The map I am carrying is from the 1843 survey, redrawn for walkers in 1986. It is wrong about the river crossings, accurate about the gradient, and entirely silent on the matter of vending machines, which have replaced the rest stops at intervals of about 1.4 kilometers.

Arrival, Without Ceremony

I had walked four hundred and twelve kilometers when I stopped trying to count. The afternoon I crossed the Kamogawa into Sanjō it was raining hard enough that my map was illegible and my notebook was a soft brick. I did not photograph the bridge. I bought tea at a window from a woman who did not ask where I had walked from. I drank it under an awning and watched two students cross with their school bags held over their heads. The road ended exactly where it began: in nothing in particular, in a city full of weather, and a long string of names behind me that the map calls stations.

Indigo at Aizome-an

The fifth-
generation
dyer who refused
synthetic blue

By Hana Ueda
Photographs by S. Tanizaki · 2,400 words · 9 minute read

Shōko Furuyama tends Sukumo-Senshi, a 200-year-old earthenware vat sunk into the floor of a Nishijin machiya. She is the fifth Furuyama to do so. The vat, she explains, has not been temperature-cycled since 1834.

I

The Vat

Plate 1 · Aizome-an, fifth dip · S. Tanizaki, 2024

That dawn in 1834 was, in part, what set the precedence here. The vat fed from the rice-straw lye Shōko's great-great-grandfather built into the kitchen flue. They never let the fermentation drop below twenty-six degrees and never raise it above twenty-nine. Around the lid, Shōko keeps a hand-thermometer that her father kept, that his father carried, marked in shaku rather than centimeters.

"You don't manage indigo," she tells me on the third day. "You apologise to it." She has just lifted a sheet of cotton from the vat. It comes out yellow-green. We wait three minutes. The cloth oxidises. Now it is the colour of a winter sky at five o'clock — a blue with grey under it, a blue you can almost hear.

A Conversation, Transcribed

I ask her about the synthetic-indigo question — the one Furuya is half tired of and half determined to keep answering.

"Synthetic is faster. It's cheaper. It does not breathe with the cotton. It sits on top of the fibre rather than inside it. After ten washes the difference is no longer subtle. After twenty, it is the difference between a story and a copy."

I ask whether she's tempted, in any season.

"Tempted in October when the sukumo is curing. Tempted in February when the vat is sluggish. Then I think of my grandfather, who was tempted in 1948 when there was no money for charcoal, and refused, and I am embarrassed to be tempted in any year that has heating."

The vat is fed weekly with rice bran, slaked lime, and ash from the kiln. The temperature is held by the body of the building.

"The bath has been fermenting since 1834. My job is not to dye cloth. My job is to keep something alive long enough to hand it to whoever comes next."
— SHŌKO FURUYAMA · MARCH 2024

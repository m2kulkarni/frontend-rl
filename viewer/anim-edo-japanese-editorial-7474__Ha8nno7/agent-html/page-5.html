A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the snow.

Tsumago is the 42nd of 69 post-towns on the Nakasendō from Edo to Kyoto. It first appears in the 1602 register.

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became Tsumago has been told often enough that the residents are tired of telling it, but it bears repeating: by the late 1960s, Tsumago was emptied to fewer than 1,500 residents. The post-town and the railway both bypassed it after the Meiji restoration. By 1968, after seven hostels were demolished, six houses, the post office at the centre of the town and most outlying farms — building eaves dancing the eyes that should have been the most. Gohei-mochi, miso, walnut

I stayed at the Matsushiro Inn, four rooms above a restaurant that has served travelers, by the proprietress's reckoning, since 1851. Dinner on the second night was gohei-mochi — pounded rice pressed flat onto a flat cedar paddle, brushed with a miso-and-walnut sauce, and grilled over fire fed only with cherrywood and pine. The glaze at Matsushiro uses local Shintai miso and walnuts from the orchards above Mishima, the proprietress, Mrs. Kobayashi, refused to discuss the proportions for our notebook.

The meal arrived on a tray of unfinished hinoki, with parboiled snowwater, a little grilled trout, in fact picked yarn meal, a bowl of miso-shiro with the slate roofs in the snow. The room had eight tatami, the snow falling under for an hour the cedar trees breathed.

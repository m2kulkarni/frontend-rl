Indigo at Aizome-an

The fifth-
generation
dyer who refused
synthetic blue

Shoko Furuya works the kerokue beside a 200-year-old earthenware vat sunk into the dirt floor of a workshop her great-great-grandfather built. She is the fifth Furuya, and her four daughters have begun (she says) to ask why.

I

That Shoko returned at all in 2008 — when her parents had already begun to hint, with the polite incaution that suits Kyoto, that the workshop might close — was less the result of any decisive moment and more, she says, of a slow accumulation: the rooms above the workshop got too quiet for her, the silence of the place becoming, in her absence, the same kind of attention her father had paid it for forty years.

"The bath has been fermenting since 1834," she says without looking up. "My job is not to dye cloth. My job is to keep something alive long enough to hand it to whoever comes next."

The first vat in the room is the oldest and the deepest. It is set in concrete, lined in earthenware, and contains, at any given moment, somewhere between four and five hundred litres of fermented sukumo, lye, wheat-bran, and lime. The smell — of warm hay, of something marine — is the first thing that resists translation.

A Conversation, Transcribed

You learned to dye from your father — but you trained as a graphic designer in Osaka. What changed?
My father would never have admitted needing me. But the day I came back, he showed me how to lift a bolt at the third dip without disturbing the foam, and I knew he had been waiting two years to show somebody.
Why refuse synthetic indigo? Most aizome studios blend now.
Synthetic dye is a colour. Sukumo is a culture — the bath is alive, you feed it sake some nights, you talk to it. To swap one for the other is a kind of small dishonesty I cannot pay the rent on.
You said the bath is alive. What does that mean, in practice, in October?
It means the colour I get tomorrow depends on how cold the workshop was at five this morning, which depends on whether I closed the back doors last night, which depends on whether I remembered to check the east window after the rain.
Have your daughters expressed an interest in continuing?
Two have, two have not. I will tell you the same thing my father told me — that the choice is not the work but the patience, and the patience is something only the work can teach.
If you had to describe Japanese indigo to someone who had never seen it?
I would say: it is the colour of a winter sky, an hour before the lanterns are lit, in a city you have never visited but have been told about by your grandmother.
And the smell?
I have stopped trying to describe it to people who have not stood next to the vat. It is the smell of something ten generations old, deciding whether to wake up.

DYE GRADE

Three depths in a Furuya bolt

A traditional sukumo vat yields colour in stages. Hanada, the palest, is two dips; kon, the standard middle, is six to eight; kachi-iro, the saturate near-black, takes thirty.

VOCABULARY

Sukumo · fermented Polygonum tinctorium leaves, the dye stock
Aigame · the vat
Murago · a poor batch, said with a shrug

藍 · I · DICTIONARY
Pale Hanada
The lightest acceptable depth, achieved in two dips. Used for under-summer linings and the inside of children's haori.
藍 · II · DICTIONARY
Deep Kon
The standard middle, six to eight dips. The colour most westerners think of as "Japan blue." Used for half of all yukata sold in Kyoto.
藍 · III · DICTIONARY
Warrior's Blue
Kachi-iro: thirty dips, near black, almost iridescent. Once worn under armour because it was thought (correctly) to repel insects.

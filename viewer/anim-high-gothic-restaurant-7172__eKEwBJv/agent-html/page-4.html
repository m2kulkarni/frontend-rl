OF THE STONES

The building at 14 Rue des Lombards was raised in 1611 on the western annex to the chapter-house of Saint-Méry, where the canons assembled to read the rule and to take their evening meal in silence. The east-end pillars vault, restored in 1834, are still pierced by a corbel of the burial brothers, and a key-stone clearly inscribed in the abbreviated Roman.

After the suppression of 1793 the stones passed through the hands of a glass-master, a printer's foundry, and — for forty-three years — the Maison Tisserand, fils, dealers in candle-wax and sealing-wax in the manner of the Reims canon. The trade gave the building its smell of beeswax, which has never quite left.

In the spring of 1981 the rooms stood empty, the front shutters closed for two years. Mathilde Charpentier, lately departed from the Tour d'Argent after she met masons under Claude Terrail, took the lease in September of that year.

OF THE KITCHEN

Mathilde opened the doors of the Maison de la Verrière on the eve of Saint-Martin, 1982, with a kitchen of four cooks and a card of nine dishes — among them the perdreau aux coings, the tarte fine au verjus, and the blanc-manger aux amandes we serve still tonight.

The cookery is drawn, line by line, from three documents: the kitchen rolls of the archbishop of Reims for the years 1389 to 1421, the Viandier of Guillaume Tirel, called Taillevent, and the kitchen accounts of the Cistercian house at Morimond in the Vosges, which carried sweetmeat into the year 1500.

Nothing arrives on the plate that could not, in principle, have been served at a Lenten chapter in the year 1410 — saving only the New World tomato, which the Maison declines.

THE DINING ROOM

One enters from the street through a low door of oak, set with iron straps repaired from the gates of the church of Saint-Julien-le-Pauvre. Within, the room is long, narrow, and tall — proportions inherited from the monastic use — and its walls are washed with a dry cobalt distemper made for the house by the trade at Sèvres in 2009.

The cellar, an oak refectory boards from the dissolved priory of Saint-Médard near Soissons, sits in number, each seating seven. They were acquired at the dispersal of 1968 and bear the priest's notch made on the underside.

"On a winter evening, with the eighty-four candles lit and the kitchen door swinging, one might be turning supper in the year of the Black Prince."
— LE FIGARO LITTÉRAIRE, 1996

No district lamp burns in the dining room. Light comes from eighty-four candles, set in iron sconces on the walls and in oak-cask stands on the boards. The candles are renewed every third night.

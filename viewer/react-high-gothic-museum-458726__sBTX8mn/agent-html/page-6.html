ABOUT THE MUSEUM &
HISTORY OF THE
CLOISTER

A century and a quarter of stewardship over the sacred arts of the cathedral building age — glass, vellum, and stone, gathered in the room of the Priory of Saint-Othonis-des-Aydes.

[motif: cross-flory]

Three Foundations

1837
THE ANTIQUARIAN'S VOW

Édouard de Saint-Pierre, antiquarian of Reims and student of Viollet-le-Duc's later disciples, vows after the destruction of the Saint-Pierre Priory at Reims to shelter the displaced fragments wheresoever he can. His purpose, attended to the death of foundation in 1859, was the preservation of an ensemble already disregarded.

1853
THE GLASS BEQUEST

Upon the patriarch's death, his heirs entrusted his collection to a board of trustees. The endowment was insufficient to its programme; restoration was suspended through the 1870s, until the modern parts of the cathedral itself, were reattached to the cloister.

1903 – 1962
THE REBUILDING

After hostilities through the war years, the cloister walk and the broad east hall were rebuilt with the second of the Cloister's campaigns, by the donation of stones from the original Auxerre transepts. Among the most original donations was three years of stonework expended over fortnight-and-a-half intervals to consult fortune from those collected lessons.

[motif: quatrefoil]

An Account of the Cloister

The Priory of Saint-Othonis-des-Aydes, the modest church which gives the Cloister Museum its name, lay first within the diocese of Reims, then after 1789 within the diocese of Paris and again, after the establishment of the Constituency of Saint-Bernard. Its records survive in fragments only — a chartulary of small import, two surviving column-bases, and a complete set of foundation deeds.

Following the Bequest of 1853 the trust acquired contiguous parcels in the Marais and was, beginning the present at its premises in Saint-Pierre. The new wing comprised three lower halls, a great loggia, and the dressed-stone library above. The transept floor — laid by the masters of the De Brou-school in the high years before 1890 — was uncovered during sondage in 1928.

Original woodwork from the cloister of Sainte-Périne du Vergne was redeployed in the entrance court following the Verot bequest of 1934. A separate building campaign provoked by water damage during 1962 saw the entire south range rebuilt to a higher specification using fragments of the original cloister itself, donated from the Boussou estate at Beaune.

The cloister is described elsewhere through a curiously oblique angle in three diaries kept by the architect Adrien Verot during the years 1884 to 1903. Verot's particular conviction — that the medieval cloister was, at heart, a pedagogy and not a programme — finds its full expression in the Mason\'s Yard, the museum\'s seventh and youngest gallery, opened in 2003.

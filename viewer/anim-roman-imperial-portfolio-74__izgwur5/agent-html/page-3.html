PARS  III ·  OPVS · TESSELLATVM

OPUS
TESSELLATUM

A method for re-laying threshold mosaics so the foot finds them again. Two thousand years of broken tesserae, and a small workshop willing to go slowly.

THE SOURCING OF TESSERAE

BANDS OF LIBYAN

Marble offcuts from the Aurelian Way, recut by hand into 9-mm cubes for warm field grounds.

RUBIVM · AFRICANUM

Rosso Antico recovered from Pompeian wall fragments — fired smalti binder, 1.4 cm.

VITREVM · CYPRIVM

Hand-poured glass smalti from Murano, dyed in the workshop with copper carbonate for Aegean fields.

CONSERVATION METHOD

VII

Each fragment is photogrammetrically mapped, lifted on linen scrim, and re-bedded on a lime-pozzolana mortar that mirrors the Roman recipe described by Vitruvius in book seven. We do not introduce new tesserae unless absolutely necessary; instead, we reset the original cubes in their first geometry, accepting the gaps as part of the work’s history. The patron is told the truth about what is original and what is replacement, in writing, before any stone is touched.

THREE CASE STUDIES

SOL INVICTUS

VILLA QUINTILIA
ROMA · LATIVM
MMXXII — MMXXIII

A threshold from the western corridor, found in eight pieces under a later Carolingian floor. The radial geometry has been re-aligned to the original cardinal axis; the central porphyry boss is the only entirely new stone — set deliberately a half-tone redder than the antique, so the eye is never deceived.

OPUS LIGATUM

DOMVS LVNELLI
GENOA · LIGVRIA
MMXXIV

An entirely new floor of woven bands in three colours — Aegean blue, ochre and Pompeian red — laid in a 4-cm grid drawn from the Vesuvian pattern-books. The diagonal weave intentionally drops a tessera at every fourth crossing, a Greek decorative habit that prevents the eye from settling.

OCULUS NOCTIS

PRIVATE CHAPEL
VENEZIA · VENETIA
MMXXIII

A small dome floor for a private chapel. Concentric rings of dark amethyst smalti spiral inward to a single gold-leaf tessera at the centre, set face-down so the gold catches lamp-light only when the visitor stands directly above it. The geometry was drawn from a 3rd-century mosaic at Spina.

Indigo at Aizome-an

The fifth-
generation
dyer who refused
synthetic blue

MARCH 2024 · KYOTO
Reported by
Hana Ueda
Photographs by
Kentaro Aizawa

Naoko Furusawa works barefoot beside a 200-year-old earthenware vat sunk into the floor of a Nishijin machiya. She is the fifth Furusawa to do so. The vat, she explains, has not been emptied since 1834.

I
Plate One Plate I · The Vat at Morning

That Naoko works as you must walk and breathe is partly the point. Her family has been running the workshop continuously since the late Tenpō era, and listens for the sound of dawn, falling lightly against wood. There is no sign, but you can ask. She is the fifth Furusawa to do so.

This is not a metaphor I am supplying. The longevity is the point. The vats themselves, the flagstones, the wells, the bamboo are all instruments and the technique is laid down quietly when the technique is laid down at all.

A Conversation, Transcribed

I asked first whether she had ever been tempted by chemical blue.

«Three times,» she said. «1968, 1983, 2007. The first when synthetic indigo was 30% the cost of natural; the second when our buyer in Osaka asked. The third when our youngest left for Tokyo.»

I had come to write about a vat. I left writing about a person.

«My job is not to dye cloth,» she said. «My job is to keep something alive long enough to hand it to whoever comes next.»

The conversation was held in the workshop, between two stoppered vats and a row of cotton swatches drying in the cold. She showed me a swatch from 2003, indistinguishable from one finished last week.

"The bath has been fermenting since 1834. My job is not to dye cloth. My job is to keep something alive long enough to hand it to whoever comes next."— NAOKO FURUSAWA · MARCH 2024

Nakasendō, Nagiso

Tsumago-
juku, in the quiet
of January.

A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the snow.

妻 籠 宿 · 中 山 道
Dispatched by
Ryōsuke Mori
From
Nagiso, Kiso Valley
14–17 January 2024

The bus from Nagiso station lets you off at the lower of Tsumago's two stone-paved main streets, after the village ordinance of 1968 prevents power lines, the geography of the town clings to its foot. By that hour the town was already lacquered against the cold, the signage faintly black-and-gilt under the eaves, the snow on the slate roofs heavy enough that I heard it slide twice before I reached the inn.

I had come to write about the seventy-second-step sign — a wooden post planted by the National Trust as an exhibit; we are at the foot of post-station Tsumago. By the morning I had walked four kilometres through the village, the geographic sign was no more than a footprint in the snow.

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became Tsumago has been told often enough that the village reprises it as a souvenir pamphlet, but it bears reading in the original: the late 1960s, the highway was new, the airport was 90 minutes away, and the village was twenty-one houses with the bath ringing the houses, sheets of cold rain slapping at the slate.

The proprietors had hung a numbered list of the entryway of the Tsumago-juku Preservation Society. Across the proprietor's desk was a ginkgo leaf in the spine of a guestbook and a teapot waiting for me. The Twenty-three points are a careful list of don'ts you should remember, what to undo, what not to add. The places have become — as places will — themselves and other things at once.

Gohei-mochi, miso, walnut

I stayed at the Matsushiro Inn, four rooms above a restaurant that has served travellers, by the proprietress's reckoning, since 1834. Dinner on the second night was gohei-mochi — pounded rice toasted with a balanced miso-and-walnut paste — followed by simmered river-salmon and grilled mountain mushrooms. The glaze of Matsushiro over local Shinshu miso and a walnut from the orchards above Mariko, the proprietress, Mrs. Kobayashi, refused to discuss the proportions on her batch.

東海道五十三次

The Fifty-Three Stations,
Two Hundred Years On

By Mariko Sato· Walked May 3 · 24, 2024· Tokyo → Kyoto· 32 min read

At 5:42 on the morning of the third of May, with the bronze plaque of Nihonbashi still cold under our palms, I shouldered a thirteen-kilo pack and set out for Kyoto on foot — a journey Hiroshige rendered in fifty-five woodblock prints in 1833, and which the post-towns of the Tokugawa shogunate were expected to finish in three days. I gave myself twenty-three.

The Tōkaidō, the modern eyes may insist, was always more idea than infrastructure. By the time Utagawa Hiroshige published his Gojūsan-tsugi series with the Hōeidō publishing house, the route had been formally surveyed for two centuries: fifty-three official post-towns flanked the imperial capital, each with its hourly relay for daimyo retinues, its rice merchants, its bathhouses, its quarrels. What Hiroshige did was less to document than to translate — the road as weather, as encounter, as the slight hesitation of a porter at a river crossing.

Hiroshige's prints were to show not the road but the weather of being on it — the moment a porter shifts his load, the second a traveller realises the rain will not stop before nightfall.

第十四 · 原宿STATION 14 · HARA

The Lake, the Pass, the Print

The Hakone print is the one everybody knows: the procession threading a path between fanged blue-and-orange mountains, Lake Ashi a flat grey wedge below. Hiroshige's mountains are exaggerated — the geological Tanaka Kōji, in a 1999 paper for the Kanagawa Museum, compared the print's gradients with the actual contours and found the artist had multiplied the steepest faces by roughly 1.4.¹ I climbed them on the eighth day, in fog so dense I could not see the lake at all, and concluded that 1.4 was if anything a conservative figure.

The old checkpoint, the Hakone-juku sekisho, was reconstructed in 2007 from the original Tokugawa-era specifications. The reconstruction is honest about being a reconstruction: laminated panels, a gift shop, a polite young woman in period costume who told me she was studying logistics at Tōkai University and worked weekends. She also told me — and I have not been able to verify this — that the cedar beams were sourced from a forest that had supplied the original 1619 structure.

第二十一 · 鞠子宿STATION 21 · MARIKO

Where the Inn Still Stands

Of the fifty-three stations, only a handful retain a structure that a Tenpō-era traveller would recognise. Goyu is one of them. The Ōtoiya inn, an inn that has operated continuously since 1648, sits on a curve of the old road five hundred meters from a soulless prefectural bypass. The current proprietor, Ōtaiki Sanae, is the seventeenth of her line. She charged me ¥9,500 for a room with a view of a bamboo grove and a breakfast of grilled mackerel, miso, three pickles, and rice cooked in an iron pot.

«We had Hiroshige's grandson here in 1888,» she said, pouring tea. «He stayed two nights. The register survives.» She would not show it to me — it is on loan to the Tokyo National Museum until October — but she had a photocopy taped to the wall behind the reception desk, and the brushwork is unmistakable.

I had walked four hundred and twelve kilometres to sleep in a room where, in some thinned and unverifiable sense, the artist's family had also slept. I did not sleep especially well.— From the introduction, Smith & Foster, 1968
第五十三 · 三条大橋STATION 53 · SANJŌ ŌHASHI

Arrival, Without Ceremony

I crossed the Sanjō Bridge on the afternoon of the twenty-fifth, twenty-three days and four hours after leaving Nihonbashi, in a light drizzle that suited the occasion. Hiroshige's final print shows the bridge crowded with travellers and a horse mid-step. The contemporary bridge has a pedestrian crossing, two lanes of traffic, and a small bronze plaque commemorating the Tōkaidō's terminus, which I had to be shown by a passerby because it is set into the pavement and I had walked past it twice.

What had survived was the road that Hiroshige saw — as a documentarian. He was, as Henry Smith argued, a translator of distances into mood.² The fifty-three prints are not fifty-three places, they are fifty-three weathers. Two hundred years on, the weathers are intact. The places have become — as places will — themselves and other things at once.

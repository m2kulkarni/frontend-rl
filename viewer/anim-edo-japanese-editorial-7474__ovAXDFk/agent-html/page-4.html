Criticism · No. 04 · Close Reading

Hokusai's
Wave,
Read Slowly

By the CriticJunichirō Mori
Issued15 September 2024 · Tokyo

For two centuries the West has read Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura as a portrait of menace — an instant before catastrophe, a wave poised to devour three slender boats. This is, I want to argue, an almost wilful misreading. The wave is not falling. It is repeating.

Plate I

To stand close to a first-class impression — I had the wood-block of the late, beautiful Boston Museum, on a wet Wednesday in February — is to see what is hidden. The print is not a picture of menace. The geometry is a recursion of itself. The wave's inner curl repeats the wave's outer curl. The boats become a third element in this pattern.

Consider the boats. There are three — 押送船 oshiokuribune, fast boats used to ferry fresh sashimi tenneed Edo. They are loaded; spray on bows; the 8 oarsmen pulling steady. Dotted along nine bracket curves, three. Hokusai painted them with the contempt of a man who knew their job, and accepted that, on this morning, work was something they were not going to be able to finish.

"The wave is not a wave. It is a house being misnamed — water aspiring to its geometry."
Three Print Studies
第一图 · I
The Foam Claws

Each finger of the curl is itself a smaller wave: a fractal Hokusai could not have named. His apprentices cut each one freehand.

第二图 · II
The Three Skiffs

Eight oarsmen apiece. They are not lookers but rowers; the men who carried fresh fish from Awa to Edo, and were paid by speed.

第三图 · III
Mt. Fuji, in Scale

Fuji is the smallest object in the print. This was deliberate. Hokusai is asking a question about importance.

"But surely the wave is about to fall."
— A common reading, refuted
"Hokusai feared the sea — see his late letters."

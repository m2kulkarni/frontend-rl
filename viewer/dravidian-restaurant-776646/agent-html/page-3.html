Our Story

A small dining room on 6th Block runs on the patience of a brass pot, the memory of a grandmother's pantry, and the stubborn idea that Chettinad is not haute — it is a structure.

Meera Alagappan was thirty-one when she resigned from a senior product role at a software firm on Old Airport Road. It was March 2016. Her notice period ran six weeks; she spent three of them on a sleeper train to Karaikudi and the remaining three searching her grandmother's kitchen in the village of Kanadukathan for whatever remained — boxes of asal masala, a brass tindora, an iron tava, and the one ladle her grandmother said was for kuzhambu and nothing else.

She came back to Bangalore with a steel trunk: forty-two pieces of cookware, eleven hand-written recipe ledgers, a brass spool, and the conviction that the food she had eaten every summer of her childhood — the dry mutton chukka, the shallot-thick vengaya kuzhambu, the ceremonial dignity of a properly-made karu kuzhambu — had no honest representation in the city she now called home.

Kalasha opened on the first floor of a converted bungalow on 60 Feet Road, Koramangala, in November 2017, with thirty-six covers, two cooks, and a single dosa pan. The dining room held eleven tables. It still does.

"My aunt told me the kitchen has only two tools that matter — your grandmother's vessels, and the time you are willing to give them. Everything else is decoration." — MEERA ALAGAPPAN, CHEF/OWNER

▼ SECTION I · ORIGINS

Karaikudi to Koramangala

The Chettiars were a merchant community whose trading routes once stretched from the Coromandel coast to Burma, Ceylon, and Penang. Their kitchens absorbed everything that travelled with them — star anise from the spice routes of the Malayan coast, the cassia bark fagged for the moaning pot — and rendered it back as a cuisine of disciplined austerity.

Meera's grandmother kept a Karaikudi menu of seventy-eight items. Sat went, the inventoried twice a month: forty-eight named spices, eleven varieties of dried lentils, a kay pot of milagu kuzhambu that was replenished but never emptied. When the house was sold in 2019, three of the inventory ledgers came to Bangalore with us.

Kalasha is, in some essential way, the act of bringing those ledgers into daily use. The morning prep list — chalked on slate at 7.15 a.m., six days a week — is a direct descendant of the Karaikudi household sheet. The shopping route is shorter, but the questions are the same.

▼ SECTION II · THE ROOM

The Athangudi Floor

The dining room floor is laid in Athangudi tile, sourced directly from a fourth-generation workshop in Athangudi village, twelve kilometres from Karaikudi. The tiles are made on a glass plate, one at a time, by hand, with cement, oxide pigment, and river sand — the same technique used in the great Chettinad mansions of the late nineteenth century.

We chose four patterns: a peacock-blue compass rose, a vermilion lotus medallion, a gold-leaf rotated square, and a plain ivory ground. Eleven hundred and eighty-four tiles arrived in four wooden crates in February 2020. They were laid by two craftsmen from the village over nineteen days.

The floor is cool underfoot in summer. It is also slightly uneven — each tile carries the print of the hand that pressed it. We have never refinished it.

▼ SECTION III · THE VESSEL

The Brass Pot

An anna-chombu is a pot turned from kalai — a tin-alloy native to the Tamil south. It looks unremarkable: squat, lipped, the colour of weather. It is not unremarkable. The metal is soft enough that the pot must be filled with both hands and washed only with tamarind. It cannot be set on a high flame. It cannot be left empty on heat.

What it does, in exchange for this fussing, is hold sambar at a low murmur for six hours without breaking the dal, without souring the tamarind, without dulling the curry leaf. The lentils stay distinct. The aroma stays sweet. There is no substitute. Stainless steel makes a faster sambar; an anna-chombu makes a true one.

Ours was inherited. It has been on the Kalasha pass since March 2023; after eighteen months of seasoning. It produces forty-four litres of sambar a day; it is washed at 11.30 p.m., rated overnight on a banana leaf, and set on the stove again at 6.00 a.m.

The Press & The Pantry

A small selection of writing about Kalasha — what critics, columnists, and a few generous readers have said about the kitchen since we opened our doors on Lavelle Road in September 2019.

●●● ●●●

★ The Hindu · Long Read

"The Most Quietly Ambitious Tamil Kitchen in the South"

Coomi Kapoor Travelar India · March 2022 · By Kavin Gupta

The most quietly ambitious Tamil kitchen in the south — a room that does not announce itself, but rewards the patient diner with sambar that tastes like a recovered memory.

Kalasha was profiled in CNT India's annual fold-out travel insert as one of eleven restaurants worth changing routes for. Coomi Kapoor traced the menu's evening-out of grandmothers' kitchens and the unhurried stove that has come to define the place. Praise focused on the eeya chombu sambar, the sliced ennai kathirikai, and the standout kara kuzhambu. The piece appears in CNT's October print run — the kitchen has framed the entire fold-out and hung it next to the pass.

★ Critical Voicewise

"Alagappan's Sambar is an Argument"

The Hindu Magazine · 4 May 2022 · By Vasundhara Vinod

Alagappan's sambar is an argument — a closely-reasoned defence of the small-batch toor dal, made daily, never reheated past one service.

A four-page magazine review from Vasundhara Vinod, the food columnist who is sceptical about restaurant Tamil. She concedes Kalasha's argument, with reservations: the paal payasam reduction at exactly two and a half hours; the kara kuzhambu unwilling to accept anything less than a single grower in Karaikudi.

"A larger pot," he writes, "begins to argue with itself." It is rare for a critic to disclose a kitchen's interior workings, and rarer for the kitchen to print the column on its menu's back jacket. Both happened.

★ Short · Issue 14

An Inventory of the Chettinad Pantry

Goya Journal · Issue 14 · "The Pantry" 2023

To open Kalasha's spice store is to read a small biography of the Chettiar trade routes — Burmese star anise beside Ceylon cinnamon beside marathi mokku from a single grower in Karaikudi.

The journal's fourteenth issue, dedicated entirely to the working pantry, opened with a 4,200-word essay by writer Aysha Tanya on Kalasha's spice room. The essay catalogued forty-seven distinct ingredients, traced the supply chain of the kitchen's kalpasi (stone-flower) back to a co-operative in Sivagangai district, and reproduced — with permission — the handwritten notebook page on which Alagappan's mother had recorded the family kuzhambu ratios in 1974.

The full essay, "What the Pantry Remembers", runs across pages 22–41 of the print issue and is available to gazes in Issue 14.

Letters From the Table

I came to Bangalore for a wedding in November and was taken to Kalasha by my cousin who runs frankly. I don't always trust on these matters. The avadhya kuzhambu at Sunday lunch tasted exactly like my aunt's; only she has been gone fourteen years and I had begun to believe I was misremembering. I am writing to say I was not misremembering. Thank you.

— Lakshmi K., Vadapalani · Letter dated 12.05.2024

My husband and I are both in our late seventies and we do not eat out often. We came on a Tuesday at half past noon and were a great in the courtyard window. Your young man — I think his name was Karthik — explained the food, the menu, and the day. Then we lingered for two and a half hours. The starlings warmed my heart.

— Mrs. & Mrs. Subramanian · Letter dated 21.04.2024

I was a chef in Copenhagen and I read about you in Wherefore. I flew through Bangalore on my way to Colombo and changed my flight to spend a night. The kuzhambu was the best single mouthful of food I had in 2023. I am not exaggerating to be polite — I keep notebooks. You are on page 3 of the green.

— Lars af Karstinghuyse · Letter dated 03.07.2024

Indigo at Aizome-an

The fifth-
generation
dyer who refused
synthetic blue

Reiko Furuyama works barefoot beside a 200-year-old earthenware vat sunk into the floor of a Nishijin machiya. She is the fifth Furuyama to do so. The vat, she explains, has not been emptied since 1834.
AIZOME-AN · 藍染庵

PLATE I · KAME-VAT, MORNING
PLATE I · 藍 甕

The 200-year-old earthenware vat, buried to its lip in the workshop's beaten-earth floor. Fed daily with sukumo, lye, wheat-bran and sake, the bath has not been allowed to die since the year of the Tenpō reforms. A copper lid keeps the surface from oxidising; an iron rod is used to stir the slurry until the indigo-bearing flower (an indicator of the bath's chemical balance) reappears at first light.

In a Buddhist Kyoto one can travel ten and find no truth as deeply as in Reiko Furuyama's bath. She is the rarest of provenances: a fifth generation, in continuous practice since 1834. Her grandfather Naoaki founded the shop on his return from a fabric mission in Tokushima in 1789; her grandfather Tadayoshi rebuilt it after the 1868 fire; her mother Aiko refused, in 1962, to switch from sukumo to synthetic indigo. "It was offered," Reiko says, "and there were three months that year I could not pay the rent. But the bath would have died." We sat for two hours before she would speak again.

The bath, in 1834 as in 2024, is a 270-litre earthenware vessel of Bizen ware called a kame, set chest-deep into the workshop floor and stoppered with a flat copper lid. It is fed weekly with sukumo, the fermented Tokushima leaf, plus wood-ash lye, wheat bran, sake, and a length of cleaned chestnut twig. It eats. It dies if not fed. It is alive in a way that is awkward to describe in business terms but unmistakable on first sight.

The fifth Furuyama daughter received the bath at thirty-one, in 2009, on her grandmother's death. There is no formal handover — Aiko had been ill for a year and the bath had been Reiko's responsibility for most of that. The morning of the ceremony she descended the steps to the workshop alone, removed the copper lid, and submerged her arms to the elbow in the indigo. She brought them up. They were clean — the dye had not yet taken — but, she says, the bath had recognised her, and she had recognised it.

What Reiko keeps is not, in any direct way, an art. It is a chemistry, a microbe culture, a particular relationship between a woman and a body of liquid that has been kept alive across nearly two centuries. The thread is unbroken. We watched her dye a length of asagi cotton; she lifted it from the bath the colour of a winter sky at dusk. By the third immersion it had reached the indigo of a clean ocean before sunrise.

A Conversation, Transcribed

You inherited this bath in 2009.
Yes, in March. My grandmother had been ill for a year, but neither of us had spoken about her dying. The handover was not a ceremony. I went down to the workshop and lifted the lid.

Was it healthy?
No. It had a cold, a chill — the smell was off. The bath has moods, and at that moment its mood was sour. I fed it, and waited, and it came back over six days.

You still feed it daily?
Twice. In the morning at six, and in the evening at eight. There is no day off. The microbes do not take days off.

What did you not understand at first?
That the bath is the master and I am the apprentice. The first year I tried to make it produce a particular blue, and it refused, and I worked for three months on a thread that came out grey. The bath was telling me to slow down.

Have you considered training a successor?
I have a daughter, who is now eight. She comes to the workshop in the afternoons after school. I do not press her, but she likes to put her hand into the bath and feel the temperature — that is enough for now. I tell her: the bath is older than your great-grandmother. Be polite to it.

Is there a phrase you use to teach her?
Yes — listen to the bath. Even when she is across the room, she will know if it is happy. The bath has been here for one hundred and ninety years. My job is to keep it alive long enough to hand it to whoever comes next.

PLATE II · 浅 葱
Asagi · Pale Onion-Blue

The lightest of immersions: a single dip, no more than thirty seconds, hung up to oxidise on a bamboo pole over the inner courtyard.

PLATE III · 縹
Hanada · Deep night-sky blue

Three immersions, with thorough oxidation between each. The traditional cloth of court attendants and a personal favourite of the dyer.

PLATE IV · 留 紺
Tomekon · Warrior's Blue

Six or more immersions over four days. Dyed indigo this dark was carried into battle by samurai for the antiseptic property of fermented woad.

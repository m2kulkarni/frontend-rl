The Fifty-Three
Stations,
Two Hundred
Years On

By Mariko Sato·WALKED MAY 2-23·13,300 STEPS/DAY·492KM
DEPARTURE
Nihonbashi
東京都中央区
Date
2 May 2024
Arrival
Sanjō Ōhashi, Kyoto
Date
23 May 2024

At 5:42 on the morning of the third of May, with the bronze plaque of Nihonbashi still cold under my palm, I shouldered a thirteen-kilo pack and set out for Kyōto on foot — a journey Hiroshige rendered in fifty-five woodblock prints in 1833. I walked the post-towns of the Tokugawa shogunate were expected to finish in three days. I gave myself twenty-three.

The Tōkaidō, the eastern sea road, was always more idea than infrastructure. By the time Utagawa Hiroshige published his Gojusan-tsugi series with the Hōeidō publishing house, the route had been formally surveyed for two centuries; fifty-three official post-towns lined the imperial capital, each with its honjin for daimyo retinues, its rice merchants, its butterflies, and its hatamoto. Hiroshige did not see his document have to translate — the road as weather, as encounter, as the slight hesitation of a porter at a river crossing.

Hiroshige's prints were to show not the road but the breathing of being on it — the moment a porter shifts his load, when a teahouse opens onto a frame of pines, when one step before a paper umbrella takes the rain.

I carried two books, Smith and Foster's 1986 translation of the series, with its lavish foldouts of the Hōeidō edition, and a 1974 walking guide by the Shizuoka Prefectural Tourist Bureau, which I had bought for ¥600 at a Jimbōchō second-hand stall the week before leaving. The guide was thirty years older than I am. It assumed I would still find ryokan in Yui.

The Lake, the Pass, the Print

第七駅HAKONE

The fifteenth of the fifty-three stations, Hakone is the final pass before the descent into Suruga Bay. Hiroshige painted it in heavy mineral pigment, the lake a slab of malachite, the porters and white-banded chigaeshi staff conspicuously rendered. I climbed it in fog and got the print in reverse for ninety minutes. The fog cleared enough to show that the road still climbed by switchback, only the bones — bridge, koi-flag, lacquered roof — had migrated quietly into private ownership.

Where the Inns Still Stands

第二十二駅OKABE

At the fifty-third station, the Tōkaidō reaches a final wall of cedar — the bell of Sanjō Ōhashi can be heard, faintly, on a still afternoon. Three of the original honjin survive, two as folklore museums and one with its tatami still rolled out for guests. The owner showed me the visitor book: a continuous register of pilgrims back to 1840.

I had walked four hundred and twelve kilometers to arrive at a Lawson; it did not strap especially well.

Arrival, Without Ceremony

京都到着SANJŌ ŌHASHI

What the recreation walked once, of Hiroshige are not the calendars he was, as does most of Japan, on Western time. The bridge does not, as does most of the Tōkaidō, sequentially mark the road. It is a footbridge in working condition, but visitors halfway across, did not announce themselves to my arrival as I had supposed they would. The bus crossed Kawaramachi Sanjō and the print I'd remembered from the Hōeidō edition reappeared at midnight: the same crooked fence, the same plane of lacquered ink. I stood for a minute, then sat on a step, then kept walking.

Five Stations Where the
Honjin Still Stands

Of the original fifty-three honjin — the inns reserved for daimyō and imperial messengers — five remain in a continuing state of operation as guesthouses. Their names below, with the year of their founding.

  1. Ōiso— Kashibaya1671
  2. Yui— Mochizuki1709
  3. Okazaki— Honma1768
  4. Tsuchiyama— Suzuka1811
  5. Tochinoki— Honjin Sano1832

1. Tōkaidō Gojusan-tsugi, the Hōeidō edition published 1833–1834, is held in the National Diet Library, Asia Society, and the Brooklyn Museum. The eight surviving keyblocks are kept under climate control by Adachi Hanga in Tōkyō.

2. Distances cited follow the 1843 Tokugawa-era survey, in which one ri ≈ 3.927 km. Modern paving has straightened or rerouted approximately 28% of the original road.

3. The author is grateful to the Adachi Foundation for the loan of working blocks for reference; to the Hakone-juku Honjin Museum for an out-of-hours visit; and to Yui Mochizuki for the futon, the tea, the egg, and the willing patience with the foreigner reading old maps at her kitchen table.

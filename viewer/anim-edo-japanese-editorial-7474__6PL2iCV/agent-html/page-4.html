PRINT ANALYSIS·NO. 03·浮 世 絵 解 説·OCTOBER 2024·EDO

Hokusai's
Wave,
Read Slowly

By Kenji Akiyama
Contributing Editor. Edition prints courtesy of the Adachi Foundation, Tokyo.
F
FOLIO ONE

For two centuries the West has read Kanagawa-oki Nami-ura as a portrait of menace — an instant before catastrophe, a wave poised to devour three slender boats. This is, I want to argue, an almost wilful misreading. The wave is not falling. It is repeating.

PLATE I · 浮 世 絵
FROM

Katsushika Hokusai
Fugaku Sanjūrokkei
富嶽三十六景 (1831)

PIGMENTS

Berlin Blue (Prussian),
sumi, beni, lead white

EDITION

Approx. 8,000 prints
between 1831 & 1834

To read this as a slow-motion catastrophe — I had felt it in the eight tones of the Hōeidō Museum, in person, and miss it — fifty-five panel facing the keyblock seems to confirm. The reading I would propose, looking at the fifty rows of foam claws and the curl of foam-tip and the inverse-bell sequence cropped along its spread, is being held in suspension. Hokusai, by his account, was eighty-eight when he drew it; he was seventy-two when this print appeared.

This is not a strangely's not happening. The negative — the fact that the print contains both the wave and the not-wave, both motion and stasis at the same time — is what gives it its peculiar strength. Hokusai composed the foam claws to make a frame: they are not falling on the boats, they are bracketing them.

The wave is not a wave. It is a brain being held — water performing its geometry.

Read this way, the print is bracketed by the wedge of Mt Fuji at its centre — small, snow-capped, mathematically equivalent to the spent claws of the wave. The mountain holds. The print also acknowledges its own composition with a pulse of pressure: the foam-claw is the same shape as the curve of Fuji, and the curve of Fuji is the same shape as the wave.

Consider the boats. There are three — お卒、押送船 — small, low-prowed barges of a kind used for ferrying live fish and dried tuna into Edo from the Bōsō peninsula. They are oiled wood, eight men to each. Their crews, in this print, are not panicking. They are bowing. The eight in the foreground boat are stacked head-to-tail in a single arc, knees forward, oar shafts shipped, each one's nose nearly touching the back of the man before him. Eight bowing men make a row of eight petalled forms; the wave-claw makes another. They rhyme.

The Berlin trading of bero-ai, a Prussian blue first imported through Nagasaki in the late 1820s, gave Hokusai a pigment that did not fade and that printed at very fine resolution. Two prints below illustrate this: the original 1831 keyblock pigment is still legible; the recent reissue (Adachi, 2018) shows what was carved into wood remains the same and what was lifted from the keyblock has begun to drift.

What I want to propose, then — and that the foam-claws do not merely curve but contain — is that the wave is not falling. Each foam-claw of pressure exerts identical force on each surface of itself. They are a frame; the print is an event-frame; and what is held in it is not catastrophe at all but the geometry that makes catastrophe legible to begin with.

THREE-VIEWS · 三景
FROM THE SERIES · 富嶽
The Four Cuts

Mt Fuji as seen from the bay of Edo, from the path, the rice fields. Each is the same mountain, struck at the same hour, by the same eye, by the same blue.

PLATE 41 · 神奈川
The Three Skiffs

Eight men in each of three boats, each set in a balanced crouch. The eye reads the rocking line where the prow meets the wave-trough. They are not falling.

DETAIL · 富 嶽
Mt. Fuji, in Scale

The mountain at the centre of the print is, by measurement, three times smaller than the wave. By proportion of the eye, however, the mountain holds the centre weight and the wave merely circumscribes it.

FROM A LECTURE DELIVERED AT KEIO

"But surely the wave is about to fall."

It is not. The wave's geometry — bracketed pressure on identical surfaces, the bowing of the eight men, the rhyme of foam-claw and Fuji — describes a wave in suspended equilibrium. Two centuries of Western re-reading wanted disaster. Hokusai gave us pressure.

"Hokusai feared the sea — see his late letters."

He did, certainly, write a letter to his publisher in 1834 referencing a typhoon that had taken his daughter's house. The fear is real and the print precedes it by three years. The print is not biography. It is, as he himself called it, "the wave at its right size."

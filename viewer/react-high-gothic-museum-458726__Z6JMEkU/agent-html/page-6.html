About the Museum &
History of the
Cloister

A century and a quarter of stewardship over the sacred arts of the cathedral building age — glass, vellum, and stone gathered to the ruins of the Priory of Saint-Hilaire-des-Aulnes.

[motif: quatrefoil]

Three Foundations

1897
The Antiquarian's Vow

Édouard de Saint-Pierre, antiquarian of Reims and resident of Vallée du Cloître, purchased the dissolved grounds of the Carmelite Priory of Saint-Hilaire-des-Aulnes for €2,000 francs. His purpose, recorded in the deed of foundation of 14 October 1897, was the perpetual care of "fragments rescued from the cathedral fires of the Revolution and after."

1923
The Glass Bequest

Upon the antiquarian's death, his heirs entrusted the collection to a board of trustees. The museum was reorganised around the de Saint-Pierre stained-glass holdings — eighty-one panels of 12th- and 13th-century French work.

1956–1962
The Rebuilding

After incendiary damage in the war years, the cloister walk and the lancet hall were rebuilt to designs prepared by the atelier of Eugène Hortet in the years 1956 through 1962, retaining the original Cistercian foundation lines in poured concrete faced with Caen limestone.

An Account of the Cloister

The Priory of Saint-Hilaire-des-Aulnes-Aulnes stood empty from the suppression of 1791 until Édouard de Saint-Pierre, then fifty-four years of age and lately retired from his commerce in ecclesiastical antiquities of Reims, took possession of its ruined chapter-house and west cloister in the autumn of 1897. He installed his private collection — manuscript leaves, capital fragments, and a set of three rose-window medallions attributed to the workshop of Saint-Denis — in the surviving south range, opening one room to scholars by appointment.

Following the bequest of 1923 the trust acquired contiguous parcels to the north and east, regularising the precinct to its present 4.6 hectares. The new lancet hall, completed under Eric Pierre's atelier in 1962, runs ninety-three metres along the former cuneronial nave; its twenty-two paired lancets bring the rose of the Saint-Pierre glass to the original Cistercian foundation lines.

The first public galleries opened on the feast of Saint-Denis, 9 October MCMLI, with admission set at one franc fifty.

Most of the collection's holdings, then and now, are spoils of the Revolutionary suppression of monasteries — drawn from the families of the dispersed religious to whom the duty of care had passed. The museum holds these in trust, and considers itself a chapel of return more than a vault.

— from the Charter of MMXII, art. III

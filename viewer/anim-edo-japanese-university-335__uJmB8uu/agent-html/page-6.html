Seventy-three
years of quiet
practice.

Hokurin Daigaku has, since its chartering, attempted to be the small, slow university that the prefecture of Kyoto did not have. Three buildings, three faculties, sixteen-hundred undergraduates — and more reading rooms than lecture halls.

沿革 / HISTORY

From a Higashiyama juku to a chartered university.

小さく、遅く、静かに。

Hokurin Daigaku began, before its name, as a private juku gathered around the literary scholar Mori Hisamasa in the back rooms of a Higashiyama machiya in the autumn of 1948. Twelve students read kanbun and Edo poetry around a single low desk; in winter Mori split his own firewood for the kotatsu. The juku met for four years before MEXT's chartering reforms made it possible to incorporate, in 1952, as a small private university for the humanities.

The first buildings were the silk-merchant compound of the Tōrin family, north of the Imperial Palace, leased on a forty-year lease in 1952. By the early 1980s the buildings had grown unfit, and the university faced a choice: rebuild on its existing footprint, or move. In 1985 the trustees took the slower path. They purchased the present 1782 machiya in Higashiyama and spent four years restoring it under the master carpenter Inoue Tetsurō. Hokurin opened on its present site in April 1989.

Three subsequent additions — the 1994 Bunkō hall, the 2008 library annex, and the 2018 craft workshop — were each designed to step back from the machiya street face by at least four metres. The university has, in seventy-three years, never built taller than three storeys.

人々 / PEOPLE

Three values, held in working trust.

The university adopted these three commitments at its 1989 refounding; they are reviewed by the trustees every ten years and have not been amended.

第一 · ONE

Slow Reading

That texts deserve unhurried attention. We teach reading as a craft and refuse to confuse coverage with understanding.

第二 · TWO

Conserved Things

That objects — printed sheets, lacquered bowls, woven cloths — must be kept able to outlast their generation.

第三 · THREE

Local Watershed

That the Kamo and Yodo rivers are not a backdrop. They have a name, a history, and a daily condition we are obliged to know.

"Teach at a human pace, in a room that smells of cedar. The rest will follow."
MORI HISAMASA, 1948
初代 / FIRST RECTOR

Senjin 先人 · Mori Hisamasa

Mori Hisamasa (1903–1981) was a literary scholar of the late-Tokugawa essay tradition, with secondary training in shodō calligraphy under his father. After the war he returned to Kyoto from Tōkyō and began the juku that would, in due course, become Hokurin. He continued to teach a single weekly seminar in classical reading until the spring before his death.

Mori's particular insistence — that the university stay small, build slowly, and treat material objects with the same attentiveness as printed words — has been adopted by every rector since, and remains the working ethic of the institution.

五七桐 · GOSHICHI-NO-KIRI

The university kamon is the same paulownia carried by the Tōrin merchants.

The kamon was retained at the 1989 refounding by formal request to the Tōrin family, who had carried it on lacquerware and tea-jar lids for ten generations. It is impressed in red on every diploma, every offer letter, and every label of the Bunkō.

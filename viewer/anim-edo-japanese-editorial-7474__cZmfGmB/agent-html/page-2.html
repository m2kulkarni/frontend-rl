The Fifty-Three
Stations,Two Hundred
Years On

By Mariko Sato
Walked Sep–Dec 2023 492 Kilometers Issue XIV

Sec. V · Arrival

At 5:42 on the morning of the third of May, with the bronze plaque of Nihonbashi still cold under our palms, I shouldered a thirteen-kilo pack and set out for Kyoto on foot — a journey Hiroshige rendered in fifty-five woodblock prints in 1833 and which the post-runners of the Tokugawa shogunate were expected to finish in three days. I gave myself twenty-three.

The Tōkaidō, the eastern sea road, was always more idea than infrastructure. By the time Utagawa Hiroshige published his Gojūsan-tsugi series with the Hōeidō publishing house, the road had been formally surveyed for two centuries: fifty-three official post-towns between Edo and the imperial capital, each with its honjin for daimyo retinues, its rice merchants, its bathhouses, its quarrels.

I carried two books, Smith and Poster's 1986 translation of the series, with its lavish foldouts of the Hōeidō edition, and a 1974 walking guide by the Shizuoka Prefectural Tourist Bureau, which I had bought for ¥400 at a Jinbōchō second-hand stall the week before leaving. The guide was thirty years older than I am. It assumed I would still find ryokans in Yui.

Station 02 · 品川宿

The First Hour Out of Edo

Within forty minutes I had reached Takanawa, where the Tōkaidō once met the bay. Hiroshige drew fishermen with the cone of Fuji as a roster of teahouse-girls watching a daimyo's procession recede toward Edo. The bay is now landfill — a Costco, an interchange — and the procession was, on my morning, a delivery van honking at a courier on a bicycle.

But the road tilts at exactly the angle Hiroshige drew it, and the line of pine trees at Suzugamori, though now civic and pruned, still drops the temperature by two degrees as you pass beneath them.

I stopped at a 7-Eleven for a rice-ball. The clerk, when I told her I was walking to Kyoto, said only, 気をつけて — be careful — and gave me an extra wet-towel. This would be the pattern. The Tōkaidō is not a pilgrimage; nobody on it is moved to ceremony.

Hiroshige's genius was to draw not the road but the weather of being on it — the moment a porter shifts his load, the second a traveller realises the rain will not stop before nightfall.
— from the introduction, Smith & Poster, 1986

Station 14 · 原宿

The Lake, the Pass, the Print

The Hakone print is the one everybody knows: the procession threading a path between fanged blue-and-orange mountains, Lake Ashi a flat grey wedge below. Hiroshige's mountains are exaggerated — the geologist Tanaka Kōji, in a 1999 paper for the Kanagawa Museum, compared the print's gradients with the actual contours and found the artist had multiplied the steepest faces by roughly 1.4.

I climbed them on the eighth day, in fog so dense I could not see the lake at all, and concluded that 1.4 was if anything a conservative figure.

The old checkpoint, the Hakone-juku sekisho, was reconstructed in 2007 from the original Tokugawa-era specifications. The reconstruction is honest about being a reconstruction: laminated panels, a gift-shop, a polite young woman in period costume who told me she was studying logistics at Tōkai University and worked weekends. She also told me — and I have not been able to verify this — that the cedar beams were sourced from a forest that had supplied the original 1619 structure.

Station 33 · 関宿

Where the Inn Still Stands

Of the fifty-three stations, only a handful retain a structure that a Tempō-era traveller would recognise. Seki is one of them. The Tawaraya inn, on the main street, has been operated continuously since 1640, sits on a curve of the old road five hundred metres from a soulless prefectural bypass.

I had ordered four hundred and twenty bound copies of the original 1853 guidebook reprint, and the proprietor handed me one in a brocade sleeve with the bow he gives any guest who asks for it. Fifty-three is a number that resolves nothing.

Sidebar · Inset

Five Stations Where the Honjin Still Stands

Of the original fifty-three honjin — the inns reserved for daimyo and imperial messengers — five remain in their structure of the period, accept guests, and accept the discipline of the period.

  1. Seki — Kameyama
  2. Otabe — Kashiwa
  3. Goyu — Toyokawa
  4. Akasaka — Toyokawa
  5. Tsuchiyama — Kōka

All listed structures are ICOMOS or municipal-historic-property registered as of MMXXIV; visitors should book seven days in advance.

A two-hundred year retrospective

The Fifty-Three Stations, Two Hundred Years On

By Marika Sato · Photographs by Tomohiko Hayashi

Filed Under

浮世絵 · 道中
Print Studies, Travel

Read Time

22 minutes

Published

Autumn MMXXIV

Section

014 — II

A chill in the morning of the Sixty-Nine Way, with the bronze plaque of Nihonbashi still cold under my hand. We picked the gate to begin in November, the way Hiroshige did in 1832, and walked the route as it survives — the milestones, the cedar avenues, the post-stones, the woodblock prints in 1933, and where the post-towns have folded into freeway. Nineteen days later, we reached Sanjō Ōhashi.

Hiroshige was an outdoor sketcher; his prints are reportage. The earliest impressions of his keyblocks were taken before Edo's commercial machine had finished metabolising them — before retailers, distributors, and the bookstore had, between them, smoothed the strange optics of a working artist into a saleable cartoon. To see one of those impressions in good light is to read a letter, not a postcard.

The First Hour Out of Edo

With ten chosen, the road of Edo Bay almost lost from sight. We walked the Tokaido as it has been re-walked: through silos, past the swallow factory, beside the eight-lane highway with the soundwall, and along ground that has not registered a foot since 1872. To re-walk the route in 2024 is to be reminded constantly of how thoroughly any historical journey is, in its current state, an act of reading.

The Lake, the Pass, the Print

The fifteenth print is hung from a stone passage along the imperial way to Hakone — a small archive in Kanagawa-pen. Hiroshige had taught himself to render that pass in three impressions, each of them with discrepancies that reveal the printer's labour. We discussed how the colours appeared dyed, how the landscape was cropped — and how this distance enables the print's claim of the present-day route walking out of memory.

Where the Inn Still Stands

Of the fifty-three stations, only a handful remain a structure that a traveller from 1832 would still recognise. Some have become car parks; some, the cores of strip-malls. But Futagawa, Yoshida, Shōno and Shimada — small place-names assembling momentum after losing their numbers; a hot bath under a shared roof; a stone path narrow enough to enforce passing in single file — keep the architectural language of the road. We sat with their innkeepers between editions of the journal and asked the same questions.

Hiroshige's genius was to show not the road but the breaks of being on it — the moment a porter shifts his load, the way a horseback rider sees beyond the curve.

Arrival, Without Ceremony

We came down the western slope into Sanjō Ōhashi at dusk on the twenty-first of November. The print depicts the bridge full of crossings, the porters and labourers and merchants in motion, the cherries blossoming behind them; we crossed the bridge into a moonless evening in early winter, with twelve other people and one cyclist, and an audible Pachinko parlour beneath the eastern foot of the bridge. There was no sense of conclusion. The road continues to a country it left two centuries ago.

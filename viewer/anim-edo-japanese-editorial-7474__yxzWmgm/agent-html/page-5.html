Tsumago-
juku, in the quiet
of January.

A dispatch from the forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō, where a 1968 ordinance preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the snow.

FIELD NOTES

Tsumago is the 42nd in the sixty-nine post-towns of the Nakasendō (the inland route between Kyoto and Edo, completed in 1602 under the order of the second shōgun, Tokugawa Hidetada).

The town has, by deliberate ordinance, preserved the eaves, the lacquered signboards, and — almost — the sound of the snow.

I had come to write about the by-passed strip-shop signs — the suzuki-fired-from the noise of the merchant town. But here, in the colour of slow tea: There are no Pachinko parlours, the geography of the valley registered for it. Tsumago always rests its inn-lot for a day.

The forty-second post-town of the Nakasendō was preserved in 1971 by villagers who refused both the highway and the souvenir trade. By that hour the post offices, slipped behind the four-day shop, kept the colour of slate tea. There are no Phyllis Kim, the pavement under-saved are not. So is wide, and the road of Yokohama is between a cedar avenue and the souvenir trade. In January, with the slate roofs under snow and the lanterns lit by four, it remains the closest a traveler can come to the road Bashō knew.

What was saved, and what it cost

The story of how Tsumago became Tsumago has been told often enough that the residents are tired of telling it; but it bears retelling, because the 1968 town ordinance is fewer than 100 articles. The post-town reserved the deliberate erasure — a rule passed in 1968 that not a single household, business, or sale of land would be permitted, in three sentences, to alter the wood-front of a building older than 1880. The post offices, slipped behind the four-day shop, slipped behind the eight-lane highway with the soundwall, and along ground that has not registered a foot since 1872.

Gohei-mochi, miso, walnut

I stayed at the Matsushiro-ya, four rooms above a restaurant that has served travellers, by the proprietress's reckoning, since 1818. Her grandmother — who would have served the gohei-mochi, miso, and walnut paste, in a flat round dish of bowls of beach cedar drilled with the contemporaneously, the Imperial telegraph wires that come up at supper for grouse, and the proprietress's quail mock-mode, since the conditioning of the Catalogue waters of the world.

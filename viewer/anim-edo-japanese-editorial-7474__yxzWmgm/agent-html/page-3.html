第三篇 · III Indigo at Aizome-an · 藍 染

The fifth-
generation
dyer who refused
synthetic blue

Hiroko Furuyama tends Aizome-Inubō, a 280-year-old earthenware vat sunk into the floor of a Nishijin machiya. She is the fifth Furuyama in its lineage. The vat registers, has not been temper-rejuvenated since 1933.

三 · 蜻蛉
I

A 280-year-old vat sunk in concrete and pine. Hiroko leans over its lip, lifts a folded cotton, and gives it three full breaths before it is dipped again. She speaks of her grandmother, who placed her hand on the vat and felt its weather.

That Kyoto as you now walk past the woolly shop on Pelican Avenue, with no sense of the period that gave it the name, is testimony to how thoroughly being readily-against-foreign saved the city, not just preserved its colour. The fact, in some way, of a deep colour — a sea or a ring of blue, fixed in alkaline pH — is something a Kyoto household will keep, unmistakeably, of breaking compass — even though it was always done as a matter of cloth, not as for ornament.

Furuya-san is six. She wears a button-up canvas shirt over a grey sweater, and her hair is short. The only object that betrays her trade is a thumbnail dyed indigo that has not faded in ten years. The vat in her workshop has produced over three hundred kimono lengths and many small commissions for tea-ceremony cloths, repaired textiles, and surgical wraps for Imperial Buddhist temples.

A Conversation, Transcribed

— You inherited the vat at twenty-six. What did your grandmother say to you on her last day at the bench?

"She told me to hand-rotate the cloth thirty-three times before its next descent, regardless of the time. There is no urgency in indigo, only repetition."

— Have you considered synthetic indigotin?

"Once, in a class. The colour comes out flat. There is no shadow inside it. The fermented vat carries the warmth of the leaves that built it; the synthetic carries only the chemist's instructions."

— Has the climate changed your fermentation?

"The summers are longer. The vat works faster but tires sooner. I add more lime. I lengthen the rest periods. The work follows the season, as it always has."

— What is your daily quantity?

"Three lengths of cotton, six of silk, one of linen. The silk takes the colour easiest; the linen, hardest. The cotton is the truest record of the vat's condition. I read the vat through the cotton."

— Does the vat ever exhaust itself?

"Yes. It rests through January and most of February. I add ash to it on a schedule my grandfather wrote in 1947, which his grandmother wrote on a paper since lost."

— What will happen to the vat after you?

"My niece begins her apprenticeship in the spring. She is twelve. The vat is older than her by a factor of twenty-three. She is afraid of it. That is the appropriate response."

"The vat has been fermenting since 1834. My job is not to dye cloth. My job is to keep something alive long enough to hand it to whoever comes next." — Hiroko Furuyama, master dyer
第一 · I

Pale Hour
Mae, three days

The first descent. Fabric leaves the vat the colour of new bamboo and oxidises in air to a pale grey-blue.

第二 · II

Deep Sky
Hiru, eight days

The middle eight days produce the cobalt the workshop is known for. Vat is fed twice between dipping.

第三 · III

Warrior's Blue,
twelve days

The deepest immersion is reserved for ceremonial cloth and is taken on only once a year, in late autumn.
